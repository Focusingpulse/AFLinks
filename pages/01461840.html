Robert Metcalfe

· archived # 1,461,840
December 4, 1995. Robert Metcalfe sat down to write what would become one of the most infamous predictions in technology history. Metcalfe wasn't just any columnist. He was the man who co-invented Ethernet at Xerox PARC in 1973—the fundamental technology that allowed computers to talk to each other. He'd built 3Com into a networking giant worth billions. When Robert Metcalfe spoke about networks, the entire tech world listened. And what he said that December stopped people in their tracks: "I predict the Internet will soon go spectacularly supernova and in 1996 catastrophically collapse." Not slow down. Not struggle. Catastrophically collapse. Imagine the weight of those words. In 1995, the internet was fragile. Most people still connected through modems that screeched like wounded animals. A single webpage could take minutes to load. The infrastructure—routers, servers, bandwidth—was built for researchers, not for millions of ordinary people suddenly flooding online to check email and browse websites. Metcalfe looked at the numbers and saw disaster. Too many users. Not enough capacity. The system was buckling. To him, the math was undeniable. And he was so certain that he made a public promise: If the internet did NOT collapse in 1996, he would literally eat his words. He put it in print. No escape clause. No wiggle room.
