( click to enlarge ) The 1948 Tucker powerplant was one of the most unusual engines ever placed into an American automobile. Known as the Tucker 48 or “Tucker Torpedo,” the car was originally meant to use a flat-six engine designed in-house, but time and resources pushed Preston Tucker and his engineers to adapt an existing unit. They turned to an air-cooled 589 cubic-inch flat-six with fuel injection and hydraulic valve actuation, a radical design on paper. Unfortunately, it proved too complex and unreliable in testing, forcing Tucker to find an alternative quickly. The solution came in the form of a repurposed aircraft engine. Tucker sourced the Franklin O-335, an air-cooled, horizontally opposed six-cylinder originally built for helicopters. The engine was water-cooled for automotive use and modified with a new Tucker-designed transmission. Displacing 334 cubic inches, it produced about 166 horsepower, a strong figure for the late 1940s, and mounted at the rear of the car in a configuration that was highly unconventional for an American sedan. The flat-six’s compact layout helped keep the car’s center of gravity low while providing ample torque for smooth acceleration. Despite its unusual origins, the Tucker’s Franklin-based engine proved surprisingly capable. Paired with the car’s aerodynamic body and advanced safety features, it helped make the Tucker 48 one of the most forward-thinking automobiles of its era. While only 51 examples were built before the company’s collapse, the Tucker’s powerplant remains a testament to creativity under pressure—an aircraft engine transformed into the beating heart of one of America’s most legendary automotive stories.
