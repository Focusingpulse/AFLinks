Republic Aviation Plasma Propulsion Engine. Vacuum suited plasma physicist preparing the next high-vacuum chamber test of a new type of rocket engine developed by Republic's Plasma Propulsion Laboratory, Farmingdale, New York (1961). A plasma is an ionized gas in which the atoms have been separated into negatively charged electrons and positively charged ions, it is neither a solid, a liquid nor a normal gas. The power for Republic's engine is produced by this plasma which responds instantly to magnetic forces, thus permitting its particles to be accelerated to extremely high speeds. At the heart of the engine are two aluminum electrodes, 20 cm in diameter and about 3 cm apart. The electrodes are connected to a charged, 3000 V capacitor, but as long as the gap between them is a high vacuum (the engine works only in the vacuum of space), no spark of electricity can arc across. Every second or so, when a small amount of nitrogen gas is allowed to leak into the gap, a spark flashes, turning the nitrogen into a plasma by ionizing its atoms. The heavy current, which lasts only a few microseconds, creates a magnetic field that gives the plasma a powerful "pinch" and sends it squirting in brief bursts through a carefully shaped hole. The silent blue-white flash of escaping plasma looks harmless and feeble, but its temperature reaches as high as 100,000 K, and its particles shoot into the vacuum at 44 km/s. This enormous speed, many times as fast as a chemical rocket's exhaust, is the secret of the plasma engine's promise. All space engines must shoot something astern to gain their thrust, and as the exhaust speed rises, the engine becomes more efficient. Republic's plasma engine can run for a year on 450 g of nitrogen. The experimental engine's thrust is only 0.044 Newtons, which is less than one grasshopper-power. In orbit, the engine will be powered by solar cells and batteries and its first jobs will be to keep satellites under precise control for long periods of time.
