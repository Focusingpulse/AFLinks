Hedy Lamarr - scientist

· archived # 1,459,768
The night Hedy Lamarr slipped out of a Vienna dinner party and escaped her controlling husband by drugging a maid, wearing her uniform, and fleeing through a crowd of guards, she proved she was far more dangerous than the movie star the world thought she was. Hollywood loved her for her face. Directors framed her in soft light. Magazines called her the most beautiful woman alive. What they never understood was that Lamarr treated beauty like a disguise rather than an identity. Behind the glamour lived a mind that refused to stay quiet. She grew up in a household where scientific curiosity was expected. Her father walked her through engines and machinery. Her mother pushed rigor and discipline. By her early teens she took apart clocks for fun. By her twenties she could hold her own in conversations with military engineers. Her first husband was an arms manufacturer who kept her under surveillance. He held meetings with Nazi officials at their home. Lamarr listened. She took notes. She memorized blueprints. She learned how torpedoes were jammed during wartime and how radio signals could be intercepted. When she escaped the marriage and arrived in Hollywood, the world assumed she left all that behind. She never did. During World War II she grew frustrated that she was asked to sell war bonds rather than contribute to actual strategy.
