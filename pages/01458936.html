Ernest J. King

· archived # 1,458,936
The White House office was filled with smoke and panic. It was December 1941. The United States Navy was burning at the bottom of Pearl Harbor. The Japanese were sweeping across the Pacific. The Germans were sinking ships off the coast of New York. President Franklin D. Roosevelt sat at his desk, looking at a list of names. He needed a new Commander-in-Chief for the U.S. Fleet. He didn't need a diplomat. He didn't need a gentleman. He needed a killer. He scanned the list and stopped at the name of a man who was notorious for being the most abrasive, arrogant, and difficult officer in the service. A man who allegedly once said: "When they get in trouble, they send for the sons of bitches." FDR picked up the phone. He summoned Ernest J. King. He didn't hire him to make friends. He hired him to win the war. Ernest King was a razor blade in a human suit. He was born in Lorain, Ohio, but he seemed to have been forged in a blast furnace. He was a man of excessive appetites and absolute discipline. He drank hard. He chased women. He worked twenty-hour days. And he was perhaps the most qualified sailor to ever wear a uniform. In a Navy where officers usually picked a lane, King drove over the entire highway. He was a qualified surface warfare officer. He was a qualified submariner. He was a qualified aviator who earned his wings in his forties. He knew every bolt, every rivet, and every gear of the machine. But in peacetime, he was an outcast. He was too mean. Too sharp.
