PUBLISHED: 4 APRIL 2017 | VOLUME: 1 | ARTICLE NUMBER: 0100 meeting report IAUS 331 Supernova 1987A thirty years later First the neutrinos arrived, then the burst of light: messengers of a cataclysmic event in the galaxy next door. Alak Ray recounts IAUS 331, a conference that celebrated the thirtieth anniversary of the supernova of a lifetime, SN1987A, and explored the critical role of asymmetry in the explosions, surroundings and initial conditions. Alak Ray O ATCA 44 GHz ALMA 870 µm ALMA CO(J = 2–1) HST Hα 2014 Chandra 2011 0.5ʺ 1ʺ Offset in declination n the 23rd of February, 1987, at 07:35 UT, a blue supergiant in the Large Magellanic Cloud imploded. The explosion that followed, SN1987A, was the nearest and brightest supernova in nearly 400 years. It generated intense public interest, appearing on the cover of Time magazine in 1987, and continues to greatly interest professional astronomers. On the occasion of the thirtieth anniversary of the explosion, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) sponsored a symposium on Réunion Island, a French overseas territory with a coral reef shoreline in the southern Indian Ocean. The symposium focused not only on SN1987A (Fig. 1), but also a wide class of core-collapse supernovae (cc-SNe), covering their progenitors, their evolution and their remnants. It explored the multi-wavelength manifestations of cc-SNe through observational, theoretical and numerical studies. It also projected the bright future of the field through a new generation of telescopes, detectors, computational resources and software. To bring the meeting participants up to date with the current state of the SN1987A remnant, Mikako Matsuura related details of high-angular-resolution (0.05–0.30˝) ALMA (Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array) observations.
