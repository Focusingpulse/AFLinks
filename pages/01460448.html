Lev Landau

· archived # 1,460,448
Lev Landau was twenty-four years old when he decided to rank every physicist who'd ever lived. Not their contributions. Not their influence. Pure intellectual power—the ability to see through problems that made other brilliant minds stumble. He called it the logarithmic scale. Each level represented a ten-fold increase in ability. A physicist ranked 5 was ten times less capable than someone ranked 4, who was ten times less capable than someone ranked 3. At the top—at 0—stood Isaac Newton. Alone. Unreachable. The man who'd invented calculus and discovered gravity deserved his own category. Just below Newton, at 0.5, Landau placed exactly five names: Albert Einstein Niels Bohr Werner Heisenberg Paul Dirac Erwin Schrödinger The architects of modern physics. The men who'd revolutionized humanity's understanding of reality itself. These were the gods of physics, according to Landau. And where did Landau place himself? At 2.5. Not with the gods. Two full levels below them. He acknowledged that Einstein operated on a plane of genius he couldn't reach. But here's what made the scale remarkable: Landau ranked himself ABOVE nearly every other physicist in history. Above Max Planck. Above Enrico Fermi. Above most Nobel Prize winners. He wasn't being modest. He was being honest about his abilities while still recognizing genius greater than his own. And everyone who knew Landau's work understood: he wasn't wrong.
