Gustavus Swift

· archived # 1,459,712
In 1878, Gustavus Swift had a problem that should have been impossible to solve. He was a Chicago meatpacker who had figured out something the entire industry was ignoring. Every year, thousands of cattle were loaded alive onto freight cars and shipped east to New York and Boston. A thousand-pound steer arrived as five hundred pounds of usable meat. The rest — bone, hide, inedible weight — was paid for every mile of that journey. When an animal died in transit, the packer absorbed the entire loss. The math was brutal and obvious. If you slaughtered the animal in Chicago and shipped only the edible cuts, you cut your freight costs in half. The problem was keeping the meat cold for a week across a thousand miles of summer heat. Swift hired an engineer named Andrew Chase and gave him a single instruction: solve the temperature problem. Chase didn't think about meat. He thought about air. Cold air falls. Warm air rises. Instead of packing ice around the hanging beef — which discolored it and made it arrive spoiled — Chase installed insulated ice bunkers in the roof of the car. As the air cooled at the top, it dropped down over the hanging carcasses and pushed the warmer air out through ventilators in the floor. The meat stayed dry. The temperature held. In 1878, they loaded a test car and sent it east. When the doors opened days later, the beef was perfectly fresh. Swift had his machine. Now he needed the railroads. The railroads said no.
