Sympathetic Vibratory Physics

· archived # 2,447,924
When he was only four years old, he tried to calculate how many atoms were inside the Sun. He did not do it for a school project or to impress anyone. He did it simply because he was fascinated by how numbers worked. Many years later, he looked back at his childhood and said, "Science was exciting because it was full of numbers I could calculate." That little boy was Freeman Dyson, born on December 15, 1923, in England. He grew up in a home full of books, music, and big ideas. His father was a musician who loved science, while his mother was a lawyer. From a very young age, Freeman was surrounded by encyclopedias, math sheets, and questions that seemed far too big for a child. By the time he was eighteen, Dyson entered Trinity College at Cambridge to study mathematics. During World War II, he worked for the Royal Air Force, using his math skills to analyze technical data. When the war ended, he returned to Cambridge to finish his degree. Soon after, he received two recommendation letters to move to the United States. One professor wrote with British humor that Dyson was not completely stupid. Another professor was much more direct, calling the twenty-three-year-old youth the best mathematician in Great Britain. In 1947, Dyson arrived at Cornell University, where he met the brilliant physicist Richard Feynman. It was the beginning of an intellectual partnership that changed modern physics forever. At that time, physics had a huge problem. Whenever scientists tried to calculate the interactions between light and matter, their equations gave answers that equaled infinity. A result of infinity meant the math was breaking down and making no sense at all. Richard Feynman had amazing visual ideas and introduced clever diagrams to solve the problem, but his approach lacked strict mathematical proof.
