Robert Chesebrough

· archived # 1,461,832
His career died when petroleum was discovered. So he went to the oil fields, found industrial waste everyone hated, ate a spoonful of it every day for 70 years, and lived to 96. In 1859, a young chemist named Robert Chesebrough faced a crisis. His entire career had been built on extracting kerosene from whale oil, but petroleum had just been discovered in Titusville, Pennsylvania. Overnight, his profession became obsolete. Most men would have despaired. Chesebrough saw opportunity. He traveled to the Pennsylvania oil fields, not to mourn his dying trade, but to understand what this strange new substance could offer. What he found there would change medicine cabinets around the world forever. The oil workers in Titusville had a problem. A thick, waxy residue kept clogging their drilling equipment. They called it "rod wax," and they cursed it constantly. It gummed up gears, slowed production, and created endless maintenance headaches. But Chesebrough noticed something curious. These same workers who despised the substance were also using it on themselves. Whenever someone got cut or burned on the job, they would scoop up some of the waxy gunk and smear it on their wounds. And remarkably, their injuries seemed to heal faster. Chesebrough was fascinated. Where others saw industrial waste, he saw potential medicine.
