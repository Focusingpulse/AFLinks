The first stars, as seen by supercomputers Tom Abel Today’s telescopes cannot look far enough into the cosmic past to observe the formation of primordial stars. If you want to see that process, you need sophisticated numerical simulations. Tom Abel is a member of the Kavli Institute for Particle Astrophysics and Cosmology, an associate professor of physics at Stanford University, and an associate professor at SLAC, all in Stanford, California. He is also a visiting professor at the University of Heidelberg and the Heidelberg Institute for Theoretical Studies in Heidelberg, Germany. Some 400 000 years after the Big Bang, the universe was cool enough that electrons and protons could come together to form neutral hydrogen. Through study of the cosmic microwave background radiation, cosmologists have gained an excellent understanding of the state of the universe at that “recombination” time. By the time the universe was 1 billion years old, it contained small galaxies, gamma-ray bursters, and even bright quasars likely powered by supermassive black holes as heavy as a billion suns. From observations of such objects, astronomers have gleaned more information about the early cosmos. But most of the first billion years of cosmic history has been inaccessible to direct observation. Cosmologists call that early period the dark ages and speculate about the nature of the earliest objects in the universe and their role in shaping what was to follow. Did black holes or galaxies form first? Which was created earlier, high-mass or low-mass stars? In which stars were the first carbon and oxygen nuclei forged and distributed to intergalactic space, a necessary prerequisite to forming stars that resemble those in the Milky Way? Could some of those earliest stars still be around today? When were hydrogen atoms ionized, and where did the energy come from to do that?
