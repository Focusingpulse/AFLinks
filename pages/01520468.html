Title Isotropy in the Electromagnetic Field Read in full Link to paper Author(s) David Tombe Published 2019 Abstract In Part III of his 1861 paper “On Physical Lines of Force”, James Clerk Maxwell introduces the concept of displacement current in connection with the elasticity of the medium for the propagation of light. During the course of Part III, the luminiferous medium changes from an an-isotropic sea of molecular vortices into an isotropic dielectric solid. An attempt will be made to reconcile these two seemingly contradictory mediums. Overview Dated 3 June 2019 and posted to the General Science Journal , this four-page paper by David Tombe addresses a genuine puzzle in the history of electromagnetic theory: the medium in Maxwell 's On Physical Lines of Force changes character between its parts, and Tombe asks how the two versions can be the same thing. The problem In Parts I and II of the 1861 paper, Maxwell's medium is "a dense sea of molecular vortices" whose rotation axes trace the magnetic lines of force, with tension along the lines producing attraction between unlike poles and centrifugal force sideways producing repulsion between like poles. Such a structure is cylindrically symmetric about the field direction, and therefore anisotropic. In Part III, where displacement current is introduced, the same medium becomes "a perfect isotropic dielectric solid" whose elasticity carries light. Maxwell links the elastic constant to the speed of light using the 1855 Weber–Kohlrausch determination of the ratio of electrostatic to electromagnetic units of charge. Tombe's objection is that the derivation then proceeds entirely through electrostatics — through the polarisation around a charged sphere, giving D = ε E and Coulomb's law from D = Q /4π r 2 — while "magnetism is just as important as electrostatics as regards the source of the elasticity in electromagnetic waves".
