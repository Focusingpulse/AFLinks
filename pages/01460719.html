Michael Thonet

· archived # 1,460,719
This chair changed the world. 50 million were made. You've probably sat in one without knowing you were sitting on a revolution. In 1859, a German cabinetmaker named Michael Thonet created something that sounds impossibly simple: a wooden chair. But the Thonet No. 14 wasn't just any chair—it was the first piece of furniture designed for mass production, flat-pack shipping, and democratic accessibility. Before Thonet, furniture was expensive, hand-carved, and available only to the wealthy. Chairs were heavy, ornate status symbols that required skilled craftsmen weeks to create. Thonet had a different vision: a beautiful chair that anyone could afford. His secret? Steam-bent wood. Instead of carving elaborate joints or using heavy construction, Thonet discovered he could soften solid beechwood with steam and bend it into elegant curves. The process was almost alchemical—heat, moisture, pressure, and patience transforming rigid wood into flowing forms. The No. 14 required only six pieces of wood and ten screws. It could be disassembled, packed flat (36 chairs fit in one cubic meter), and shipped anywhere in the world. When it arrived, anyone could assemble it in minutes. This was 1859. Thonet had invented IKEA a century before IKEA existed. But the genius wasn't just in the manufacturing—it was in the design itself.
