Sympathetic Vibratory Physics

· archived # 2,410,692
Margaret W. Rossiter didn’t just study history—she rewrote it. In 1993, she named a truth that had been hidden in plain sight for centuries: the Matilda Effect —the systematic erasure of women’s achievements in science. She named it in honor of suffragist and abolitionist Matilda Joslyn Gage, who had sounded the alarm more than a hundred years earlier. Rossiter spent decades combing through archives, letters, and forgotten publications, uncovering the stories of women who worked without pay, without recognition, and sometimes without even the right to put their own names on their discoveries. Her three-volume Women Scientists in America is more than scholarship—it’s an act of restoration. Every biography she unearthed was a quiet revolution, placing women back at the center of the scientific story where they always belonged. She proved that their exclusion wasn’t the result of individual failures but of systemic barriers built into the structure of science itself. Her research inspired programs and policies to ensure that today’s women in STEM are credited, published, and remembered. Honors like the Sarton Medal, a MacArthur Fellowship, and a Guggenheim recognized her scholarship, but perhaps the most fitting tribute is the Rossiter History of Women in Science Prize—awarded to those carrying forward her mission. Through her work, Margaret Rossiter didn’t just restore names to history. She ensured the world would never again have the excuse of saying they didn’t know. MatildaE
