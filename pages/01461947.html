Samuel Clemens - Mark Twain

· archived # 1,461,947
Mark Twain once lost a fortune chasing an invention he believed would make him richer than his books ever could — and it nearly ruined him. In the 1880s, Twain poured his savings into a machine called the Paige typesetter, a brilliant but overly complex device meant to revolutionize printing. It broke down constantly, cost millions to perfect, and was ultimately made obsolete by a simpler rival. Twain, who had earned today’s equivalent of tens of millions from Tom Sawyer and Huckleberry Finn, was bankrupted. He had to go on a grueling world lecture tour in his 60s just to pay back debts. That was Twain’s life in miniature: genius mixed with self-sabotage. Born Samuel Clemens, he had lived as a printer, a riverboat pilot, a failed gold prospector, and a journalist before inventing himself as Mark Twain. He wrote in a raw, American voice — full of slang, irony, and bite — at a time when “literature” was supposed to be polished and proper. Readers loved him because he sounded like real people. Behind the wit, though, was rage at hypocrisy. His later works, like The Mysterious Stranger, dripped with cynicism about man’s cruelty. He was also a man of contradictions. Twain smoked cigars constantly, swore freely, and mocked politicians and priests in print. Yet he was deeply devoted to his family, writing hundreds of tender letters to his wife Olivia and their daughters. When two of his children died young, his humor darkened, and friends noticed the weight of grief never left him.
