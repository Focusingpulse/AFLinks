Yvonne Brill

· archived # 1,469,025
A hydrazine engine sits on a metal workbench in 1960s New Jersey. It is small enough to hold in one hand. It smells faintly of ammonia and cold steel. To the engineers at RCA Astro Electronics, it looked like a side project. To Yvonne Brill , it was the answer to a problem that threatened to ground the satellite age. In the early years of the space race, satellites had a fatal flaw. They ran out of fuel too quickly. They used two different systems to stay in place. One system used gas to steer. The other used heat to move. Carrying two types of fuel made satellites heavy and short-lived. Yvonne worked in a world of gray suits and slide rules. She was often the only woman in the room. She was an expert in chemistry and math. She saw a way to use a single fuel—hydrazine—to do both jobs. If she was right, satellites could stay in orbit for years instead of months. The halls of the aerospace industry were quiet and rigid. Decisions moved through committees of men who had been doing things the same way for decades. Yvonne’s desk was tucked away, her ideas often viewed as secondary to the "real" work being done by the full-time male staff. The system was built on the logic of the "Organization Man." To the 1960s aerospace firm, a serious engineer was a man who stayed in the office for twelve hours a day. This schedule ensured total dedication to the Cold War mission.
