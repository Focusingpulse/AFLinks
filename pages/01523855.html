Saturday Science Chats sponsors by the Chappell Natural Philosophy Society Summary An open chat hosted by Franklin Hu with Dennis Allen , Bob, Roger, Bill Lucas , Alistair Riddoch and Ian Cohen, taking up an argument running on the society's email list. Do a heavy and a light object really fall together? Ian Cohen set out the case, carefully and at length, that they do not quite. The textbook derivation cancels the small mass from both sides and leaves the acceleration as GM/r ²; but the earth is not stationary, and the object attracts it too, so the relative acceleration is G(M+m)/r ². Two consequences follow. The heavier object accelerates very slightly faster. And, if the two experiments are run one after the other, the heavier object has slightly less distance to cover, because the earth has moved further to meet it. He was emphatic that this is not Aristotle vindicated — the difference is of the order of the object's mass to the earth's — but that it is not exactly zero either. Hu accepted the distance argument readily and resisted the acceleration one, on the ground that inertial resistance rises in proportion to the force. Ian took him through it again, and the disagreement did not resolve. Riddoch made the observation that clarified the case: if both objects are released at the same moment, the earth's movement toward them is common to both, so that effect drops out and only the acceleration difference remains — which is exactly the thought experiment Ian had proposed. He then scaled it up memorably: if the whole human population jumped a metre at once, the earth would move about a twelve-trillionth of a metre the other way, and when one person alone jumps, it moves eight billion times less.
