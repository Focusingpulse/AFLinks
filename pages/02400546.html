This paper argues the opposite: incompleteness is the operating condition, and completion is the single forbidden state. The argument runs from one constraint. If every state must admit a successor distinct from itself, then a complete description — one with nothing left to change — is a fixed point, and fixed points are excluded. Completion is therefore not merely unreached; it is structurally unreachable, and a system that reached it would stop. We show this is not an abstract prohibition but a mechanism with measurable consequences. A rule whose position cannot move dies at its own boundary: fixed-point arithmetic overflows above and underflows below, losing continuation at the top and distinction at the bottom. The engineering answer — letting the point float — is the same repair the constraint predicts, arrived at independently by four decades of numerical pain. Its two fields, mantissa and exponent, are measurably independent channels: value may change without shape changing, and shape may transform without value changing.
