PDF Print Share Send link George Eastman For eight years, one of the richest men in America secretly funded the institution that would shape the modern world — and told nobody. In 1912, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology had outgrown its Boston campus and needed to build a new one across the Charles River in Cambridge. The price tag was enormous. A letter arrived offering $2.5 million — with one condition: absolute secrecy. The donor's name could never be revealed. MIT President Richard Maclaurin accepted. He called the mysterious benefactor only "Mr. Smith." Even MIT's governing board had no idea who was behind the money. Only three people knew: Maclaurin, his wife, and his secretary. Remember that number. The donations didn't stop at $2.5 million. They kept coming. By 1920, Mr. Smith's anonymous contributions had reached nearly $20 million — transforming MIT from a respectable technical school into one of the world's premier scientific institutions. Boston society was obsessed. Two New York millionaires, each convinced the other was Mr. Smith, had dinner together and spent the entire evening trying to get the other to confess. Neither cracked. MIT students wrote a song about it. On January 10, 1920, Maclaurin was finally scheduled to reveal Mr. Smith's identity at the annual alumni dinner. He never got the chance. Exhausted from years of fundraising, he had contracted pneumonia in December 1919 and died on January 15, 1920. He was 49 years old. His speech was read by others. Mr. Smith was George Eastman. Founder of Eastman Kodak. Inventor of roll film. The man behind the slogan "You press the button, we do the rest." One of the wealthiest and most reclusive men in America. To understand why he hid, you have to go back to 1861. George Eastman was born on July 12, 1854, in Waterville, New York.
