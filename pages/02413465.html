The next two chords , in which the measure closes, may either be viewed as the tonic and dominant of the key , or the subdominant and tonic of the new key . The second measure opens with the same chord which closes the first measure , and is best defined as the tonic of the new key ; the second chord is clearly the dominant of the new key , and the whole of the second measure is in the new key , and reads, T. D. S. T. compound D. T. Some of these chords might be read as chords of the old key , so near to each other and so kindred are the contiguous keys . All contiguous keys to a certain extent overlap each other, so that some of the chords may be variously read as belonging to the one or to the other. In the opening of the third measure the tune returns to its own key by striking the tonic . This case is a very simple illustration of how a composition will move with perfect naturalness in more keys than one, the keys so grow out of each other, and may either merely snatch a passing chord from a new key , or pass quite into it for a phrase or two, or for a whole measure , then return as naturally, either by a smooth and quiet or by a strongly contrasted turn, according to the chords between which the turn takes place.
