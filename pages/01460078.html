John Vincent Atanasoff

· archived # 1,460,078
It was the winter of 1937. A frustrated physics professor in Iowa climbed into his car and just… drove. He had been fighting with numbers for years. The calculators of his era ran on grinding metal gears — slow, stupid, and completely useless for the complex equations his students needed to solve. He needed something better. Something that worked at the speed of electricity itself. After two hundred miles and one state line, he pulled into a roadhouse in Rock Island, Illinois. He ordered a bourbon. And then, on a paper napkin, he drew the blueprint for the modern world. His name was John Vincent Atanasoff. And almost no one has ever heard of him. The four principles he sketched that night — binary numbers, electronic vacuum tubes for logic, capacitor memory that refreshed itself, and direct logical calculation — are the exact same principles inside every smartphone, laptop, and computer on Earth today. Every single one. Back in Iowa, working with a graduate student named Clifford Berry in a basement with a $650 grant, he built it. A seven-hundred-pound machine of vacuum tubes and spinning memory drums that processed information as ones and zeros. The world's first true electronic digital computer. They called it the Atanasoff-Berry Computer. Then he made a mistake. Not a technical one. A human one. He trusted the wrong person. In 1941, a scientist named John Mauchly came to visit.
