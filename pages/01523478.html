There are many things missed out from being taught to physics students, one of those things is Boscovich's theory from 18th century. Summary Roger J Anderton 's subject is the eighteenth-century theory he has spent years championing, and the session is less a complaint about relativity than a case study in what physics education leaves out. His claim is that the first unified theory of physics was published in 1758, that modern unification descends from it, and that a physics graduate will never have heard of it. Boscovich Roger Joseph Boscovich was a Jesuit priest and polymath — mathematician, astronomer, diplomat — born in Ragusa in 1711. Anderton set his Theoria Philosophiae Naturalis of 1758 in its institutional context: the Church had condemned Galileo and had staked its physics on Aristotle, so a replacement unified account was needed, and Boscovich supplied it. The theory has three moving parts. Matter consists of point particles with no size or extension. They do not touch; they interact through a single force law that varies with distance, repulsive at very short range, attractive at intermediate range, falling away at large range, oscillating between the two along a curve. And there are no hard atoms at all — every physical property of matter arises from the force interactions between dimensionless points, so that the solidity of a table is an effect of repulsion rather than a fact about its contents. What makes it a unified theory is that one law was meant to cover everything then known: gravity, cohesion, elasticity, chemical affinity.
