Erno Rubik

· archived # 1,458,939
When Hungarian architect and professor Erno Rubik created a small wooden prototype in his Budapest apartment in the spring of 1974, he had no intention of designing what would become the world's best-selling puzzle. His goal was practical and modest: he wanted a hands-on teaching tool that could demonstrate spatial relationships to his architecture students. Rubik taught at the Academy of Applied Arts and Crafts in Budapest. He was trying to help students understand how objects could move independently while remaining connected as a unified structure. It's the kind of three-dimensional thinking that's crucial for architects but difficult to visualize from diagrams alone. So he started carving wooden blocks. The challenge was mechanical: how could he create a cube where each face could rotate freely, but the entire structure stayed together? He experimented with rubber bands, carved notches, tried different internal mechanisms. After numerous attempts, he finally created a cube where each of the six faces—each composed of nine smaller squares—could twist and turn independently while the whole thing remained intact. It worked perfectly. The blocks turned smoothly. The structure held together. From a teaching perspective, it was exactly what he'd hoped for. Then Rubik decided to add another element: he colored each face differently. Six colors, six faces. White, red, blue, orange, green, yellow.
