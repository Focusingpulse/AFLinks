Louis Ballatt

· archived # 1,460,520
He was a one-eyed orphan. They were millionaires. The 1899 newsboy strike didn't ask for a fair wage. It shut their empires down. William Randolph Hearst. Joseph Pulitzer. Men who started wars to sell newspapers. Men who dined with presidents. Men who controlled the flow of information for the entire country. The arrangement was standard. The boys bought 100 papers for 50 cents. They sold them for a penny each. They made a 50-cent profit. If they didn't sell them all, they ate the loss. During the Spanish-American War, Hearst and Pulitzer raised the wholesale price to 60 cents. The boys absorbed the hit. The papers sold fast anyway. But the war ended. Circulation dropped. Other papers lowered their wholesale price back to 50 cents. Hearst and Pulitzer did not. They kept the price at 60. For a newsboy, that extra dime was the difference between a meal and going hungry. It meant working until midnight just to break even. In July 1899, Louis Ballatt—known on the streets as Kid Blink—paid 60 cents for his stack. He took the risk. He lost money. The next day, thousands of boys paid 60 cents for their stacks. They took the risk. They lost money. The day after that, Kid Blink stopped buying. At the time, the publishers relied entirely on child distribution. Delivery trucks were scarce. The final mile of circulation rested on the backs of impoverished children.
