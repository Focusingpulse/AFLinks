Prolific green algae that capture carbon dioxide to make biodiesel. Dr. Mae-Wan Ho The article, along with others, will form part of ISIS’ 2006 Energy Report, Which Energy to be submitted to UK government’s energy review. Carbon Capture and Storage mega-project beset with problems The UK government has invested in a £25 million Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) demonstration project [1] led by British oil company BP [2] as a way of reducing greenhouse gas emissions. This involves capturing carbon dioxide from the exhausts of power stations and hydrogen or oil and gas production facilities in a relatively pure form and storing it geologically underground, in porous layers of rock, depleted or near-depleted oil and gas fields, deep saline aquifers (porous rock layers containing salty water deep underground), or in coal seams that cannot be mined [3]. UK’s energy sector is responsible for 35 percent of carbon emissions, so CCS seems an effective way of tackling the problem head on. UK’s sector of the North Sea has a large storage potential, estimated at ~20 000 to 260 000 Mtonne CO 2 . But it is illegal to dump large quantities of CO 2 under the North Sea at the moment. Nevertheless, the Norwegian company Statoil has been re-injecting CO 2 co-produced with natural gas into a deep aquifer overlying its offshore Sleipner field since 1996, and nothing seems to have leaked from the 6 Mtonne CO 2 stored so far [1]. Geologists are continuing to monitor the situation. Another approach already adopted in the North Sea Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) programme is to pump CO 2 underground to dissolve in the oil, making it more mobile and easy to extract [3].
