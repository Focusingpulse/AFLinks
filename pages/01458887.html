Elizabeth Parsons Ware Packard

· archived # 1,458,887
Her husband locked her in an asylum for three years. Her crime was not violence, hysteria, or delusion. It was thinking for herself. In 1860, Elizabeth Parsons Ware Packard was forty-three years old. She had been married for twenty-one years, borne six children, and lived what outwardly appeared to be a respectable, dutiful life in rural Illinois. She was educated, articulate, deeply religious, and intellectually curious. She read philosophy and theology. She asked questions. She believed faith should allow room for conscience and reason. Her husband, Theophilus Packard, did not agree. He was a Presbyterian minister with rigid Calvinist beliefs and an absolute sense of authority over his household. Elizabeth’s questioning of predestination, her rejection of blind obedience, and her insistence on forming her own conclusions were, to him, signs of dangerous rebellion. In his eyes, independent thought in a woman was not merely disobedient. It was pathological. Illinois law sided with him completely. At the time, a husband could have his wife committed to an asylum without a trial, without medical proof, without her consent, and without any opportunity for her to defend herself. His word alone was sufficient. One day, without warning, Elizabeth was seized and transported to the Jacksonville Insane Asylum. She was not hysterical. She did not resist violently.
