Dimensional Compression and the Historical Role of Imaginary Operators in Electromagnetic Measurement David W. Thomson III Quantum AetherDynamics Institute (QADI), Alma, Illinois, USA ORCID: 0000-0002-5830-5427 June 8, 2026 QADI Essay Series Abstract The use of imaginary numbers in alternating-current theory is among the most successful mathematical conventions in electrical engineering. Yet its historical origin raises a philosophical question: did the imaginary operator enter electrical theory because nature itself required a complex-valued representation, or because engineers needed a compact method for preserving distinctions compressed by existing measurement conventions? This essay examines the emergence of complex impedance as a case study in the history of scientific representation. From Faraday’s fields and Maxwell’s mathematical synthesis to Heaviside’s operational methods and Steinmetz’s alternating-current formalism, the story reveals how mathematical structures become naturalized through engineering success. Complex impedance is not treated here as an error, but as a historically powerful solution to a representational problem. Its history reminds physicists and engineers that familiar mathematical formalisms have origins, assumptions, and conditions of usefulness. Understanding those origins helps keep open the scientific habit of asking whether future representations may reveal distinctions that older conventions concealed. Keywords: history of physics; philosophy of measurement; complex impedance; alternating current; dimensional compression; scientific representation 1 Introduction The history of physics is often presented as a sequence of discoveries about nature. Equally important, however, is the history of the representational systems through which nature has been described. Mathematical methods do not emerge in isolation.
