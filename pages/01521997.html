Title The enigmatic ether wind Read in full Link to paper Author(s) John-Erik Persson Published 2012 No. of pages 5 Abstract Overview The Enigmatic Ether-Wind is a five-page paper by John-Erik Persson , presented to the Natural Philosophy Alliance in 2012. It examines the three classes of experiment historically used to search for motion relative to a luminiferous ether — stellar aberration, the Michelson–Morley second-order test, and the Sagnac effect — and argues that each has been misread. Persson's conclusion is that two of the three were "accepted in error" as evidence against an ether wind, that the third was "abolished in error" as unusable, and that a correct reading of all three points to an ether entrained by matter. The paper's organising idea is a distinction between two things that might be meant by "the direction of light". One is the direction of the beam, given by the vector sum c + v of the light's velocity and the ether wind. The other is the normal to the wave fronts inside that beam, which Persson writes as c (1 + v c / c ), where v c is the component of the wind parallel to c . He argues that most optical instruments — telescopes, resonators, interferometers — detect the wave-front normal rather than the beam direction, and that a wind blowing within the plane of a wave front leaves that normal untouched. From this he draws the consequence that a transverse ether wind is undetectable by such instruments. Applied to stellar aberration, this means the aberration angle cannot be caused by the ether wind at all, and must instead be the ordinary consequence of the observer's own velocity u — Bradley's original 1728 "raindrop" account, which Persson argues holds for a wave model of light exactly as it did for Newton's particle model. Aberration therefore says nothing about the ether's state of motion, and the nineteenth-century argument that an entrained ether was incompatible with aberration collapses.
