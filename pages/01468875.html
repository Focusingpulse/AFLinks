Walter Pitts

· archived # 1,468,875
In the 1930s, a boy slipped into a Detroit public library to escape a home that wasn't safe. He wasn't there to hide forever. He just needed somewhere quiet. Somewhere no one would bother him. He pulled a book off the shelf — not a children's book, not a comic, not anything a twelve-year-old was supposed to read. It was Principia Mathematica — Bertrand Russell's monumental attempt to rebuild all of mathematics from pure logic alone. A book that defeated graduate students. A book that professional mathematicians approached with caution. Walter Pitts read the whole thing. And when he was done, he didn't write a fan letter. He wrote a careful, logically precise letter to Russell himself — pointing out errors in the argument. Russell, one of the greatest minds of the 20th century, was impressed enough to respond. Walter couldn't take him up on it. He was twelve, living in poverty, with nowhere to go and no one looking out for him. He ran away at fifteen. Found his way to the University of Chicago with no degree, no credentials, nothing but a mind that had been quietly sharpening itself in stolen hours between library shelves. There he met Warren McCulloch — a neurophysiologist who recognized what most people had missed their whole lives: that Walter Pitts was extraordinary. In 1943, the two of them — a self-taught runaway and a seasoned scientist — published a paper describing how neurons in the brain could function like logical circuits.
