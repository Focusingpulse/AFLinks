Mary Sherman Morgan

· archived # 1,460,667
A heavy metal canister sits on a workbench in North American Aviation’s Rocketdyne lab. It is 1956. Inside the canister is a substance that should not exist. Outside, the world is waiting for the Soviet Union to win the race to the stars. Mary Sherman Morgan looks at the chemical formulas on her notepad. She is thirty-four years old. She has no college degree. If she cannot find a way to make this fuel stable, the American satellite program will fall back to Earth in a ball of fire. The lab smells of ammonia and scorched metal. The air is thick with the hum of ventilation fans. Mary works in a room filled with men who hold PhDs from prestigious universities. She is a farm girl from North Dakota who escaped a life of dirt and silence by teaching herself the secrets of the periodic table. Her son, George, waits at home, knowing very little of what his mother does behind the high fences of the facility. If Mary fails, the United States remains grounded, and her career in a male-dominated field effectively ends. The problem is the Redstone rocket. It is powerful, but its fuel is weak. To reach orbit, the engineers need a massive boost in thrust. The military demands a solution that fits the existing hardware. They do not want a new rocket; they want a miracle in a bottle. Mary spends her days mixing volatile liquids that most chemists fear to touch. She is looking for a propellant that is both more powerful than alcohol and stable enough not to explode on the launch pad. The U.S.
