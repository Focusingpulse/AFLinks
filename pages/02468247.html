FOREWORD Use, abuse and dependence on a variety of licit and illicit substances is the major public health problem facing the U.S. and many other countries. It is the leading cause of new HIV infections, a major cause of cancer deaths as well as automobile and boat accidents, the largest contributor to our burgeoning prison population, the largest cause of crime, violence, domestic and child abuse and community destruction. In the U.S. there are over 50 million nicotine addicts, at least 15 million alcoholics and problem drinkers, over 3 million dependent upon marijuana, 2 – 3 million cocaine addicts and over a million addicted to heroin. The number of “hardcore” addicts to illicit drugs is well over 6 million. It is not surprising, given these numbers and social costs, that theories abound as to ways to improve the situation. From total prohibition to total legalization and numerous steps in between, arguments rage as to the best approach. For experienced observers of the situation, not blinded by partisan beliefs and rhetoric, it appears clear that there is no one answer. Mencken’s observation about simple solutions “there is always a well-known solution to every human problem – neat, plausible, and wrong” is as true now as in 1920 when he made it. Pure “supply reduction” models founder on the rocks of “need” and “greed” – the desire for the euphoric effects of these agents and the willingness of individuals to take risks to provide them because of the large profits. Likewise, the pure “demand reduction” model shows it’s inadequacy by the lack of interest of many addicts in stopping and the failures of our current prevention and treatment programs to sufficiently either prevent or treat. We need both a balanced model and better prevention and treatment methods. The current view of addiction is a marriage of brain and behavior.
