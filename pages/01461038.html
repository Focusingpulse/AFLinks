Nora Keegan

· archived # 1,461,038
Every time little Nora Keegan walked into a public restroom, she saw the same thing. Kids bolting for the door. Tiny hands clamped over ears. Eyes squeezed shut. The hand dryers were hurting them. And Nora could feel it herself — that sharp ringing that lingered minutes after the noise stopped. But when children complained, adults had a ready answer: "They're just loud. You'll be fine." Nora wasn't so sure. At 9 years old, she asked a question that no researcher had ever formally studied: What if hand dryers aren't just annoying — what if they're actually dangerous for children's ears specifically? So she did what scientists do. She tested it. With her parents — both doctors — driving her across Calgary, Nora visited 44 public restrooms. She came armed with a professional decibel meter, a ruler, and a hypothesis: children stand closer to the sound source than adults do, so they're exposed to louder noise. Over two years, she took 880 measurements — at different heights, different distances, hands in the airstream, hands out. Adult ear level. Child ear level. Toddler ear level. The results stopped her cold. Every single Xlerator model tested exceeded 100 decibels. Multiple Dyson Airblade units hit 105 decibels when measured at a 3-year-old's ear height. Her loudest reading — a Dyson Airblade — registered 121 decibels. That's the same as an ambulance siren, blasting next to a child's ear in a tiled room.
