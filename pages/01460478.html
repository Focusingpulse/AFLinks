Lillian Gilbreth

· archived # 1,460,478
June 1924. Lillian Gilbreth stands at the funeral of her husband, Frank, surrounded by eleven children who still need feeding, clothing, education. She's 46 years old with a PhD in psychology and over two decades of engineering expertise. Within weeks, every corporate contract is canceled. The reason? She's no longer part of a team. She's just a widow. And in 1924 America, widows don't do engineering. Most women in her position would have been ruined. Society expected her to accept charity, perhaps remarry for financial security, maybe take in sewing. Instead, Lillian did something unexpected. She looked at the one room in every house that men dismissed as beneath their attention and saw an opportunity. The kitchen. The place where millions of women performed backbreaking, repetitive labor that nobody even called "work" because it happened unpaid, behind closed doors, invisible to the world that valued only what happened in factories and offices. Lillian had spent years filming factory workers, analyzing their movements frame by frame, eliminating wasted motion to make industrial work faster and less exhausting. She'd helped revolutionize American manufacturing. Now, she would do the same for the most undervalued workplace in America. She interviewed over 4,000 women. She asked questions no one had bothered to ask: Why do your backs hurt? Which tasks cause you pain? What makes your daily work harder than it needs to be? The answer was infuriating.
