Lue Gim Gong

· archived # 1,460,533
The 1894 freeze destroyed the Florida citrus industry. A Chinese immigrant spent the next fifteen years breeding the cure. Lue Gim Gong lived in DeLand, Florida. He arrived in the United States in 1870. He worked his way down to a small plot of land where the soil was sandy and the winters were unpredictable. He kept to himself. His neighbors noticed he spent more time looking at the branches of his trees than the fruit they produced. In the winter of 1894, the temperature dropped to eighteen degrees. The sap froze inside the trunks. By morning, bark split open like rifle shots across the county. The state’s economy collapsed in a single night. Growers packed their bags and abandoned their groves. If a tree couldn't survive the frost, the land was worthless. Lue didn't leave. He started grafting. He took pollen from a cold-resistant grapefruit and brushed it onto the flowers of a sweet Mediterranean orange. It is a process of blind patience. You dust the flower, wrap it, wait for the fruit, plant the seed, and wait five years for the new tree to bear its own fruit. His first attempts yielded bitter flesh. The second batch dropped off the branches too early. At the time, botanical cross-pollination was largely the domain of university laboratories and wealthy estate owners. The United States Department of Agriculture kept detailed ledgers of approved varietals. There was no institutional mechanism for a self-taught farmer, particularly a Chinese immigrant in the Jim Crow South
