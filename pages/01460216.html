Ken Thompson

· archived # 1,460,216
Ken Thompson once built an entire computer system alone in a locked office — not for fame or money, but just to see if he could. It quietly became the foundation of the modern world. In the late 1960s, at Bell Labs , Ken Thompson was just another young computer scientist surrounded by geniuses. The lab was a temple of innovation — telephones, satellites, early computers — and Ken, quiet and thin with a dry sense of humor, was obsessed with one question: “How small can something powerful be?” He’d helped build a huge, clunky operating system called Multics — a government-funded project that was supposed to change computing. It was brilliant but bloated, too ambitious for its time. When Bell Labs pulled out of the project, everyone else moved on. Ken didn’t. He stayed behind, alone, after hours, using an abandoned PDP-7 computer that no one else wanted. He started from scratch. No team. No funding. No manual. Just curiosity. In weeks, he built UNIX , a simple, elegant system that could run programs, handle files, and multitask — everything complex computers were doing, but cleaner, smaller, smarter. His entire code fit on a few pages. His colleagues barely noticed until it worked. Then came the twist: it didn’t just work — it changed everything. UNIX became the DNA of nearly every modern system that followed. The internet, Apple’s macOS, Android, Linux — all trace back to Ken’s stripped-down creation.
