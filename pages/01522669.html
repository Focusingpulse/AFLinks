Coriolis force is the sideways deflection experienced by a body moving within a rotating system — the apparent curving of winds and ocean currents on the spinning Earth being the standard example. It acts at right angles to the body's velocity and to the rotation axis, and in textbook mechanics it takes the form 2 m Ω × v , where m is the mass, Ω the angular velocity of the frame, and v the body's velocity within it. The expression was written down by the French engineer Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis in an 1835 paper on the energetics of rotating machinery such as water wheels, where he called it the compound centrifugal force . Like centrifugal force , its companion, the Coriolis force is classed in modern mechanics as a fictitious or inertial force: a term that appears in the equations only when motion is described from a rotating frame, with no physical agent behind it. That classification is the point of dispute in the literature archived on this wiki, and this page follows the same division as the centrifugal-force article — the standard account first, then the objections raised by the researchers here. The mainstream account Newton's first law holds that a body free of net force moves in a straight line at constant speed. Seen from a frame rotating at angular velocity Ω , that straight-line motion appears curved, and the equations of motion acquire two extra terms to preserve the form of Newton's second law: a centrifugal term directed outward, proportional to Ω², and a Coriolis term, proportional to Ω and to the body's velocity, acting perpendicular to its motion. On the orthodox reading these are bookkeeping devices, not forces. They have no agent and no reaction partner, and they vanish when the description is returned to an inertial frame.
