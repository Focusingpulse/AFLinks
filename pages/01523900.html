Sloan Digital Sky Survey

· archived # 1,523,900
The Sloan Digital Sky Survey ( SDSS ) is a long-running imaging and spectroscopic survey of the sky, begun in 2000 and named for the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation, which funded it. It is the source of much of the observational material that arguments about redshift , quasars and large-scale structure have been conducted on for the past twenty-five years — including a number of the arguments recorded on this wiki. This article is a short account of the survey oriented to that use. It describes what SDSS is and where it now stands, and then sets out the particular data products that pages on this wiki draw on, and what the disputes over them have been. The survey SDSS observes with two telescopes: the Sloan Foundation 2.5 m telescope at Apache Point Observatory in New Mexico, which has carried the survey since the beginning, and the du Pont 2.5 m telescope at Las Campanas Observatory in Chile, which extends it to the southern sky. Its work divides into two kinds. Imaging records the positions, brightnesses and colours of objects; the imaging camera was retired in 2009, by which time the survey had photometered several hundred million objects over roughly a third of the sky. Spectroscopy — now the whole of the survey's activity — measures the spectrum of a selected object, which is what yields a redshift and hence, on the standard interpretation, a distance. The survey has run in five phases: SDSS-I (2000–2005) — the original imaging and redshift survey SDSS-II (2005–2008) — adding a Milky Way structure survey and the Sloan Supernova Survey SDSS-III (2008–2014) — including BOSS, the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey SDSS-IV (2014–2020) — extending to the southern hemisphere SDSS-V (2020– ) — the current phase, organised as three "mappers": the Milky Way Mapper , the Black Hole Mapper (a time-domain spectroscopic survey of quasars and X-ray sources) and the Local Volume Mapper The most recent public release is Data Release 20 , issued on 30 July 2026.
