Evelyn Berezin

· archived # 1,459,020
They told her the men on the trading floor cursed too much for a woman's delicate ears. So they took away her promotion. She had already built the computer system they all depended on. That didn't matter. What she did next changed how the entire world writes, communicates, and works—and most people still don't know her name. Inside a manager's office at the New York Stock Exchange in the early 1960s, Evelyn Berezin received news that would quietly define the next century of technology. She had been offered the position of head of technology. She had accepted. The role made perfect sense—she had designed the computerized market price system the exchange relied on. Work so specialized that perhaps a dozen people on Earth possessed the skills to accomplish it. By her own clear-eyed assessment, she was one of them. Then the board stepped in. The explanation came politely. Almost apologetically. As head of technology, she might occasionally need to be on the trading floor. The language used there, they explained, was not appropriate for a woman's ears. The profanity. The rough talk. Surely she understood. On that basis—and that basis alone—the appointment was rescinded. Not because her designs were flawed. Not because her competence was questioned. Not because she lacked leadership ability.
