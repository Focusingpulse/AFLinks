Norbert Rillieux

· archived # 1,461,039
The air inside the sugar house stayed at 120 degrees. Slaves stirred boiling cane juice in open iron kettles. When a bubble burst, it threw scalding syrup across the room. The planters called it the Jamaica Train. It was slow, expensive, and lethal. Norbert Rillieux did not work in the sugar houses. He was born a free man of color in New Orleans in 1806. His father was a wealthy white planter. His mother was a free Black woman. At 18, he traveled to Paris. He studied physics at L'École Centrale. By 24, he was teaching applied mechanics. He returned to Louisiana in 1833. The sugar economy was stalling. The open-kettle method burned up massive amounts of wood. It produced dark, low-quality sugar. Planters were losing money. Rillieux looked at the boiling vats and saw a thermodynamic problem. He knew how to harness steam. In the 1830s, the thermodynamics of latent heat were strictly theoretical. European engineers understood that steam contained energy, but applying it to industrial refining had stalled. Records from the French Academy of Sciences show multiple failed attempts to build enclosed vacuum pans. The difficulty lay in pressure regulation. If the vapor from one chamber could not successfully heat the next without combusting, the system collapsed. Rillieux spent ten years designing a new system.
