Marie Tharp

· archived # 1,460,647
In 1952, Marie Tharp was sitting at a drafting table in New York surrounded by rolls of sonar paper that smelled of the sea. She was not allowed on the research ships - women were considered bad luck— so she charted the ocean floor from a basement office. As she sketched the depth profiles of the North Atlantic, her hand traced a distinct, recurring V-shaped notch running down the center of the ridge. It was a rift valley, a crack in the Earth where the planet was pulling itself apart. When she showed it to her partner Bruce Heezen, he dismissed it immediately. "That sounds like girl talk," he scoffed. At the time, the idea of continental drift was scientific heresy, ridiculed as a fairy tale. But Tharp refused to erase her work. She began plotting earthquake epicenters on top of her map, and they lined up perfectly inside her rift valley. The evidence was undeniable. The ocean floor was not flat and dead; it was moving, growing, and alive. In 1957, they published the first map of the Atlantic, forcing the scientific establishment to accept plate tectonics. She had drawn the unseeable. Marie Tharp gave the world its true face, proving that the mountains beneath the waves were just as majestic as the ones above.
