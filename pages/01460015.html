Jacobo Grinberg-Zylberbaum

· archived # 1,460,015
In 1994, Mexican consciousness researcher Jacobo Grinberg-Zylberbaum and his colleagues published one of the stranger experiments ever to appear in the scientific literature. The paper was titled The Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen Paradox in the Brain: The Transferred Potential and appeared in Physics Essays, Volume 7, Issue 4, pages 422–428. The experiment was extraordinary. Pairs of participants were first allowed to interact. They were then separated and placed inside semi-silent Faraday chambers approximately 14.5 meters apart, electrically shielding them from ordinary electromagnetic communication. EEG activity from both subjects was recorded simultaneously. One participant received a series of 100 light flashes, producing measurable evoked electrical potentials in that person’s brain. The other participant received no flashes. Yet Grinberg and his colleagues reported that when distinct evoked potentials appeared in the stimulated participant, corresponding electrical patterns ~ which they called “transferred potentials” ~ sometimes appeared in the EEG of the isolated, unstimulated participant. Their control subjects reportedly did not display the same effect. The authors interpreted their findings boldly: they proposed that the experiment demonstrated a form of brain-to-brain nonlocal correlation and raised the possibility of a macroscopic quantum component to brain function.
