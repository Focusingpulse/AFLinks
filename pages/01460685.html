Maya Lin

· archived # 1,460,685
Her professor gave her a B. Then her design beat 1,421 competitors—including the world's top architects. When they learned a 21-year-old Asian-American woman had won, powerful men testified before Congress to stop her. May 1981. Yale University. Maya Lin was studying for finals when someone knocked on her dorm room door. Two men stood in the hallway asking if she was the Maya Lin who'd submitted entry #1026 to the Vietnam Veterans Memorial competition. Her stomach dropped. Had she broken some rule? Submitted the wrong forms? "You won," they said. Out of 1,421 anonymous entries—submissions from decorated architects, established design firms, professionals who'd spent careers building monuments—the jury had chosen hers. Maya was 21 years old. A senior architecture student. She'd never designed anything that actually got built. She'd just won the most significant memorial competition in modern American history. For approximately 48 hours, she felt pure joy. Then America learned who she was. Rewind to fall 1980. Maya's architecture professor at Yale was teaching a seminar on funerary design. When he mentioned a national competition for a Vietnam War memorial in Washington D.C., it seemed like a good portfolio piece. The competition was open to anyone. No age limits. No credentials required. Entries would be judged anonymously—just numbers, no names attached. Maya had spent time studying abroad, observing how different cultures approached death and remembrance.
