Potato Patch Families of Idaho

· archived # 1,461,396
"The Potato Patch Families of Idaho, 1932 During the Depression, hundreds of “broken” families from Oklahoma and Kansas stalled in Idaho with nothing left. The state didn’t want them, but the soil did. Local farmers offered them 1-acre “potato patches” on the Snake River floodplain – land no one else would farm because it flooded every spring. Whole families worked them. Men dug irrigation ditches, women planted by moonlight to avoid heat, and children as young as four picked potato beetles by hand. They lived in tents and paid rent in sacks of spuds. By 1934, these “Patch Families” were feeding half of Boise. The kids went to school barefoot, carrying potatoes for lunch. When the 1936 flood came, they didn’t flee – they linked arms and built a human levee to save the crop. A Boise newspaper wrote: “Idaho didn’t give them charity. They gave us dinner.” Most families bought their patches by 1940. Their grandkids still farm them.
