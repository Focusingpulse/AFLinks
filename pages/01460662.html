Mary Allen Wilkes

· archived # 1,460,662
The first home computer sat in a Baltimore living room in 1965. It was the size of a refrigerator. The woman who coded its operating system was later classified as an administrative assistant. The standard history of personal computing begins in the 1970s. It involves men in California garages. The textbooks mention circuit boards, soldering irons, and names like Gates and Wozniak. The narrative relies on a specific image of engineering, one built around consumer electronics and silicon valleys. The accepted timeline dictates that before the mid-1970s, computers belonged exclusively to governments, massive corporations, and universities. They were monolithic machines locked in climate-controlled rooms, operated by technicians in white shirts who fed them stacks of perforated paper. A decade earlier, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology was working on a different premise. They built the LINC. The Laboratory Instrument Computer was designed to process biomedical data directly in the lab. It was a radical departure from the room-sized mainframes of the era, but it was massive by modern standards. It featured dual oscilloscope displays, four boxes of logic circuits, and a heavy metal chassis. It was loud, it ran hot, and it required constant mechanical maintenance. But it was small enough to be operated by one person. Mary Allen Wilkes did not build the physical hardware. She built the logic. The machine needed an operating system to function. Wilkes wrote it.
