Marvin Minsky

· archived # 1,460,660
Marvin Minsky didn’t just imagine artificial intelligence — he dared to believe the human mind could be reverse-engineered. Long before “AI” became a buzzword, before Silicon Valley existed, Minsky was a quiet rebel at MIT who looked at the brain and saw not mystery, but machinery. He once said, “The mind is what the brain does.” For him, thought itself was a system waiting to be decoded — if you had enough courage, curiosity, and wiring diagrams. In the 1950s, computers were still the size of rooms and dumber than calculators. Scientists were using them to count. Minsky wanted them to think. While others built hardware, he built ideas. He created the first neural network machine — the SNARC — using vacuum tubes and wires that simulated how neurons might fire in the brain. His colleagues thought it was eccentric tinkering. Minsky called it “the first child of thinking machines.” When MIT launched its Artificial Intelligence Laboratory in 1959, Minsky became its restless heart. He filled rooms with young misfits who questioned everything — how memory works, why we dream, whether machines could ever understand humor.
