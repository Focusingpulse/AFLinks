Overview Frederick David Tombe examines why some of the energy delivered to a forming magnetic field radiates away while the rest remains trapped and returns to the circuit when the power is switched off. The account follows from his double helix theory of the magnetic field. The medium is a sea of tiny dipolar vortices filling space, each an aether sink (an electron) orbiting an aether source (a positron), so that a miniature electric circuit and magnetic field is present everywhere. These vortices align electron to positron in a double helix along their mutual rotation axes, tracing the local lines of force. Time-varying induction is an overflow of pressurised aether from one vortex to its neighbour, together with angular acceleration. The near-field account runs as follows. When a DC circuit is switched on, Ampère's circuital law takes hold and the vortices begin to align in solenoidal rings around the current, with pressurised aether flowing tangentially from the wire into the field — the same process as in wireless radiation. Once alignment is complete, the aether inside the vortices flows opposite to the aether in the wire at the point of contact, and since "flowing aether cannot pass laterally through itself," no further transfer occurs. As the current rises, centrifugal force and conservation of angular momentum spin the vortices faster and the field strengthens, but all the energy is now trapped. There is no far-field radiation.
