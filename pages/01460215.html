1960s. Nucor was dying—a struggling nuclear instrument company bleeding money. Ken Iverson took over. Removed corner offices. Paid workers more than managers. Refused layoffs for 60 years. Built a $40 billion steel empire by breaking every industry rule. They removed the corner offices first. That was Ken Iverson's opening move—a signal that everything people assumed about corporate hierarchy was about to change. In the 1960s, Nucor Corporation was not a powerhouse or industry leader or anything resembling successful. It was actively bleeding cash—a struggling nuclear instrument manufacturer trying desperately to survive in an industry increasingly dominated by massive, entrenched traditional steel corporations. Then Ken Iverson took control as CEO and began systematically dismantling every assumption about how steel companies—or really any industrial corporation—should operate. The traditional steel industry model in the 1960s was rigid, hierarchical, and capital-intensive. Massive integrated steel mills. Enormous blast furnaces requiring billions in capital investment. Thousands of unionized workers. Layer upon layer of middle management. Executive suites and perks. Endless bureaucracy slowing every decision. U.S. Steel. Bethlehem Steel. Republic Steel. These giants had dominated American steelmaking for generations using this model. They seemed invincible, permanent fixtures of American industrial might.
