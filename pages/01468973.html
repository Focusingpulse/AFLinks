Will Keith Kellogg

· archived # 1,468,973
He was called "dim-witted" and spent 25 years shining his brother's shoes. At 46, he walked away and built a billion-dollar empire. Will Keith Kellogg sat in the one-room schoolhouse in Battle Creek, Michigan, squinting at the blackboard. He couldn't read the letters. They were blurry shapes, meaningless symbols swimming in front of his eyes. When the teacher called on him, he stuttered through answers, guessing at words he couldn't see. The other children whispered. The teacher sighed. Even his own family believed it: Will was slow. Dim-witted. Not worth educating. It was the 1870s. His parents were devout Seventh-day Adventists living simply, expecting Christ's return any day. Formal schooling seemed pointless when the world might end tomorrow. Besides, Will clearly wasn't bright enough for it anyway. At 13, his schooling ended and he was apprenticed to his father's broom-making business. "As a boy," he later recalled, "I never learned to play." And that, everyone assumed, would be Will's life: a struggling broom salesman in a tiny Michigan town, destined for obscurity. Then, at age 20, something extraordinary happened. Will got his first pair of glasses. Suddenly, the world snapped into focus. Letters became readable. Signs became clear. The "dim-witted" boy had never been stupid at all—he'd had severe, undiagnosed near-sightedness his entire childhood. Everything people believed about him had been wrong.
