Powel Crosley Jr.

· archived # 1,461,399
In 1946, he built an engine so revolutionary that Detroit called it impossible. It weighed 133 pounds and got 50 miles per gallon. Then the world forgot it ever existed. Powel Crosley Jr. wasn't supposed to change the automobile industry. He was a radio pioneer, a refrigerator manufacturer, a serial entrepreneur who jumped from one industry to another with wild ideas that somehow worked. But in 1946, while America's Big Three automakers were busy making cars bigger, heavier, and more powerful, Crosley asked a different question: What if we made them smaller? Not just smaller cars. Smaller engines. Lighter. More efficient. More affordable. Post-war America was drunk on abundance. Gasoline was cheap. Chrome was king. The American Dream meant a big car in every driveway. Crosley's vision of fuel efficiency and compact design seemed almost un-American. But Crosley had learned something during World War II that Detroit had forgotten: limitations breed innovation. During the war, materials were scarce. Steel was rationed. Iron was precious. Engineers had to think differently, work with less, make impossible things possible. So Crosley did something no one had attempted before. Instead of casting an engine from a single heavy block of iron—the way it had been done since engines were invented—he took thin sheets of stamped steel, shaped them precisely, and brazed them together with copper.
