Sympathetic Vibratory Physics

· archived # 2,407,318
Nobody knew his name yet. In 1806, Frederic Tudor loaded 80 tons of ice onto a ship and pointed it toward Martinique. The ice was already melting. The clock had already started. His friends thought he had lost his mind. Boston merchants laughed openly. The idea of harvesting winter lakes, packing the blocks in sawdust and wooden hulls, and sailing them across the equator and around the Cape of Good Hope to British India was not just ambitious. It was, by any reasonable measure, insane. The first voyages failed. Ice arrived as water. Customers in tropical ports had no idea what to do with it, or why they should want it. Tudor had to teach people to want cold. He started giving samples away. He showed bartenders in Havana what a chilled drink felt like in July. He convinced physicians in Calcutta that ice could reduce a fever. He built his own ice houses in Madras and Bombay so the cargo would survive the last mile. The insulation breakthrough changed everything. Sawdust, it turned out, was nearly as good as a vacuum. Ice packed in it lost only a fraction of its mass on a four-month, 16,000-mile voyage. Tudor had solved the physics problem almost by accident, and then he turned it into a system. Through the 1830s and 1840s, his operation ran like clockwork. Gangs of workers cut ice from Walden Pond and Fresh Pond in Massachusetts each January, loaded it into insulated ships, and dispatched them to the Caribbean, South America, and India. Cities that had never experienced refrigeration built their kitchens around it. Hotels in New Orleans began advertising chilled wine. Surgeons in Boston requested ice on standing order. The man who had been laughed at in 1806 became known, in the business press of his era, as the Ice King. He built an empire on a commodity that cost nothing to make, fell from the sky every winter, and had never once been considered tradeable. Mechanical refrigeration ended it.
