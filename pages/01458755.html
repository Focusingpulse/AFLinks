Don Wetzel

· archived # 1,458,755
A Dallas businessman stood irritated in a long bank teller line one afternoon in 1968, watching the clock creep toward closing time, and realized right there in that line that there ought to be a machine that could simply do what the teller in front of him was doing. Don Wetzel worked as vice president of product planning for Docutel, a company based in Irving, Texas that had built its reputation designing automated luggage handling systems for airports rather than anything related to banking. He believed a machine could handle at least ninety percent of the routine transactions a teller normally processed, and Docutel's leadership agreed the idea was worth pursuing, greenlighting development in early 1969. Working alongside engineers Tom Barnes and George Chastain, Wetzel built a machine around two technologies combined in a genuinely new way, a plastic card carrying a magnetic stripe paired with a personal identification number, letting a customer's identity and account access travel together in their own pocket. Ironically, the finished machine never actually debuted in Wetzel's own home state. Texas law at the time prohibited branch banking entirely, complicating whether an ATM even counted as a separate branch, so the first machine instead debuted at a Chemical Bank branch in Rockville Centre, New York, on September 2, 1969, advertised with the promise that the branch would open that morning and never close again.
