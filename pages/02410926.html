Log in I forgot my password Sympathetic Vibratory Physics &raquo; Velcro Page actions PDF Print Share Send link Velcro In 1941, a Swiss mechanical engineer named George de Mestral was taking a walk in the Alps with his dog when, upon returning home, he noticed that both his clothes and his dog’s fur were covered with small burdock seeds. These seeds, known for their ability to easily stick to any surface, piqued his curiosity. Upon closer inspection, he realized that the seeds had a fascinating structure: they were covered with tiny hooks that latched onto the loops of fabric and fur. Intrigued by how this natural mechanism worked, George decided to investigate further and examine one of the seeds under a microscope. It was then that he discovered that the small hooks, called "cips," adhered effortlessly to the loops in the fibers of his clothes. This seemingly simple but highly effective phenomenon sparked an innovative idea in his mind: What if he could replicate this fastening mechanism in a synthetic material that would be practical and easy to use across different fields? Motivated by this discovery, George de Mestral began experimenting with synthetic materials and designing a system that mimicked the structure of the burdock seeds. After years of trials and improvements, in 1955, he was able to patent it under the name "Velcro," a combination of the French words "velours" (velvet) and "crochet" (hook), which refers to the two main parts of the system: one strip with small hooks and another with small loops. When pressed together, the hooks latch onto the loops, creating a strong and firm connection that can easily be separated when desired.
