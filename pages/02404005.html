Chapter 7 Replications 7.1 Fleischmann In 1989, Martin Fleischmann and Stanley Pons reported experimental evidence suggesting that nuclear fusion could occur at room temperature, which they referred to as ‘cold fusion’ [232]. Their work, conducted via electrolysis of heavy water (D2 O) on palladium electrodes, reported what they described as anomalous energy production, later referred to as ‘excess heat’, beyond what conventional chemical reactions could explain. Fleischmann and Pons employed calorimetric methods to measure energy output, observing bursts of energy under specific conditions of prolonged electrochemical polarization. Notably, some of their experiments claimed heat outputs several times larger than the energy input, which they hypothesized as evidence of a nuclear reaction pathway distinct from traditional fusion processes involving neutron emission and tritium production. Although the initial announcement generated significant excitement, subsequent replication efforts by other laboratories often failed, leading to skepticism about the validity of their findings [233]. Fleischmann and Pons argued that subtle experimental variables – such as electrode preparation, electrolyte purity, and precise control over electrolysis parameters – were critical to producing excess heat. They noted that only after prolonged polarization periods and substantial deuterium loading into the palladium lattice did excess heat manifest, suggesting that high deuterium-to-palladium loading ratios, often exceeding 0.85, were essential [234, 235]. However, their limited reporting on critical preparation steps, such as heat treatments and cleaning of palladium electrodes, further complicated replication efforts. The original experiments used relatively large palladium samples and high current densities, often exceeding 500 mA/cm2 , which presented several challenges for calorimetry.
