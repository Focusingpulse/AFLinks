Wallace Kantor Born 1922 Residence San Diego, CA, United States Nationality American Known for Experiments on the speed of light from moving sources; an experimental critique of special relativity Scientific career Fields Physics , experimental optics Wallace Kantor (born 1922) is an American experimental physicist, based in San Diego, California, known for a long series of experiments and theoretical papers challenging Einstein's special theory of relativity . He is best remembered for his 1962 claim to have measured a dependence of the speed of light on the motion of its source — a result that would support an emission (ballistic) theory of light against the second postulate of special relativity. Scientific work The 1962 moving-source experiment Kantor's most-cited result is the experiment reported in "Direct First-Order Experiment on the Propagation of Light from a Moving Source" ( Journal of the Optical Society of America , 1962). Using glass plates mounted on a rotating arm between the mirrors of a modified Michelson–Morley interferometer, he reported a first-order fringe shift — on the order of the roughly 2.9 fringes predicted by emission theory — which he interpreted as showing that light passing through the moving glass is re-emitted at speed c relative to the glass rather than to any fixed frame. If correct, the result would contradict the constancy of the speed of light. The claim was tested and not confirmed. In 1964 H. E. Babcock and T. G. Bergman repeated the experiment with roughly four times the sensitivity and, where Kantor's emission theory predicted several fringes, found a shift of less than 0.02 fringe — a null result consistent with special relativity. Kantor's positive result is accordingly regarded by mainstream physics as an experimental artifact, though it remains a reference point in the emission-theory and critical-relativity literature.
