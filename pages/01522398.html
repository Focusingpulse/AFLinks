Scientific Theory Name Allais Effect Type Reported anomaly in pendulum and gravimeter behaviour during solar eclipses Author(s) Maurice Allais (1954) Keywords Gravity , Gravitation , Aether , eclipse, paraconical pendulum, gravitational shielding, Le Sage gravity , graviton Year 1954 The Allais effect is an anomalous change in the behaviour of pendulums and gravimeters reported during total solar eclipses. It is named for the French physicist and economist Maurice Allais , who observed on 30 June 1954 that the plane of oscillation of his paraconical pendulum swung sharply away from its established trend as the eclipse progressed — an effect, in his words, that "gave the very definite impression of a screen effect." The effect matters far out of proportion to its size. If the Moon passing between the Earth and the Sun can measurably alter gravity at the Earth's surface, then gravity is something that can be blocked — and a thing that can be blocked must be carried by something. That conclusion is unavailable to general relativity, in which gravity is the curvature of spacetime and there is no flux to intercept. It is, however, exactly what the older corpuscular or pushing theories of gravity predict: if gravity is a flux of particles streaming through matter, then interposing a body must attenuate it. A number of researchers catalogued on this wiki have drawn precisely that inference, and the Particle Model developed by Bob de Hilster and David de Hilster is built on it. That is the argument. This article sets it out, together with the evidence for and against the effect itself — and with the disagreements among its proponents, which are substantial. Allais himself did not think the effect was gravitational shielding at all. Allais's experiments The paraconical pendulum Allais's instrument was not a Foucault pendulum. It was short — an equivalent simple-pendulum length of about 83 cm — and instead of hanging from a wire it rested on a fulcrum: a bronze disc of 7.5 kg
