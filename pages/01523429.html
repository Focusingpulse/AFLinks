Title Mechanical Perpetual Motion Author(s) Leslee A Kulba Keywords Perpetual motion, overbalanced wheel, conservation of energy Published 2010 Journal Electric Spacecraft Journal Volume 48 Pages 4-7 Mechanical Perpetual Motion is a four-page article by Leslee A Kulba , published in 2010 in issue 48 of the Electric Spacecraft Journal , the journal's final double issue. It opens the ten-part handbook on perpetual motion and its testing that Kulba, the journal's technical editor, contributed to that issue, and it takes the oldest and simplest form of the claim: a machine of weights, wheels and levers that is supposed to run on its own arrangement. The text of the article has not been recoverable, and this page describes its subject and its place in the series rather than its argument. The subject The overbalanced wheel The archetype is a wheel whose weights swing further from the axis on the descending side than on the ascending one, so that the descending side is always heavier and the wheel is always turning. It is the oldest mechanical proposal of its kind: Bhāskara II described a version with mercury-filled spokes in India around 1150, and the sketchbook of Villard de Honnecourt, from about 1230, contains a wheel hung with mallets on the same principle. Robert Fludd's water-screw mill of 1618 — a mill that pumps back its own water — is the hydraulic cousin. The generic answer to all of them is a counting argument rather than an appeal to authority. Moving the weights out on the descending side does increase each weight's torque, but the same geometry that moves them out puts fewer of them on that side, and the two effects cancel exactly. Simon Stevin had shown the same kind of thing in 1586 with the chain draped over a pair of inclined planes: if it did not balance it would move forever, which he took as absurd, and the figure was carved on his tombstone.
