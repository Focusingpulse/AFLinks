Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation

· archived # 1,458,477
The white coating on the aluminum was the first suspect. It was sticky, messy, and absolutely everywhere. In the spring of 1964, two young astronomers stood inside a massive, horn-shaped antenna in Holmdel, New Jersey. They were not looking at the stars. They were looking at the walls of their instrument, which were covered in what they politely called "white dielectric material." To anyone else, it was pigeon droppings. Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were serious scientists working for Bell Labs. They had access to one of the most sensitive radio telescopes on Earth. Their goal was to map radio signals from the Milky Way, a task that required absolute silence. But the machine would not be quiet. Wherever they pointed the giant horn, a persistent, low-level hiss filled their headphones. It was a sound that should not have been there. It was faint—barely detectable—but to men whose job was measuring silence, it was a deafening roar. The system they worked within was built on precision. In radio astronomy, static is failure. A receiver that hums is a receiver that is broken, or poorly calibrated, or interfering with itself. The scientific standard of the time was rigorous: eliminate the error. If the data is messy, the fault lies with the observer, not the universe. This rule works. It forces engineers to build better machines and scientists to double-check their math.
