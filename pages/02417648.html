Kulik September 2026 Lead-in Put some indistinguishable tokens on the vertices of a finite graph, one per vertex, and let a token step to an empty neighbour. That is the whole system. The graph is the substrate; the space of configurations and legal steps is the arena. Now change something about it — add an edge, subdivide one, glue two vertices together, allow a site to hold two tokens instead of one, or simply look at the system with a coarser symmetry. Each of those changes alters which transformations exist. The question this paper asks is narrow and answerable: When the rules of movement change, what happens to the structure that was there before? The naive answers are that structure is created or destroyed. Both turn out to be wrong in a precise and checkable sense. Every elementary change either extends the transformation structure, re-expresses it, or hides it from a particular observer. Structure is never annihilated. The one operation that does produce a genuine kernel — restricting which configurations are admissible — produces it as a relative cohomology class: something present in the larger admissible space and absent from the smaller one, with an exact dimension formula. The headline object is a single integer attached to each such restriction. For a distinguished site allowed to hold 𝑟 tokens instead of 𝑟 + 1, that integer is the first Betti number of a small quotient graph, and it can be computed without ever building the arena.
