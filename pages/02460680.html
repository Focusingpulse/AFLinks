Sympathetic Vibratory Physics

· archived # 2,460,680
When a nineteen-year-old girl in 1952 asked her high school guidance counselor about enrolling in advanced mathematics classes, the counselor gave her a condescending smile. "Honey, nice girls go to finishing school," he told her. "Learn to pour tea." Judith Love Cohen ignored him completely. She enrolled in engineering, walking into lecture halls where she was routinely the only woman among hundreds of male students. Undeterred by classmates who scoffed when she raised her hand, she earned both her bachelor's and master's degrees from USC, eventually becoming part of the tiny 0.05% of female engineers in America. In the 1960s, her talent landed her a spot on the NASA contractor team tasked with building the Abort Guidance System (AGS) for the Apollo Lunar Module. The AGS was the ultimate fail-safe, the emergency backup computer designed to fly astronauts home if the primary system failed. As Judith bluntly put it: "It had to work, because if you needed it, you were already dying." In August 1968, nine months pregnant and still working obsessively at her desk, Judith felt her contractions begin. Instead of resting, she gathered her trajectory printouts, circuit diagrams, and logic sheets, brought them to the hospital, and continued writing equations on computer paper from her hospital bed between contractions. Right there in the delivery room, the logic clicked. She solved the final critical bug in the guidance system, set down her pencil, and gave birth to a baby boy named Thomas Jacob, whom the world would later know as actor Jack Black. The next day, she called her boss from her hospital room with a brief update: "I fixed the guidance problem. Oh, and the baby came too." Twenty months later, on April 13, 1970, an oxygen tank exploded aboard Apollo 13, two hundred thousand miles from Earth.
