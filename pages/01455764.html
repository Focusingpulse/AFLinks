Anna Bissell

· archived # 1,455,764
In 1889, a widow had a choice: disappear into history, or rewrite it. March 1889. Melville Bissell was gone at forty-five, pneumonia taking him in days. Anna Bissell stood in their Grand Rapids factory, surrounded by carpet sweepers ready to ship. Four children waited at home—ages twenty-one down to one. Business partners waited for her decision. The world waited for her to step aside. Women couldn't vote. In most states, married women couldn't control their own property. Newspapers called business "almost wholly a masculine field." Anna had no business degree. No board experience. No male relatives to front the operation. But she had seventeen years of building this company from sawdust and stubbornness. It started in a crockery shop where packing-crate sawdust triggered Melville's relentless allergies. When Anna complained about the nightmare of cleaning their carpets, her husband invented a solution—a mechanical sweeper with rotating brushes. Anna saw what he couldn't: this wasn't just their problem. This could transform every home in America. She became the company's first salesperson, traveling town to town, demonstrating the sweeper to skeptical housewives who'd never imagined cleaning could be easier. She convinced Wanamaker's—one of America's first department stores—to stock it.
