Sympathetic Vibratory Physics

· archived # 2,465,095
A self-taught mathematician working in a small Irish college quietly wrote a book in 1854 that would one day power every smartphone, laptop, and computer on earth. He had no idea. George Boole was born in Lincoln, England in 1815 to a shoemaker father who couldn't afford a proper education for his son. So Boole educated himself, learning Latin, Greek, and advanced mathematics largely on his own. He eventually landed a professorship at Queen's College Cork in Ireland, where he began pursuing one of the strangest questions in mathematics: could human reasoning itself be written as an equation? The result was a system he called the algebra of logic. The core idea was that logical statements — things like "this is true" or "this is false" — could be expressed using symbols and manipulated mathematically, just like numbers in a standard equation. Boole was not trying to build machines. He was doing philosophy with algebra, convinced that the laws of human thought could be expressed symbolically. His 1854 book, "An Investigation of the Laws of Thought," was a mathematical exploration of how the mind reasons and draws conclusions. Bertrand Russell, one of the greatest mathematicians of the 20th century, later described that book as "the work in which pure mathematics was discovered." Boole died in 1864 at just 49 years old, long before telephones, radio, or anything resembling an electronic machine existed. He never saw what his ideas would become. Decades later, engineers discovered that electrical switches — which exist in only two states, on or off — could be wired together to perform the exact logical operations Boole had described on paper. His abstract system of true and false, yes and no, mapped perfectly onto the two physical states of any electronic circuit. What Boole had called a philosophical investigation into the structure of thought became the operating language of every digital device ever built.
