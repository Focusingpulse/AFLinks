Summary A departure from the society's usual theoretical-physics subjects, arranged after Edo Kaal suggested the speaker to host Ian J Cowan . Bruce Leybourne is a geologist and geophysicist who spent years on seafloor mapping, gravity and magnetics for the US Navy and later on oil and gas surveys; he now runs the Geoplasma Research Institute, a non-profit in Colorado that sponsors the journal New Concepts in Global Tectonics , of which he is editor-in-chief. The case against convection The starting point is that plate tectonics, as a convection- and friction-driven model, cannot account for the induction effects of space weather on the Earth. Leybourne's alternative treats the planet as part of an electrical system — the "stellar transformer" hypothesis he developed with his Italian collaborator Giovanni Gregori — in which the Earth's internal energy is roughly seventy per cent tidally driven, with the remaining thirty per cent coming from solar variability and capable of arriving in episodic and violent bursts. He identified three sets of circuits. North–south circuits along the mid-ocean ridges, visible in GRACE satellite gravity data as hot lines, which he reads as electrically heated rather than convectively; vertical conduits, Gregori's "sea urchin spike" model, supplying the volcanoes; and east–west circuits along the seafloor fracture zones, which he and N Christian Smoot treat as the coils of the transformer.
