In the early 1970s, a computer scientist named Bob Kahn was looking at a problem that seemed almost impossible to solve. It was not a technical problem, exactly. It was a human one. Networks already existed. The military had them. Universities had them. Research institutions had them. Each one was carefully built, carefully guarded, and run by people who had no intention of handing control over to anyone else. These networks did not talk to each other. They could not. They were built on different hardware, different software, different rules. The world was full of isolated systems, each one an island. Kahn's insight was both simple and, to many people at the time, deeply uncomfortable. You did not need everyone to give up control. You just needed everyone to agree on one shared language — a common protocol that each network would use to send and receive information. Each network would keep its own structure, its own rules, its own ownership. But at the edges, where one network met another, they would all speak the same way. No single authority in charge. No central system that could be shut down or captured. Just an agreement — and the data would find its way. The pushback was real. No central control meant no enforcement. And if no one was enforcing the rules, who would make sure it actually worked? The idea ran against everything that powerful institutions understood about how systems were managed.
