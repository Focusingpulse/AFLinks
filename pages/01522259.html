3C 273 3C 273 imaged by the Hubble Space Telescope, the central quasar occulted by a coronagraph to reveal the host galaxy and jet. [ 1 ] Object type Quasar (blazar; Seyfert 1) [ 2 ] Constellation Virgo Right ascension (J2000) 12 h 29 m 06.7 s [ 2 ] Declination (J2000) +02° 03′ 09″ [ 2 ] Redshift z 0.158339 ± 0.000067 [ 2 ] Distance 749 Mpc (2.44 billion light-years), luminosity distance on the cosmological interpretation [ 3 ] [ 4 ] Apparent magnitude 12.9 [ 2 ] Absolute magnitude −26.7 [ 5 ] Central mass 886 ± 187 million solar masses (reverberation mapping) Other names PGC 41121, [ 2 ] HIP 60936 Noted for Optically brightest quasar; first quasar spectrum identified 3C 273 is a quasar in the constellation Virgo, and the first object of its kind ever identified. It is the optically brightest quasar in the sky (apparent magnitude ~12.9) and one of the nearest, with a redshift of z = 0.158. [ 6 ] It appears on this wiki for a reason beyond its place in mainstream astronomy. When Maarten Schmidt measured its redshift in 1963, the number implied a luminosity so extreme, from a region so small, that it opened the quasar redshift controversy — the longest-running dispute in twentieth-century cosmology and the central subject of the critical tradition documented here. 3C 273 is the object that began it, and the object against which nearly every argument on both sides was first tested. That history is set out below alongside the standard account. Discovery The name records that it was the 273rd object, ordered by right ascension, in the Third Cambridge Catalogue of Radio Sources, published in 1959. Its 13th-magnitude optical counterpart had in fact been photographed since at least 1887, with no one aware of what it was. In 1962 Cyril Hazard and colleagues obtained an accurate position by timing the source's occultation by the Moon at the Parkes Radio Telescope, [ 7 ] and the radio source was associated with an unresolved, star-like optical object.
