Mary Phelps Jacob

· archived # 1,460,666
November 1913. Mary Phelps Jacob stood in her New York City bedroom staring at a beautiful sheer evening gown hanging on her door and the instrument of torture laid out on her bed. The gown was exquisite—delicate, flowing fabric in the latest Paris fashion, purchased specifically for that evening’s debutante ball. The problem was the corset, that rigid cage of whalebone, steel, and tight lacing that every respectable woman was required to wear beneath her clothes. Mary was 21 years old, born into New York high society, expected to marry well and live a life of elegant compliance. But she had always chafed against the restrictions placed on women, both literal and figurative. And tonight, staring at that beautiful gown and that brutal corset, she reached her breaking point. The corset was impossible. The gown was sheer and designed to drape naturally over the body, following the new, softer silhouettes emerging from Paris fashion houses. But the corset underneath would show through—its rigid boning, its harsh lines, its unnatural shaping of the female form. The metal stays would be visible through the delicate fabric. The whole effect would be ruined. Mary tried it on anyway, hoping somehow it would work. It didn’t. She looked ridiculous. Worse, she could barely breathe. The corset compressed her ribs, restricted her movement, and made even simple actions like sitting or bending nearly impossible.
