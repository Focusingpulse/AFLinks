Jeno Paulucci

· archived # 1,460,034
In the frozen food aisle of almost every grocery store in America, there is a snack that defined the 1980s. But the name on the original box belongs to a boy from the iron mines who started with a $2,500 loan. Jeno Paulucci didn't invent the pizza roll just to feed teenagers. He invented it to survive the crushing poverty of a Minnesota winter. Hibbing, Minnesota, in the 1920s was a place where men went deep into the earth and sometimes didn't come back out. The Iron Range was a harsh, unforgiving geography. His parents, Ettore and Michelina, were Italian immigrants who arrived in America with nothing but a willingness to break rock. The wind off Lake Superior was merciless, dropping temperatures to thirty below zero, and the pay from the mining companies was worse. To keep the family fed, his mother began selling homemade roasted garlic, peppers, and wine from their front room. The floors of that small, drafty house always smelled like sharp vinegar and heavy coal dust. He watched her work eighteen-hour days just to afford winter boots for her children. He watched his father come home covered in a grime that soap could never fully wash away. By the time he was a teenager, he was unloading boxcars and hauling groceries at the local market to bring home extra coins. He decided early on he would not die in a mine shaft.
