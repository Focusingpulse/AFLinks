Katherine Esau

· archived # 1,460,131
In 1928, male faculty dismissed her research as gardening. This woman botanist went on to write the book they would be forced to teach from for the next 40 years. Katherine Esau arrived in America in 1922. She spoke Russian and German. She was thirty-four years old. Her Mennonite family had built their life in Ukraine. Then the Bolshevik Revolution destroyed it. In 1918, they escaped on a midnight train to Germany, leaving behind their farm and their fortune. When she came to the United States, she carried almost nothing except her notebooks and a collection of seeds. She entered a doctoral program in Davis, California. The department had little space for a woman botanist. The men in the university’s agriculture program studied crop yields and farm economics. They looked at the fields from the outside. Katherine wanted to look inside the stems. She studied plant anatomy. More specifically, she focused on the phloem, the microscopic tissue that carries sugar through a plant. The male faculty saw it as a dead field. Plants had already been classified in the nineteenth century, they believed. There was nothing important left to discover. They treated her microscope work as something small and domestic. Gardening. Then, in 1928, a crisis struck California’s fields. Curly top virus was destroying the sugar beet crop.
