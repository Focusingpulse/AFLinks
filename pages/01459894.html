The steel cables were bolted deep into the Connecticut soil. The engine block sat exposed, leaking oil onto the grass. He sat in the open pilot seat wearing a dark business suit and a fedora. He pushed the throttle forward. The machine shook violently, fighting against the tethers, trying to tear itself apart. It was September 14, 1939. He was fifty years old. He had a wife, four sons, and a secure job managing a division at United Aircraft. He was already a wealthy man who designed the massive flying boats that crossed the oceans. His colleagues respected him. His board of directors thought he was wasting their money. If the cables snapped, the machine would likely flip and crush him. He had been trying to build this machine for thirty years. The first attempt happened in 1909 in Kyiv. He bought a twenty-five horsepower Anzani motorcycle engine and bolted it to a wooden frame. He built two sets of canvas rotors. When he turned the engine on, the vibration was so severe he could not stand next to it. It destroyed itself without lifting a single inch off the ground. He tried again in 1910. The result was the same. He boxed up the parts and went back to building standard airplanes. At the time, the engineering problem of vertical flight was considered fundamentally unsolvable. Records from the era show dozens of inventors attempting similar machines in France, Britain, and the United States. Almost all ended in severe injury or mechanical failure.
