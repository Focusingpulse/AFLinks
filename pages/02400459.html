For over a century, the Hilbert-Pólya conjecture hypothesized that the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function, 𝜁(𝑠), correspond precisely to the spectrum of a self-adjoint operator. This correspondence would guarantee, through the application of the spectral theorem, that all such zeros must lie exactly on the critical line defined by Re(𝑠) = 1/2. While historically treated as an elegant heuristic to explain the apparent collinearity of the zeros, modern advancements at the intersection of noncommutative geometry, spectral triples, and high-precision numerical computation have materialized this hypothetical operator into a rigorously defined, computationally verifiable mathematical object. Recent developments have catalyzed a paradigm shift, establishing that verifying the Riemann Hypothesis is mathematically equivalent to proving the strict positive semidefiniteness of a specific infinite-dimensional quadratic form: the Weil explicit formula evaluated as a Gram matrix.
