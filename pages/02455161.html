Sympathetic Vibratory Physics

· archived # 2,455,161
In 1913, a Black Army sergeant was called into his commander’s office and told he had the intelligence, education, and record to become an officer. Then came the reason he would not get the commission: his skin was the wrong color. The commander even told him he would not be welcome in the officers’ club. He answered that he did not want the club. He wanted to be an officer. The Army still said no. So he came home, went back to school, became a teacher, learned six languages, earned eleven degrees, collected five law degrees, and eventually gained admission to practice before the United States Supreme Court. At 100, he was still sitting in law school seminars with students young enough to be his great-grandchildren. Beside his bed sat stacks of books and freshly sharpened pencils because he believed the moment a person stopped learning was the moment life began to end. He kept his law office open until June 25, 1996, his 107th birthday. His name was John Morton-Finney, the son of a man born into slavery, a Buffalo Soldier, educator, civil rights attorney, and a man believed to have practiced law longer than any attorney in American history. The country once told him his skin color made him unfit to lead. He spent the next eight decades proving that the door they closed was far too small for the life he was going to build. John had been born in Uniontown, Kentucky, on June 25, 1889, one of seven children in the family. His mother had been born free, his father had been born enslaved, and both of them made sure their children understood the value of an education. When John was fourteen, his mother died and the children were sent to live with their grandfather on a farm in Missouri. School was about six miles away, which meant a twelve-mile round trip whenever he wanted to sit in a classroom. He kept going.
