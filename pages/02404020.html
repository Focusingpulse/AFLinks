Chips The thrust of this campaign was to use the tools of modern lithography in order to reduce the size of palladium targets, with an eye towards both making novel structures to help generate LENRs as well as increasing our sensitivity for detecting them. Nanostructures The goal of the Nanostructures effort was to use our cleanroom facilities and nanofabrication resources to quickly prototype custom nanostructures that were used to support mainline experimental campaigns. The primary focus was to make structures that enabled stimulation and detection of AHE. The Nanostructures campaign primarily focused on structures for physical archaeology (Fig 1): plasmonic structures using pyramids and bowties, as well as nanopillars, nanofilms, and split ring resonators. These structures were then tested in mainline campaigns. Figure 1: The Nanostructures campaign focused on developing several types of structures: (a) nanopyramids, (b) bowties, and (c) nanopillars {discussed in the Physical Archaeology section of this report}. Nanostructures : Nanopyramids Introduction: Plasmonics refers to strong light-matter interactions that result in collectively oscillating free electrons at metal surfaces, and generally involving high electric fields. A variety of efforts were made to design plasmonic structures which enhance the field and may trigger AHE. For example, simulations showed that metal pyramids have high enhancement at edges and sides 124 of the pyramids. The challenge, however, is using the optically lossy material of choice, palladium. Our group discovered a method to anisotropically etch single-crystal palladium and used this to fabricate plasmonic nanopyramids. The accompanying goal was to try to decrease dimensions so as to increase field enhancement. This was difficult and required using ebeam lithography, finer control of surface polishing and crystallinity, and improved etching handling procedures to reduce the sizescale of inverted pyramids down to 200 nm.
