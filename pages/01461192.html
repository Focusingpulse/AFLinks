Panama Canal

· archived # 1,461,192
The mountain was eating the machines. It was 1907. The Isthmus of Panama. The greatest engineering project in human history was becoming a spectacular, humiliating graveyard. The French had tried to build a canal and failed, losing 22,000 lives and bankrupting their investors. The Americans took over, but the jungle was beating them, too. Two brilliant, highly paid civilian chief engineers had already come down, looked at the suffocating heat, the deadly diseases, and the sheer, impossible scale of the dirt, and quit. President Theodore Roosevelt was furious. He was tired of civilians packing their bags when things got hard. "I will give it to the Army," Roosevelt declared. "They can't quit." He chose a fifty-year-old Army Corps of Engineers officer who had spent his career quietly building locks and dams. His name was George Washington Goethals. When Goethals arrived in Panama, the workforce was terrified. They thought a rigid, screaming military general was going to treat them like boot camp recruits. Goethals surprised them. He refused to wear a military uniform. He walked the muddy trenches every single day in plain civilian clothes, holding a yellow umbrella to block the brutal sun. But he demanded absolute, unquestioned authority. He essentially became the dictator of the Canal Zone. He didn't just have to dig a ditch. He had to sever two continents.
