Immanuel Velikovsky Born ( 1895-06-10 ) June 10, 1895 Vitebsk, Russian Empire (now Belarus) Died November 17, 1979 (1979-11-17) (aged 84) Princeton, New Jersey, United States Residence United States Nationality Russian-born American (Jewish) Known for Worlds in Collision , Catastrophism, Revised Chronology, Electric Universe Scientific career Fields Psychiatry , Psychoanalysis, Comparative Mythology , Catastrophism Immanuel Velikovsky (Russian: Иммануил Великовский ; Vitebsk, 10 June 1895 – Princeton, 17 November 1979) was a Russian-born American independent scholar, physician and psychoanalyst, best known as the author of a series of controversial books reinterpreting the events of ancient history — above all the 1950 bestseller Worlds in Collision . Drawing on comparative mythology and ancient literary sources (including the Bible), Velikovsky argued that the Earth suffered catastrophic near-collisions with other planets — principally Venus and Mars — within human memory, and that electromagnetic forces play a far greater role in celestial mechanics than mainstream astronomy allows. His ideas were vigorously rejected by the academic establishment, yet his books sold widely and inspired a generation of catastrophist and dissident researchers. The dispute over his work and the campaign to suppress it became known as "the Velikovsky affair." His emphasis on planetary catastrophe and cosmic electricity later became a foundational influence on the Electric Universe movement and on the work of researchers such as David Talbott and Wallace Thornhill . Early Life and Education Velikovsky was born in Vitebsk, in the Russian Empire (now Belarus), into a Jewish family. A gifted student, he traveled widely in his youth and studied at Montpellier in France and in Palestine before enrolling in medicine.
