Paul Allen

· archived # 1,461,245
He co-founded the most valuable software company on earth, got cancer at 29, and his own partners tried to take his shares while he was in treatment. A magazine cover led to putting a computer on over a billion desks around the world. Paul Allen was 21 years old. Working as a programmer in Boston. College dropout from Washington State University. Son of a librarian. Nobody was watching him. Then one day in January 1975, he walked past a newsstand near Harvard Square and saw the cover of Popular Electronics magazine. It showed a small rectangular box called the Altair 8800. The headline called it the world’s first minicomputer kit. Most people flipped past it. Allen stopped. He saw something almost nobody else could see. Not a blinking box with no keyboard and no screen. He saw the future. He saw that software would be the thing that mattered. Not hardware. Software. He grabbed the magazine and ran to show his friend Bill Gates, who was still a student at Harvard. They had been obsessed with computers since they were kids at Lakeside School in Seattle. Allen was in ninth grade. Gates was in seventh. While other kids played sports, they lived in the computer room. Now Allen had an idea. Write a programming language for this new machine before anyone else does. Sell the software. Build a company around it. Gates thought he was crazy. They didn’t own an Altair.
