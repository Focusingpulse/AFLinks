CHAPTER 1 Definitions 1.1 INTRODUCTION Heavy oil is a type of petroleum that is viscous and contains a higher level of sulfur than conventional petroleum and occurs in similar locations where petroleum is obtained (Ancheyta and Speight, 2007; IEA, 2005; Speight, 2007, 2008, 2009). The nature of heavy oil is a problem for recovery operations and for refining—the viscosity of the oil may be too high, thereby rendering recovery expense and/or the presence of sulfur content may be high and which increases the expense of refining the oil. The name heavy oil can often be misleading as it is being used in reference to (1) fuel oil that contains residuum left over from distillation, that is, residual fuel oil, (2) coal tar creosote, or (3) viscous crude oil; for the purposes of this text the term is used to mean viscous crude oil. When compared to conventional petroleum, heavy oil typically has relatively low proportions of volatile constituents with low molecular weight and substantially higher amounts of high molecular weight compounds of lower volatility. The high molecular weight fraction of heavy oils are comprised of complex assortment of different molecular and chemical types containing a mixed bag of compounds (not necessarily just paraffin constituents or asphaltene constituents) with high melting points and high pour points that greatly contribute to the poor fluid properties of the heavy oil thereby contributing to low mobility (compared to conventional crude oil). More generally, heavy oil typically has low levels (if any at all) of paraffin constituents (straight-chain alkanes) with moderate-to-high levels of asphaltene constituents.
