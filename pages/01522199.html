When and Where is a Current Electrically Neutral?

· archived # 1,522,199
Title When and Where is a Current Electrically Neutral? Read in full Link to paper Author(s) Thomas E Phipps Keywords special relativity theory , Lorentz contraction, electric field Published 2011 Journal Proceedings of the NPA Volume 8 No. of pages 2 Pages 449-451 Abstract Many textbooks of electromagnetism give an example in which a current-carrying wire is alleged to be electrically neutral when at rest in the laboratory. They then show that the Lorentz contraction of moving charge, demanded by special relativity theory, causes a bunching of positive charge and a thinning of negative charge in the inertial system co-moving with the conduction electrons, with a resulting charge density imbalance and non-vanishing electric field measurable in that system. By a more careful application of special relativity theory, we show, on the contrary, that the wire cannot be strictly neutral in its rest system. Therefore the textbook calculations are in error. Overview When and Where is a Current Electrically Neutral? is a two-page paper by Thomas E Phipps Jr., presented at the 18th Natural Philosophy Alliance Conference in July 2011 and published in the Proceedings of the NPA . It attacks a standard textbook demonstration — the one used by Purcell, French and Ohanian to show that magnetism is the relativistic aspect of electrostatics — and argues that it rests on an assumption made by fiat. The textbook problem: a current-carrying wire at rest in the laboratory frame S is taken to be electrically neutral. A test charge moving parallel to it at the electrons' drift speed v feels a purely magnetic force qvB. Transforming to S′, the electrons' rest frame, the test charge is at rest and the magnetic force vanishes; the same deflection must therefore come from an electric force, which it does, because in S′ the lattice ions are moving and Lorentz-contracted while the electrons are not, leaving a net positive charge density.
