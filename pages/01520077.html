Birkeland currents are electric currents that flow along magnetic field lines — in the original and best-established case, along the Earth's field into and out of the polar ionosphere, carrying charge between the ionosphere and the outer magnetosphere. They are named after the Norwegian physicist Kristian Birkeland (1867–1917), who proposed them to explain the aurora, and the name was given by Hannes Alfvén . They occupy an unusual position in the material catalogued on this wiki. The currents themselves are not a heterodox idea: they were rejected for six decades, then measured directly by satellite in the 1970s, and are now ordinary space physics. What remains disputed is how far the same picture can be carried — to stars, to galaxies, and to the structure of the universe. The standard account Birkeland's proposal Birkeland argued from the aurora. In a series of experiments beginning in the 1890s he built a terrella — a magnetised sphere suspended in an evacuated chamber — and directed a beam of cathode rays at it. The discharge did not strike the sphere evenly: it was steered by the magnetic field into glowing rings around the magnetic poles, reproducing the shape and position of the auroral ovals. From this he concluded that the aurora is produced by charged particles from the Sun guided down the Earth's magnetic field into the polar regions, and that the associated currents flow along the field lines rather than across them. He set out the argument with data from his Norwegian Aurora Polaris Expedition of 1902–1903, published in two volumes from 1908. The theory was rejected. The principal opposition came from Sydney Chapman, whose account of geomagnetic storms placed the currents entirely within the ionosphere and required no current flowing along the field into space; the prevailing view held that the intervening space was effectively empty and could not carry a current at all.
