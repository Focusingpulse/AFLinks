The Equivalence Principle as a Consequence of the Third Law

· archived # 1,524,085
Title The Equivalence Principle as a Consequence of the Third Law Read in full Link to paper Author(s) David F Roscoe Keywords Equivalence Principle, Newton's Third Law, inertial mass, gravitational mass, action at a distance, Mach's principle Published 1992 Journal Apeiron Volume 13 Publication date June 1992 No. of pages 3 Pages 11-13 Abstract One of the enduring mysteries of classical and modern physics arises from the fact that the value obtained for the gravitational-mass ratio of two particles compared in a weighing experiment is identical to the value obtained for the inertial-mass ratio of the same two particles compared in a collision experiment (to within experimental error). For this reason we speak of the equivalence between inertial and gravitational mass, and tend to use the concepts interchangeably. However, whilst practitioners of science and engineering have been content to accept this equivalence as a matter of fact, there has, until recently, been no rational understanding of it. However, recently, Ghosh, Assis and I have each given the basis for such an understanding. Overview A three-page paper written while David F Roscoe was in the Department of Applied Mathematics at the University of Sheffield, published in Apeiron in June 1992. Its purpose is to give an argument short enough to be followed in a sitting for a conclusion that Roscoe, Assis and Ghosh had each reached by longer routes: that the equality of gravitational and inertial mass is not a coincidence requiring a separate principle, but a consequence of the structure of two-body Newtonian interaction. Roscoe sets out the alternatives before giving his own. Assis and Ghosh divide the Universe into a homogeneous part and a non-homogeneous part, and argue that inertial effects on a body arise from the gravitational influence of the former while manifest gravitational effects arise from the latter; assuming a Galilean-invariant theory of gravitation, they arrive at the equivalence principle
