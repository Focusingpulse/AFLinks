Saturday Science Chat hosted by Franklin Hu and sponsored by the Chappell Natural Philosophy Society. Summary An open chat hosted by Franklin Hu , with Dennis Allen , Harry Ricker , Ian Cohen and others, running on two threads: what a photon is, and how the earliest radio detector worked. The shape of the photon Hu repeated the claim he had made the previous week — that a photon occupies the space of a rod, still a compression wave, with the rod's orientation carrying the polarisation — and drew from it the distinction he thinks matters. At the high-frequency end everything looks like particles; at the radio end everything looks like waves; and that, he argued, is not a continuum of behaviour but two different things. Light generated by atomic electron transitions comes in these rod-like packets of fixed energy; a radio wave is more like an ocean wave, broad and of any amplitude. Harry Ricker pressed the obvious objection: the electromagnetic spectrum appears continuous, from the very low frequencies once used to reach submerged submarines up to the extreme high frequencies — so where does one behaviour stop and the other begin? Hu's answer was that the boundary is technological, set by how fast electrons can be made to oscillate in a circuit, and he asked repeatedly what the highest radio frequency actually achievable is and where it sits on the spectrum. Ricker's own answer was that the spectrum is one thing described by two different models at the two ends, that the choice is a matter of which model one is using, and that this does not answer what either a photon or a radio wave physically is.
