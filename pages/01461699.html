Ray Tomlinson, ARPANET and the @ symbol. Two mainframe computers sat ten feet apart in a fluorescent room. They were not supposed to communicate. It was the late fall of 1971 in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The weather was turning cold, and the heating pipes in the Bolt Beranek and Newman building clicked intermittently. BBN was a prominent private contractor. They held a highly specialized, heavily audited assignment from the United States Department of Defense. The military wanted a resilient communication grid. The project was called the ARPANET . It was a massive, classified infrastructure initiative intended to link government and academic computers across the country. Ray was a thirty-year-old engineer on the BBN payroll. His official assignment was entirely different. He was tasked with maintaining a local time-sharing system for the PDP-10 computer. The PDP-10 was not a desktop machine. It was a sprawling series of metal cabinets that required dedicated air conditioning and a raised floor to hide the thick, black cables. Processing time was extremely expensive. Every minute of usage was logged and justified to the project managers. At the time, digital communication was severely restricted by physical geography. A utility program existed on the system called SNDMSG. It functioned almost exactly like a virtual corkboard.
