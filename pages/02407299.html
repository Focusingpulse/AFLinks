Such a structure dominates European professional music (academic and pop) from the 18th century to the present day. Western Equal Temperament divides an octave into 12 equal semitones, while the Japanese Kumoidjoshi octave has five unequal intervals, and the Indian scale has 22 unequal shrutis. The equal temperament system caused deviations from the acoustic ("natural") purity of consonances, as a result, small beats appeared in them. Many even saw the loss of "natural" purity as an attack on the "purity" of music. “But why was temperament needed at all? The fact is that if in a natural tuning (namely, it was replaced by a uniformly tempered one) to change the tonic - the sound from which we "count" the tonality - for example, from the note "do" to the note "re", then all interval relationships will be violated, and the only the way to fix this is to make all the intervals a little fake, but equal to each other.” In the Pythagorean and pure tunings used before, there were small pitch differences between the enharmonic sounds: they did not match in pitch with each other, for example, the sounds of C-sharp and C, D-sharp and E-flat. This, it is believed, hampered the development of the major-minor key: it was necessary either to design instruments with several dozen keys per octave or to abandon transitions to distant keys.
