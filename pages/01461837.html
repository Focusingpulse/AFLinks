Robert Kearns

· archived # 1,461,837
In 1963, a popped champagne cork in Detroit changed the history of the American automobile forever. Robert Kearns , a devout engineer and family man, was legally blinded in his left eye by the accident. While driving his Ford Galaxie through a light rain shortly after, the constant, rhythmic squeaking of the windshield wipers grew maddening. He wondered why a machine couldn't blink like a human eye—wiping only when moisture accumulated. He went into his basement, soldered components together, and emerged with the intermittent windshield wiper. He took his invention to the Ford Motor Company , believing he had struck gold. The engineers were stunned by his prototype. They took his unparalleled research, asked for his samples, and promised to get back to him. But the call never came. Years later, the new line of Mustangs rolled out featuring his exact electronic design. Ford claimed their own engineers had invented it. They argued that Kearns’ arrangement of resistors and capacitors was "obvious" and not worthy of a patent. Most men would have accepted the theft as bad luck. Others would have taken a quiet settlement. Kearns did neither. He launched a legal war that would span nearly three decades. He turned his basement into a law library. He slept on the floor surrounded by legal briefs. His obsession was absolute. His wife eventually left him, unable to bear the weight of his single-minded crusade.
