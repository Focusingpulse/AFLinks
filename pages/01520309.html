In the Pike's Peak mountain range, overlooking Colorado Springs, an eccentric Serbian-born inventor began at the dawn of the twentieth century a series of experiments on electrical properties of the atmosphere in a newly built laboratory 8,000 feet above sea level... Overview This is a long-form magazine essay rather than a technical paper. Christopher Bird and Oliver D Nichelson set out to recover the parts of Nikola Tesla 's work that the twentieth century absorbed and the parts it discarded. The first half is biography: the visionary child in Smiljan, the flash of insight in a Budapest park that produced the rotating magnetic field, the alternating-current generator and motor that made long-distance power distribution possible, and the 1895 transmission from the Niagara Falls turbines to Buffalo, 22 miles away, without intervening generating stations — a stroke that made Thomas Edison 's direct-current scheme obsolete overnight. The second half is the part that gives the article its dissident edge. The authors argue that Tesla's later Colorado Springs and Wardenclyffe work — wireless transmission of power, the "magnifying transmitter," and a receiver for collecting atmospheric charge — was never refuted but simply abandoned, and that a succession of later inventors who claimed to draw usable power from the ambient environment were ridiculed, bought out or destroyed rather than tested. The essay's thesis is not a physical theory but a historical one: that a technically live line of research was closed for financial and institutional reasons, and that Tesla's estate of some 100,000 documents in Belgrade has never been properly worked through.
