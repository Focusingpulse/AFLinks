Consideration as to whether the 1979 laser-interferometric experiment of Brillet and Hall did provide a null result or in fact detected the aether. Presented by Jim Marsen, hosted by Ian Cowan. Summary Run as a two-way conversation between Jim Marsen and host Ian J Cowan rather than a formal presentation — a choice criticised at the end, and one Marsen undertook to correct in a later session. The claim Brillet and Hall's 1979 laser-interferometric experiment at the National Bureau of Standards in Boulder was a Michelson–Morley of vastly greater sensitivity, and is cited as the definitive null result. Marsen's contention is that it recorded a real signal and set it aside. The authors called it a persistent spurious signal; it was present throughout, survived their efforts to eliminate systematics, and — as their own oscilloscope traces show — was not buried in noise requiring extraction, but plainly visible in the raw data. What removed it was the analysis. Following the Robertson–Mansouri–Sexl test-theory framework, which treats any signal not conforming to its predictions as garbage, they averaged the data over a sidereal day. That is exactly the procedure that cancels a signal of constant direction — and a signal arising from the Earth's rotation within the Earth-centred inertial frame would have constant direction. Marsen showed the paper's own figure, in which the raw laboratory-frame vectors cluster in one direction while the sidereal-averaged points collapse to zero at the centre.
