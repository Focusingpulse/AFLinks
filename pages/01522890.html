The expanding universe is the standard interpretation of the observed redshift of distant galaxies: that the galaxies are not moving apart through a fixed space, but that the space between them is itself increasing in scale, so that light in transit is stretched in proportion to the growth of the scale factor while it travels. The picture assembled quickly. Vesto Slipher measured large radial velocities for spiral nebulae between 1912 and 1917; Alexander Friedmann found expanding and contracting solutions of Einstein's field equations in 1922; Georges Lemaître connected the solutions to the observations in 1927 and proposed a "primeval atom" origin; and Edwin Hubble published the linear redshift–distance relation in 1929 (see Hubble Constant ). Extrapolated backwards, an expanding universe implies a hot dense beginning — the Big Bang — and with it a finite age, currently quoted as about 13.8 billion years. Four observations are usually presented as the evidential core: the redshift–distance relation itself; the Cosmic Microwave Background and its near-perfect blackbody spectrum; the abundances of the light elements, in particular the roughly one-quarter helium fraction by mass, which big-bang nucleosynthesis calculates from a single parameter; and the apparent time dilation of distant Type Ia supernova light curves, which stretch in proportion to (1 + z ) as expansion predicts. To these is usually added the Tolman surface-brightness test, in which the surface brightness of galaxies should dim with redshift far more steeply in an expanding universe than in a static one. Genuine open problems remain inside the model — the Hubble tension , the unexplained magnitude of the cosmological constant , and the surprising maturity of the earliest galaxies observed by JWST, which has forced revisions to models of early structure formation though not to the expansion framework itself.
