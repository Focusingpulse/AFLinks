Reexamining Wave-Particle Duality: A Critical Analysis of the Particle Nature of Light Abstract: This paper critically examines the widely accepted concept of wave-particle duality, mainly focusing on the particle nature of light. While the photon model has proven mathematically useful and has successfully predicted various phenomena, we argue that the physical evidence for light as discrete particles warrants careful reexamination. The paper analyzes key experiments historically interpreted as supporting the particle nature of light—including the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and double-slit experiments—demonstrating that these observations can be explained through alternative frameworks without requiring light to be particulate. We identify several significant conceptual challenges in the particle theory of light, including the need for a precise mechanism for photon emission and absorption, issues with energy conservation in long-distance photon travel, and difficulties reconciling particle behavior with well-established wave phenomena. The paper explores alternative explanations, including wave-based interpretations, quantum field theory perspectives, and other theoretical frameworks that offer more coherent explanations of observed phenomena without requiring light to be composed of discrete particles. Through a rigorous examination of both direct and indirect evidence, we distinguish between mathematical models and physical reality. We highlight how the success of the photon concept as a mathematical tool has led to its gradual reification as a physical entity without conclusive empirical support. This analysis has important implications for quantum mechanics, technological applications, and our broader understanding of scientific methodology.
