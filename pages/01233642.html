Kulik December 2025 Chapter 1: The Illusion of Chaos and the God Who Does Not Play Dice The Human Craving for Order For as long as human beings have possessed the capacity to wonder, we have looked up at the night sky and sought to impose order upon the apparent chaos of the cosmos. We drew lines between the stars, naming them after gods and monsters, creating constellations to make sense of the scattered lights. We tracked the wanderings of the planets—the "wanderers," as the Greeks called them—and built clockwork mechanisms to predict their motions. In doing so, we revealed a fundamental truth about the human mind, and perhaps about the universe itself: we crave structure. We abhor the vacuum of true randomness. We operate on the implicit assumption that beneath the noise, beneath the tumult of the storm and the roll of the dice, there exists a hidden, deterministic logic—a set of laws that, if only we could read them, would explain everything from the fall of a leaf to the birth of a galaxy. Yet, in our modern pursuit of a Theory of Everything, we have found ourselves staring into a new kind of abyss. It is not an abyss of stars, but an abyss of data. We have built machines that generate streams of numbers so complex, so unpredictable, that we have surrendered to the idea that they are truly random. We have enshrined this surrender in the heart of our computer science. We call them "Random Oracles." We treat the output of our cryptographic algorithms— specifically the ubiquitous Secure Hash Algorithm-256 (SHA-256)—as a black box of stochastic noise, a digital chaos that mimics the unpredictability we once ascribed to the whims of the gods.1 Copyright Dean A.
