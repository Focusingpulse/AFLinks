Title More Insights into Pion Analogies: Lord Kelvin's Ether Density; and 'SRT' Limitations Read in full Link to paper Author(s) Carl R Littmann Keywords density, Ether , SRT Published 2012 Journal Proceedings of the NPA Volume 9 No. of pages 5 Pages 313-317 Abstract To hopefully add more insight into topics previously addressed by the author and others, he now presents the following: One more way of constructing the 'pion-to-electron' mass ratio; Lord Kelvin's old ether density estimate and why it is still relevant; and Questioning Einstein's inference -- that one coordinate system can NOT be deemed closer to 'absolute rest' than another, when one passes by the other at high speed. Overview This is a conference paper in three loosely joined parts, presented to the 2012 NPA meeting. Littmann is candid throughout about the status of what he is offering — "my attempt to accurately picture and describe an exact mechanism will likely err with respect to details", "I will not give all details here, nor try to guess all possible objections or rebuttals" — and invites readers to check his sources and judge for themselves. The pion ratio. Littmann has previously matched the average pion-to-electron mass ratio, 270.10, to a volume ratio in a packing of spheres. Here he offers a second construction reaching the same number: three spherical substructures, each containing six electrons around a central one in a hexagonal pattern, arranged triangularly to define the pion. He presents the existence of two independent routes to 270.10 as evidence of fractal or hierarchical structure in nature. Kelvin's ether density. In 1854 William Thomson estimated a minimum density for the luminiferous medium from the mechanical energy carried by sunlight, obtaining 1.027×10⁻¹⁹ kg/m³ — a result Maxwell and Tait both referred to respectfully.
