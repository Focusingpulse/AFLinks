In July 1969, as Apollo 11 descended toward the moon's surface, alarms suddenly blared. The lunar module's computer was being flooded with unnecessary radar data, threatening to abort humanity's first moon landing. But Margaret Hamilton had foreseen such a crisis. Her revolutionary code, written during late nights at MIT, had been designed with an intelligent priority system. When the alarms sounded, the software automatically dropped low-priority tasks and focused solely on the critical landing calculations. Three minutes before touchdown, while Mission Control and the astronauts held their breath, Hamilton's code steadied the spacecraft. The computer executed her carefully crafted priority scheduling, ensuring that despite the data overload, all vital landing systems remained operational. Thanks to Hamilton's foresight and ingenuity, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin touched down safely on the lunar surface, marking one of humanity's greatest achievements. The crisis, caused by a simple radar switch left in the wrong position, had been averted by a working mother's dedication to excellence in the new frontier of software engineering. The Apollo 11 computer had 72 kilobytes of memory—less than a single text message. One woman's code had to be perfect, or three astronauts would die.
