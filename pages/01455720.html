Alice Huyler Ramsey

· archived # 1,455,720
In 1909, a 22-year-old New Jersey housewife climbed behind the wheel of a car and pointed it at San Francisco nearly four thousand miles away, across a country that had almost no paved roads. She brought three female companions with her. Not one of them could drive. What she was attempting had never been done by a woman, and most people thought it couldn't be. Fifty-nine days later, she proved them all wrong and she did it by out-fixing, out-driving, and out-lasting every assumption anyone had about her. Her name was Alice Huyler Ramsey, and in June 1909, she was an unlikely candidate for a legend. A young wife and mother from New Jersey, she had only learned to drive the year before, after her husband John who, wonderfully, never learned to drive himself bought her a car, worried she'd be safer in an automobile than on her skittish horse. Alice took to it instantly. And she didn't just want to steer the thing; she wanted to understand it the engine, the mechanics, everything that could go wrong, and how to put it right. In an era when most women were expected to treat cars as their husbands' toys, that curiosity would turn out to be everything. The car company Maxwell-Briscoe spotted an opportunity. If a young woman could drive one of their cars clear across the continent, it would be the publicity coup of the decade proof that their automobile was so reliable and easy to handle that even a "lady" could manage it.
