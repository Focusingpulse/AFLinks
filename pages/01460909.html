Nawal El Saadawi

· archived # 1,460,909
She was imprisoned for speaking the truth—so she wrote her memoir on toilet paper with a smuggled eyebrow pencil. In the 1960s, Dr. Nawal El Saadawi worked as a physician in rural Egypt, treating patients who walked miles through dusty villages to reach her clinic. Many came with injuries they couldn't name and stories they were afraid to tell. Women arrived with complications from genital mutilation performed when they were children. Girls showed signs of abuse that their families dismissed. Mothers whispered about violence behind closed doors. Nawal listened. She took notes in the margins of her medical charts—details she couldn't forget, patterns she needed to document. As a trained psychiatrist and public health physician, she understood that these weren't isolated medical cases. They were symptoms of a system built to control women's bodies and silence their voices. And she refused to stay silent. In 1969, she published her first major non-fiction work: Women and Sex. In clear, unflinching language, she wrote about female genital mutilation, sexual oppression, and the ways religion was misinterpreted to justify violence against women. She connected women's physical suffering to political and economic oppression. The book was banned immediately in Egypt. In 1972, authorities stripped her of her position as Director-General of Public Health in the Ministry of Health. The magazine she had founded and edited, Health, was shut down.
