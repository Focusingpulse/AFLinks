George Bird Grinnell

· archived # 1,459,615
In 1882, a contract sat on a desk in Washington, designed to dismantle Yellowstone National Park piece by piece. A railroad syndicate wanted the geysers. They planned to lease the land, build toll gates around its natural wonders, and charge admission to the wild. The paperwork was nearly finished, and the signatures were a mere formality the Department of the Interior had already agreed to the terms in private. A monopoly was about to take ownership of an area larger than some eastern states, legally turning public geography into private inventory. The park existed on paper. Congress had drawn a boundary around the Wyoming territory a decade earlier, in 1872. But there were no laws to protect it, no penalties for destroying it, and no funding to guard it. George Bird Grinnell knew exactly what lay inside that boundary. A zoologist who had ridden through the boiling landscape on a government expedition in 1875, he remembered the staggering weight and presence of the place. He had smelled the heavy sulfur in the air and heard the deafening roar of water erupting from the earth. He had also witnessed the destruction. Commercial hunters were slaughtering elk by the thousands, taking only the hides and leaving the meat to rot. Souvenir hunters used axes to chip pieces off the geysers, packing ancient mineral formations into crates to sell in eastern markets.
