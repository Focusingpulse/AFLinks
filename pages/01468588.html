Thomas MacDonald

· archived # 1,468,588
In 1919, if it rained in Nebraska, the state closed. The creator of the Interstate Highway System didn't drive a bulldozer. He sat at a wooden desk in Washington, D.C., holding a red pencil. His name was Thomas MacDonald. Look at a map of 1920s America. The major cities were connected by steel rails. Everything else was an island. In the countryside, a farmer couldn't haul grain twenty miles after a spring thaw. A doctor in rural Pennsylvania couldn't reach a patient two towns over if it had rained the night before. The roads were mostly dirt. They were maintained by local counties and townships, using whatever materials they happened to have on hand. This meant a graded, packed-gravel road in one county turned into an impassable swamp the exact second it crossed the municipal line. There was no national standard. There was barely a state standard. A cross-country drive wasn't a commute. It was a multi-week expedition that required spare parts, tow ropes, and a shovel. Cars routinely broke axles in deep ruts left by horse-drawn wagons. Communities existed in complete isolation, separated not by distance, but by the physical friction of the earth. The United States was not a cohesive landmass. It was an archipelago of towns floating in a sea of mud. Thomas MacDonald arrived at the Bureau of Public Roads in 1919.
