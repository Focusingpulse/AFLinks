LIVE: Saturday Science Chats Summary David de Hilster hosted in Franklin Hu 's absence, with Ian Cohen, Harry Ricker , Bob Gray and Carl Rife. The greater part of it is Ian's account of how he came to the subject, which is the most complete the series has recorded, followed by Ricker on unipolar induction and de Hilster on where the society might go. Ian Cohen's account Asked for his background, Ian gave it at length. He is an engineering scientist from Dublin with degrees in engineering and mathematics, and a doctorate in mathematical modelling and computer simulation of the thermal response of buildings, done during the first energy crisis. He remembers being struck at eighteen, in a first-year physics course, by the reciprocity paradox in special relativity — the question Dingle put, how a clock can be both faster and slower — and finding the answers unsatisfactory; but he went on with the mainstream. What turned him was Al Kelly . In the early to mid nineties Kelly gave lectures in Dublin, the first at Trinity College, Ian's own university, arguing from the Sagnac effect that the postulates of special relativity are suspect — an effect Ian had never been taught. He went, found colleagues in the audience dismissive, and was struck enough to look into it: the alternative readings of Michelson–Morley, an ether travelling with the earth in its orbit. There followed a correspondence in the Irish Times between Kelly and the academic community, including the Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies — founded in 1940 by de Valera on the model of Princeton's — whose replies Ian described as hostile and insulting.
