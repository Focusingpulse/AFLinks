Garrett Birkhoff

· archived # 1,459,583
A heavy book sat on the desk in 1940. It was filled with math that few people could read. Garrett Birkhoff looked at the pages. He was a young man at Harvard. The world was starting to burn. In Europe, tanks were moving. Planes were dropping bombs. The ocean was full of ships that did not want to be seen. Garrett did not carry a gun. He carried a pencil. He did not look like a soldier. He looked like a teacher who forgot his lunch. The air in the lab smelled like old paper and chalk dust. Outside, students were shouting. They were worried about the draft. Garrett was worried about water. He watched how water moved around a boat. He watched how air moved over a wing. To most people, it was just splashing. To him, it was a code. If a boat moves too fast, the water breaks. If a wing is the wrong shape, the plane falls. These were not just ideas anymore. They were matters of life and death. The military had a rule. They believed that only engineers should build machines. They thought math was for classrooms. They believed fancy equations could not help a soldier in the mud. The Navy had its own way of doing things. They used old charts. They used rules that were a hundred years old. They did not want a young man telling them their ships were slow. This rule works. It keeps things simple. It stops people from taking risks with expensive boats. It works until it meets this person. Garrett sat in a small room. He had a stack of white paper.
