Fred Hoyle Born ( 1915-06-24 ) June 24, 1915 Bingley, Yorkshire, England Died August 20, 2001 (2001-08-20) (aged 86) Bournemouth, England Nationality British Known for Stellar nucleosynthesis, the triple-alpha carbon resonance, steady-state cosmology, coining the term "Big Bang", quasi-steady-state cosmology, panspermia Scientific career Fields Astrophysics, cosmology, nucleosynthesis Institutions University of Cambridge Sir Fred Hoyle (24 June 1915 – 20 August 2001) was an English astrophysicist, for most of his career at Cambridge, who did foundational work on how the chemical elements are made in stars and who spent five decades as the most prominent scientific opponent of the Big Bang . His central mainstream achievement is stellar nucleosynthesis . In 1954 Hoyle argued that carbon-12 must possess a previously unobserved excited state near 7.65 MeV, because without such a resonance the triple-alpha process could not produce carbon in the quantities observed; the state was subsequently found by Willy Fowler's group at Caltech, and it remains one of the most striking successful predictions in nuclear astrophysics. In 1957 Hoyle, with Geoffrey Burbidge , Margaret Burbidge and Fowler, published the review known as B²FH , which set out the reaction networks by which stars build the elements heavier than helium. Fowler received a share of the 1983 Nobel Prize for this work; Hoyle's exclusion has been widely regarded, including by many who disagreed with his cosmology, as an injustice. In 1948, with Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold , Hoyle proposed the steady-state cosmology: a universe that expands but has no beginning, its mean density held constant by the continuous creation of matter at a rate far too small to detect directly. It was a genuine rival to the evolving-universe models until the 1965 detection of the microwave background, after which most astronomers abandoned it. Hoyle did not.
