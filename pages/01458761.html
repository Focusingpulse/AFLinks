Dorothy Lee

· archived # 1,458,761
In 1958, a NASA mathematician in the space race stood at a chalkboard in Hampton, Virginia. She had just found a fatal number. Her calculations showed that a pointed rocket returning to Earth would reach 3,000 degrees Fahrenheit. The metal would vaporize. The astronaut inside would burn alive. The military generals in the room wanted a sharp, aerodynamic missile. They were staring at a woman holding a piece of chalk, telling them their design was a death sentence. Dorothy Lee did not fit the military’s image of an aeronautical engineer. She wore pearls and a pressed skirt to the Langley Research Center. Her desk was a gray metal rectangle in a room filled with mechanical Friden calculators. They clacked endlessly under rows of buzzing fluorescent lights. She was the only woman in the aerodynamics branch working directly on the blunt-body theory. The stakes were absolute. The margin for error was zero. If her numbers were wrong, the capsule would skip off the Earth's atmosphere like a flat stone on a pond, disappearing into deep space. If she miscalculated the entry angle by a fraction of a degree, the friction would incinerate the ship. She bought her own graphite pencils from a pharmacy in downtown Hampton. The government-issued ones snapped too easily when she pressed hard against the endless stacks of graph paper. She needed dark, thick lines to see the trajectories clearly.
