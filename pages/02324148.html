Prof. Peter Saunders Concern that information on clinical trials is not generally available Over the past few years, and certainly well before the TGN1412 incident, there has been increasing disquiet about drug trials and their regulation. Not all clinical trials are registered with the authorities, and even when they are, the information provided may not be sufficient to allow the public, or the regulators who act on our behalf, to know how the trials were carried out and what results were obtained. This information is crucial in contributing to knowledge on both the safety and efficacy of the drugs. Clinical trials go through several phases and there can be important questions about each of them. Were the Phase 1 and 2 trials of the drug done in accordance with accepted ethical standards and with adequate attention to the safety and well being of the subjects? Were the subjects given enough information about the purpose of the trials and the possible hazards so that they could give proper informed consent? As the TGN1412 trial demonstrates, under the present regime we may not be allowed full information on key aspects of a trial even after it has gone badly wrong (“ Post Mortem on TGN1412 disaster ”, this series) Were the Phase 3 trials properly carried out and analysed?
