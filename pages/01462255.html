Susan Kare

· archived # 1,462,255
This is Susan Kare, and she designed the language we all speak now—even if we don't realize it. In 1982, Apple was building the Macintosh, a revolutionary computer that would bring technology to everyday people. But there was a problem: computers were cold, intimidating, filled with command lines and technical jargon. Steve Jobs had a vision of making them friendly, but he needed someone who could translate that vision into something people could touch and feel. Susan Kare, a sculptor and graphic designer with a PhD in fine arts, was working at a museum when her friend at Apple asked if she wanted to design a few icons. She said yes, not knowing she was about to change how humans interact with technology forever. Working pixel by pixel on graph paper—before design software even existed—she created the smiling Mac that greeted you at startup. She designed the command key symbol (⌘), the trash can, the paint bucket, the stopwatch. She created fonts like Chicago, Geneva, and Monaco that made words feel warm and accessible instead of mechanical. But here's what makes her truly revolutionary: She didn't just design pretty pictures. She invented a visual language that told you what something did before you even clicked it. A trash can meant delete. A folder meant storage. A paintbrush meant create.
