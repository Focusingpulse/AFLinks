The patent drawing from August 1893 shows thirty metal hooks lined up like teeth. The mechanism is overly complicated. The name at the top of the page is not the one printed on the tag of your winter coat. The modern zipper is a flawless piece of engineering. It works on tents, dresses, and luggage. It seals completely and opens in a second. The history books usually point to a Swedish-American engineer named Gideon Sundback. He filed a patent in 1913 for the interlocking teeth we know today. His design was elegant. It solved a universal problem. The industrial world rewarded him. He became wealthy. Twenty years earlier, a traveling salesman stood in a booth at the Chicago World's Fair. The summer of 1893 was brutally hot. Dust off the unpaved paths coated the exhibits in the Machinery Hall. He had twenty pages of mechanical drawings. He had a physical prototype made of stamped brass. He called it a "clasp locker." He pitched it as a way to fasten high-button shoes with a single hand. You pulled a slider up, and the metal hooks locked together. At the time, the Columbian Exposition was saturated with electric marvels. Attendees walked past his display to look at the first Ferris wheel. They gathered around the alternating current generators. At the time, late-Victorian dress required strict, laborious fastening.
