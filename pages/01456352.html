Cinnamon

· archived # 1,456,352
Cinnamon used to be worth more than gold. Wars were fought over it. Empires rose and fell for control of the bark. Then we replaced it with sawdust from Chinese tree farms and called it the same thing. It's not. I'm Dr. Cornelius Carter. I spent 34 years as a medical historian at Johns Hopkins, specializing in ancient pharmacology—the medicines that existed before pharmaceutical companies decided what counted as medicine. Three years ago, I stopped being a historian and became a patient. Fasting glucose: 181. A1C: 7.1. My physician said the words I'd been hearing my colleagues dread for years. "We need to start you on metformin." I nodded. Took the prescription. Filled it at the pharmacy downstairs. And left it in my desk drawer for three months. Not because I'm reckless. Not because I'm in denial. But because I've spent my entire career studying what medicine looked like before we industrialized it—and I knew there were answers buried in history that modern endocrinology had stopped looking for. I'd written two books on ancient trade routes and medicinal compounds. I'd translated Arabic medical texts from the 10th century. I'd traced the movement of healing plants across empires. And I knew something most doctors don't. Cinnamon wasn't always a spice. It was a medicine. One of the most valuable substances on earth for over 2,000 years.
