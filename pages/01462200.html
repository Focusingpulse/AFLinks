Steven Pinker

· archived # 1,462,200
Steven Pinker was a college sophomore when a professor returned his paper with a low grade and a note telling him that his writing was too clear to be serious scholarship. Pinker stared at the margin comment and decided he would spend his career proving that clarity was not a weakness. It was the whole point. Pinker grew up in Montreal in a household filled with arguments that sounded like sport. His father debated economics at the dinner table. His mother challenged assumptions for fun. Pinker learned early that ideas were not furniture. They were tools, and tools had to be sharpened. At McGill University, he fell in love with psychology because it aimed at the question that had been haunting him since childhood. Why do humans think the way they do. He devoured Chomsky. He challenged behaviorists. He wanted answers grounded in evidence instead of fashion. His Ph.D. work at Harvard and MIT turned him into a rising figure in cognitive science. He studied how children acquire language, how the mind processes structure, how the brain turns sound into meaning. In 1994, his book The Language Instinct argued that humans were born with an innate capacity for grammar. Critics called it provocative. Linguists called it infuriating. Pinker expected both reactions. He valued intellectual friction. Then he did something unusual for a scientist. He stepped into public debate.
