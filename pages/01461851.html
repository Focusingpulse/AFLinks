Rose Blumkin

· archived # 1,461,851
The most ruthless immigrant business story in Omaha started in a basement. Three major manufacturers refused to sell to her. She was forty-four years old. She stood five feet tall. She spoke English with a heavy Belarusian accent. The men who owned the retail stores downtown didn't like her prices. They made a few phone calls. The supply lines went dead. Rose Blumkin arrived in Omaha, Nebraska, in 1917. She didn't possess a formal education. She didn't have capital. She had left a village near Minsk, traveling across Russia to China, and eventually taking a boat to Seattle. She arrived in a country where she couldn't read the street signs. For twenty years, she worked alongside her husband, Isadore. They ran a secondhand clothing shop. They lived in the tight quarters above the store. They gathered pennies. By 1937, she had managed to save five hundred dollars. She used it to open her own business. She called it the Nebraska Furniture Mart. The name was massive and aspirational. The reality was a cramped, poorly lit basement showroom selling cheap inventory. If the shop failed, the family had nothing left. The foundation of any immigrant business story during the Depression was pure survival. The economic reality in the Midwest was grim. Rose approached retail with a singular philosophy.
