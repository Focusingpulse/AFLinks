Claude Shannon

· archived # 1,456,359
His name was Claude Shannon. He grew up in Gaylord, Michigan — a small town where he spent his childhood taking apart radios, building model planes, and delivering telegraph messages for Western Union as a teenager. He loved puzzles. He loved the elegant satisfaction of understanding how things worked beneath the surface. In 1936, he graduated from the University of Michigan with two degrees — electrical engineering and mathematics — and enrolled in graduate school at MIT. He was twenty years old. To pay his way, he took a job maintaining a massive early computer called the differential analyzer, built by the legendary engineer Vannevar Bush. The machine filled an entire room. It took multiple operators to run it. The circuits inside it were a tangled, chaotic mess — designed by trial and error, fixed by intuition, held together by engineering guesswork. Shannon looked at that machine and saw something nobody else saw. Not a marvel of technology. A problem waiting to be solved. The summer of 1937, he interned at Bell Labs in New York, where telephone engineers were wrestling with the same chaos — thousands of relay switches routing millions of calls, with no mathematical framework to guide the design. Just intuition. Just experience. Just hoping it worked. Shannon kept thinking about a philosophy course he'd taken back in Michigan.
