Edmund Beecher Wilson

· archived # 1,458,837
When two scientists discover the same thing independently, the credit should split evenly. Should. But it doesn't. In 1905, Nettie Stevens and Edmund Beecher Wilson both figured out what determines biological sex in animals. Same discovery. Same year. Same evidence. But only one of them got remembered as the person who solved one of biology's fundamental mysteries. The other one was a woman, so history mostly forgot her name. Beetles aren't glamorous research subjects. They're small, difficult to work with, require patience and precision to study properly. But in the early 1900s, beetles were perfect for genetics research because they reproduced quickly and their cells were large enough to examine under microscopes. Nettie Stevens, born in 1861, built her career studying them. Not because she loved beetles particularly. Because beetles could answer questions about how inheritance worked. How traits passed from parents to offspring. How life decided what to make next. She came to science later than most men did. Women couldn't just enroll in graduate programs back then. Had to fight for access. Prove themselves over and over. Nettie earned her undergraduate degree at age thirty-five. Got her PhD from Bryn Mawr College in 1903 at forty-two. Most male scientists that age already had established careers, published papers, professional reputations. Nettie was just starting. Playing catch-up in a field that didn't particularly want her there. She set up her microscope anyway. Start
