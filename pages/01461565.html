Ralph Baer

· archived # 1,461,565
The four-page handwritten proposal sat on Ralph Baer’s drafting table at Sanders Associates. The year was 1966. Outside the window, Nashua, New Hampshire, was quiet, but inside the building, the concrete floor vibrated with the assembly of military radar systems. Ralph stared at the television set he had hauled into the laboratory. The defense contractor paid him to build electronic surveillance equipment for the Pentagon. He was using company time to force a glowing white dot to move across a glass screen. His supervisor stood in the doorway, staring at the television and then at the four pages of notes. The directive from management was explicit: stop wasting company dollars on parlor tricks. The fluorescent lights hummed above the gray metal desks. Ralph kept his hands resting on a plastic control box he had soldered together the night before. He was the Chief Engineer for Equipment Design. His job involved military contracts, securing funding for weapons systems, and keeping five hundred engineers in line. He carried a different kind of history in his head. In 1938, as a fourteen-year-old Jewish boy in Germany, he was expelled from his school. Two months before Kristallnacht, his family fled across the border. He walked the streets of the Bronx as a refugee, working in a factory for twelve dollars a week. He learned electronics by reading diagrams on subway trains.
