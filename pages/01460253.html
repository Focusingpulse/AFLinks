Kurt Vonnegut

· archived # 1,460,253
The story begins in fire. Not the warm crackle of a hearth, but the kind of fire that swallows cities whole. In February 1945, a young American prisoner named Kurt Vonnegut stood beneath Dresden, sealed in the cold belly of an abandoned slaughterhouse, while the world above him burned into ash. He was only twenty-two. A kid, really. A soldier by accident. A survivor by luck. He had been captured during the Battle of the Bulge and dragged across Europe by German guards—half-starved, half-frozen—until he arrived in Dresden, a city known for beauty rather than war. “It was the prettiest city I ever saw,” he once said. “And then it wasn’t.” Because then came the bombs. For two nights, the Allies rained down fire and explosives with an intensity meant not to defeat an army, but to erase a place. More than 4,000 tons of destruction hit the city. The air superheated. Oxygen disappeared. Firestorms roared like living creatures. Dresden became a furnace. Vonnegut survived because he happened to be locked underground, in a concrete meat vault marked Schlachthof-Fünf—Slaughterhouse-Five. “We came out after it was over,” he recalled. “Everything was gone—just the fire and the smell.” The spires, the streets, the people—gone. The guards handed the prisoners shovels. “We were the only ones left to bury the dead,” he said. “It was a terrible job.” He was numb. Shaken. The meaning of everything he’d been taught about war collapsed. “There was nothing noble about what I saw,” he later wrote.
