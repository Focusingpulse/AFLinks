SPAF-2026-main012 CDU: 81 Grice’s Theory of Implicature and the Art of Counter-Argumentation Natalia N. Olshevskaya olshevskaya.2025@mail.ru Образовательный центр ИНЦ ФИЦ ИРиХ СО РАН, специальность 45.06.01 Языкознание и литературоведение, аспирант 1 курс Relevance. In real communication, much of persuasion happens not through explicit statements but through hidden meanings – implicatures. This creates a problem for counter-argumentation: an opponent can land meaningful attacks without ever saying anything false. Theoretical framework. According to H. P. Grice, conversation is governed by the Cooperative Principle, which includes four maxims: Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner. When a speaker appears to violate a maxim, the listener infers an implicature – an extra hidden meaning. The heuristic potential of Grice’s theory lies in turning the interpretation of implicatures from subjective guesswork into an analytical procedure. Main thesis. Effective counter-argumentation is not about refuting explicit theses but about working on implicit levels: 1) explicating hidden attacks, 2) creating counter-implicatures, and 3) using maxim violations as tactical weaknesses. Strategies for countering implicatures. Strategy 1 – Explication. Drag the hidden meaning into the light and then destroy it. By stating the implicature aloud (“You are trying to imply that…”), the opponent can now refute it directly as a false assumption or a false dilemma. Strategy 2 – Counter-implicature.
