Albert Cushing Crehore Born ( 1868-06-08 ) June 8, 1868 Cleveland, Ohio, United States Died 1958 Residence Cleveland, OH, United States Nationality USA Known for A non-Bohr theory of atomic structure, and an electromagnetic theory of gravitation Scientific career Fields Electrical engineering; atomic theory Albert Cushing Crehore (born 8 June 1868) was an American electrical engineer and physicist who made his name in the 1890s in instrumentation and alternating-current theory, and who from about 1911 devoted the rest of his long life to a theory of the atom that the physics of his day did not accept. The two halves sit oddly together. His obituary called him "the most brilliant electrical engineer in America at the turn of the century"; the textbook he wrote with Frederick Bedell in 1893 was among the first systematic treatments of alternating currents in English; and the measuring instrument he built with George Owen Squier was taken up by the United States Army. Then he turned to atomic structure, argued that electrons belong inside the nucleus, and spent four decades and four books defending a position that left him steadily further outside the profession he had begun in. Biography Education Yale University — graduated Electrical engineering — Johns Hopkins University Electrical engineering — Cornell University Career Crehore was born in Cleveland, Ohio, and taught at Dartmouth College, where he met and worked with George Owen Squier . He was later associated with Cornell University. By 1912 he was living in Yonkers, New York, where the newspaper account of his gravitation work described him as a "Yonkers scientist"; he ended his life in Cleveland. Engineering work Alternating currents With Frederick Bedell of Cornell, Crehore wrote Alternating Currents: An Analytical and Graphical Treatment for Students and Engineers (1893).
