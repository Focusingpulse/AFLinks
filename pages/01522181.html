Title What Happened to Electromagnetic Theory Read in full Link to paper Author(s) Ivor Catt Published 2012 Journal Proceedings of the NPA Volume 9 No. of pages 2 Pages 80-81 Abstract For 40 years I thought that the problem at the centre of classical electromagnetism arose in around 1890, when Heaviside showed that he was unsure about how to choose between two contradictory models for the Transverse Electromagnetic Wave. Although I quote him as backing "The Heaviside Signal" [4], the truth is that he vacillated between the two. However, recently I realized that the problem of misinterpretation of experimental results arose much earlier, with Faraday in 1831. Overview What Happened to Electromagnetic Theory is a two-page paper by Ivor Catt of St Albans, published in the Proceedings of the NPA vol. 9 (2012), pp. 80–81, and presented at the 19th Natural Philosophy Alliance Conference in July 2012. Its argument is that a misreading has sat at the centre of electromagnetism since 1831. Catt had long dated the trouble to Heaviside around 1890, "when Heaviside showed that he was unsure about how to choose between two contradictory models for the Transverse Electromagnetic Wave"; he now places it earlier, with Faraday's iron-ring experiment. The reinterpretation goes like this. When Faraday closed the switch on his primary coil, what advanced was not "electricity causing magnetism" but a transverse electromagnetic step wave, carrying four things at once — conduction current, magnetic flux, surface charge and electric flux. Some of it entered the secondary and reached the galvanometer, "which could only measure electric current and failed to detect the accompanying magnetic field." So "at no stage was there isolated electricity or isolated magnetism. Electricity did not cause magnetism which did not cause electricity."
