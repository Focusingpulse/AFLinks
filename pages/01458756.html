Dona Bailey

· archived # 1,458,756
In 1980, Atari’s only female video game programmer stood in a loud, dark arcade and noticed who was missing. The room was a wall of black screens and explosions. Young men hunched over glowing monitors, aggressively destroying digital asteroids. Dona Bailey did not fit the demographic. She came from General Motors. Her previous job involved writing assembly code for a microprocessor inside the Cadillac Seville. She programmed the digital climate control system. She learned assembly language because it was the architecture of the future, but she grew tired of making numbers appear on static displays. She wanted to build things that moved. When she accepted a position in the coin-op division at Atari headquarters in Sunnyvale, California, she stepped into an entirely foreign ecosystem. The company in 1980 was flush with cash. It operated under a notorious, highly unstructured corporate culture. There were kegs in the hallways. Engineers shouted over each other. It was loud. It was aggressive. It was heavily male. Out of the thirty programmers in the arcade division, Bailey was the only woman. She was twenty-six years old. She was handed a binder of potential game concepts. Most involved shooting ships in space. She flipped through the pages and found a single sentence about a multi-segment bug winding its way down the screen. She claimed it.
