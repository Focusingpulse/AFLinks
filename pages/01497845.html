Introduction to the Special Series of Papers in Accountability in Research Dealing With ìCold Fusionî Scott R. Chubb Washington, DC Background During the 14th century, the noted theologian and philosopher William of Ockham identified and applied the ìlaw of economy,î as a fundamental postulate of logical thought. Subsequently, Galileo and others used this ìlawî as justification for the notion of ìscientific parsimony.î This idea, which is also often called ìOckhamís Razor,î states that simplicity should be the cornerstone of scientific logic: given a choice between competing theories of a particular phenomenon, the simpler explanation should be selected in preference to the more complicated ones. Despite the fact that since the time of Galileo, Ockhamís Razor has helped to advance science, it frequently fails. One important reason for this is that the simplest explanations of particular phenomena need not be correct. Also, Ockhamís Razor cannot eliminate the problems that result from incorrect information. (It canít resolve the ìGarbage in; Garbage outî problem.) Applying it may cause an additional problem: a loss of important information. (Applying Ockhamís Razor, especially to new areas of science, may result in the problem of ìthrowing out the baby with the bathwater.î) However, as Ockham observed, applying the ìlaw of economyî does provide a means of isolating irrationally-based (dogmatic) opinions that have no logical basis. Also, provided its limitations are recognized, Ockhamís Razor can be used to provide a logical structure or context, defined by increasing levels of complexity, for a particular line of reasoning. For these reasons, as information about a particular subject evolves, by repetitively applying Ockhamís Razor over a period of time, frequently, it is possible to identify particular flaws in a particular line of reasoning.
