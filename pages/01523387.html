Mass is the property by which a body resists being accelerated and by which it gravitates. Those are two distinct descriptions — inertial mass, defined by Newton's second law, and gravitational mass, defined by the law of gravitation — and the fact that they agree is a measured result rather than a definition. Newton took the equality for granted; Loránd Eötvös tested it around 1900 with a torsion balance, and modern experiments, including the MICROSCOPE satellite mission, have confirmed it to roughly one part in 10 15 . Einstein made the equality a postulate, the equivalence principle, and built general relativity on it. Special relativity added a second relation: the energy content of a body at rest is E = mc 2 . In modern usage the mass of a particle means its invariant rest mass; the older notion of a "relativistic mass" increasing with speed has largely been abandoned in the professional literature in favour of speaking about relativistic momentum and energy, though it survives in textbooks. What mass actually is remains open even on the mainstream account. The Higgs mechanism, confirmed in its broad outline by the 2012 discovery of the Higgs boson, supplies masses to the elementary particles, but the values are free parameters fitted to experiment rather than predicted. And the Higgs accounts for only a small fraction of the mass of ordinary matter: the great majority of a proton's mass is the binding energy of the field inside it.
