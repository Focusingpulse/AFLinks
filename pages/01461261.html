Percy Julian

· archived # 1,461,261
The call came in the middle of the night. A pipe had burst at the Glidden Company factory in Chicago. A massive storage tank of soybean oil—representing thousands of dollars and weeks of work—was contaminated. By the time Percy Julian arrived at the plant, the damage looked catastrophic. Water had leaked into the tank, and a thick white sludge had formed at the bottom. The oil was ruined. The batch was worthless. Factory workers stood ready to drain the tank and scrap everything. Percy told them to stop. He climbed up to examine what everyone else saw as garbage. He peered into the tank at the foamy white substance coating the bottom. And in that moment, he saw something nobody else could see. He saw the future of medicine. The white substance wasn't mold or waste. It was a crystalline structure he recognized—stigmasterol, a plant steroid he'd been trying to isolate for months. The accidental water contamination had done what his laboratory couldn't. It had separated out the exact molecular key he needed. Percy Julian didn't see a disaster. He saw an answer. But to understand why this moment mattered, you need to understand the world Percy was trying to change—and the world that kept trying to stop him. Percy Lavon Julian was born on April 11, 1899, in Montgomery, Alabama. His grandparents had been enslaved. His father was a railway mail clerk.
