Saturday Morning Science Chats Summary An open chat hosted by Franklin Hu with Bill Lucas , James Keene , Stefan and a guest, Eugene, who had written asking to present a paper. The first stretch was domestic — Hu's indoor winter garden under cheap LED lamps, aluminium recycling, and a discussion of commercial maize, the density at which it is planted, its inedibility, and the Roundup litigation. Eugene: a biogenic origin for earthquakes Eugene presented what he calls the biogenic earthquake hypothesis, posted as a preprint on EarthArXiv: that microbial life beneath the surface may trigger earthquakes. His argument runs in stages. The accepted limit on the depth of the biosphere — twelve to fifteen kilometres — rests on temperature and pressure limits he thinks badly underestimated: the most extreme organism known before 2018 tolerated 122 to 130 °C, and a strain found that year survives well beyond it. Set against models of the earth's interior composition, he arrives at a possible limit near seventy-five kilometres, which is deep enough to matter for earthquakes. For how such organisms would feed, he pointed to a documented case: bacterial chains beneath the ocean floor that perform a distributed respiration, the deeper cells taking electrons from sulphur and passing them up the chain to cells at the surface that attach them to oxygen — effectively feeding on an electric current. He suggests telluric currents could do the same tens of kilometres down. He also gave energy estimates that he says are adequate even for a magnitude nine event.
