A.P. Giannini

· archived # 1,455,637
The San Francisco earthquake in 1906 left half the city burning. The major banks responded by locking their steel vaults. It was a standard procedural move. The board of directors at the city's largest financial institutions agreed it would prevent a run on cash. The money was secure behind heavy timed locks. The people standing in the ashes of their homes had nothing. A.P. Giannini was not a Montgomery Street financier. He was the son of Italian immigrants who had started out selling produce from a horse-drawn cart. He ran a single-room operation in the North Beach neighborhood. He called it the Bank of Italy. His clients were not railroad barons or shipping magnates. They were Portuguese fishermen, Italian bakers, and Irish dock workers. The financial establishment of San Francisco did not consider them a viable market. They did not own large estates. They lacked formal collateral. He was 36. A former produce broker. No formal banking education. No pedigree. No ties to the elite. An outsider. Giannini operated differently. He walked the wharves. He knew their names. He knew who worked six days a week and who drank their wages on Fridays. If a baker needed fifty dollars for an oven, or a fisherman needed twenty for a net, the standard banks required a property deed. Giannini required a look in the eye.
