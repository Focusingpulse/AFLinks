Nikolaus Otto

· archived # 1,461,020
He couldn't afford engineering school, so he sold tea and sugar for a living—then he invented the engine that powers the world. Nikolaus Otto wanted to be an engineer. He dreamed about it constantly. He wanted to attend technical school, to study machines, to build things that mattered. But his father—the village postmaster and innkeeper—had died shortly after Nikolaus was born. His mother was raising him alone. And in 1848, when the German revolution failed, the economy collapsed. Technical school was impossible. The family had no money. So at 16, Nikolaus took a job as a grocery store clerk. Then he became a traveling salesman, trudging across western Germany with cases full of tea, sugar, and kitchenware, knocking on doors, making small talk with shopkeepers. It wasn't engineering. But it paid the bills. He never stopped dreaming, though. While traveling his sales circuit in the late 1850s, Nikolausheard about something revolutionary: Etienne Lenoir, a French-Belgian inventor, had created the world's first workable internal combustion engine. It ran on gas. It actually worked. For the first time in history, humans had an engine that didn't require external combustion like steam engines did. But Lenoir's engine had massive problems. It was incredibly noisy—like an explosion happening over and over. It was wildly inefficient, wasting most of its energy as heat. It generated so much heat it was dangerous. And worst of all, it required expensive fuel that had to be transported
