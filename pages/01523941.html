Scientific Theory Name Steady State Theory Type Cosmological model Author(s) Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle (1948) Keywords perfect cosmological principle, continuous creation, Big Bang , quasi-steady state , eternal universe Year 1948 Steady state theory is the cosmological model in which the universe has no beginning and no end, presenting on the large scale the same appearance at every epoch, with new matter created continuously to keep the mean density constant as the universe expands. The standard account The theory was put forward in 1948 in two papers in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society : one by Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold , the other, on a different formal basis, by Fred Hoyle . Its governing assumption is the perfect cosmological principle — the universe is homogeneous and isotropic not only in space, as ordinary cosmology assumes, but also in time. There is therefore no privileged epoch, no "early universe," and no origin event. The model accepted the observed expansion. To keep the perfect cosmological principle in the face of expansion, matter must appear continuously; the required rate is very small, of the order of one hydrogen atom per cubic metre every billion years, far below any rate that could be detected directly. The universe is thus infinitely old, infinitely extended, and always the same in the mean, with galaxies of every age present everywhere at every time. The steady state model was the principal rival to the evolving hot-origin cosmology through the 1950s and early 1960s. It was Hoyle who gave the rival theory its name, using the phrase "big bang" dismissively in a 1949 BBC broadcast. The model was falsifiable in a way its rival was not: because it forbids evolution, any evidence that the distant — and therefore earlier — universe differs systematically from the nearby one counts directly against it.
