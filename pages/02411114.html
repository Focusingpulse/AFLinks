( click to enlarge ) Linoleic Acid (LA): Definition and Overview 1. What is Linoleic Acid? Linoleic acid (LA) is a polyunsaturated omega-6 fatty acid with the chemical formula C₁₈H₃₂O₂. It is one of the essential fatty acids, meaning the human body cannot synthesize it and must obtain it from dietary sources. Structure: 18 carbon atoms with two cis double bonds, making it a polyunsaturated fat. Omega-6: The first double bond is on the 6th carbon from the methyl (omega) end of the molecule. 2. Origin and Sources Linoleic acid is primarily derived from plant oils, especially industrial seed oils. These oils have been heavily promoted since the early 20th century but were largely absent from the ancestral human diet. High-LA Food Sources: Vegetable and seed oils: Soybean oil (~50% LA) Corn oil (~59% LA) Sunflower oil (~65% LA) Safflower oil (~75% LA) Cottonseed oil Grapeseed oil Processed foods: Most packaged snacks, fried foods, and baked goods Nuts and seeds (moderate levels, depending on type) Originally, humans consumed omega-6 and omega-3 in a balanced 1:1 to 4:1 ratio. Today, the ratio can exceed 20:1 in favor of omega-6—largely due to excess linoleic acid. 3. Role in the Body Linoleic acid plays some essential roles, including: Structural component of cell membranes (phospholipids) Precursor to arachidonic acid, which in turn produces prostaglandins and leukotrienes (signaling molecules in inflammation and immunity) But excessive intake distorts these pathways. 4. What It Does to the Body (Excessive Intake) a. Drives Chronic Inflammation LA is oxidized in the body to create oxidized linoleic acid metabolites (OXLAMs). These promote systemic inflammation, a known driver of: Cardiovascular disease Cancer Neurodegeneration Autoimmune conditions b. Promotes Obesity and Metabolic Dysfunction High-LA diets alter mitochondrial function and impair fat metabolism. LA contributes to insulin resistance and fatty liver disease. c.
