Tim Berners-Lee

· archived # 1,468,615
Tim Berners-Lee didn't set out to change the world. He set out to solve a problem. CERN, the European particle physics laboratory in Geneva, was the largest internet node in Europe. Thousands of researchers from dozens of countries passed through each year, each bringing their own computers, their own systems, their own incompatible ways of storing information. Finding and sharing documents was a nightmare. "Creating the web was really an act of desperation," Berners-Lee later said, "because the situation without it was very difficult." His solution combined several existing technologies in a new way. Hypertext—clickable links within documents—had existed since the 1960s. The internet already connected computers worldwide. Domain name systems made addressing possible. Berners-Lee's insight was to merge all of these into something seamless. In March 1989, he submitted his proposal: "Information Management: A Proposal." His manager, Mike Sendall, scribbled on the cover page: "Vague, but exciting." By late 1990, Berners-Lee had built the first web browser (which also served as an editor), the first web server, and the first website—a page explaining what the World Wide Web was and how to use it. He had invented HTML for creating pages, HTTP for transmitting them, and URLs for finding them. On August 6, 1991, the World Wide Web went public.
