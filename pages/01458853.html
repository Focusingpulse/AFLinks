Elda Emma Anderson

· archived # 1,458,853
In a government archive in Los Alamos, New Mexico, there is a locked drawer that holds the records of 1944. The Manhattan Project physicist who prepared the first pure sample of uranium-235 was systematically erased from public reports. Her calculations were critical, but her name was left out of the textbooks. Most people know the names of the men who built the atomic bomb: J. Robert Oppenheimer, Enrico Fermi, and others. History books show rooms full of men in loose suits, smoking cigarettes and writing complex equations on massive chalkboards. They tell us how the brightest minds in America gathered in the remote desert to split the atom—a story presented as one of towering male genius. The photographs from 1945 show rows of generals and scientists standing shoulder to shoulder. But the textbooks skip the basement laboratory where the physical uranium was actually handled. They skip the part where theoretical physics had to become real matter. Before a bomb could be built, someone had to figure out how to isolate the exact isotope required for a chain reaction. While the men at the top drafted the blueprints, they needed someone else to handle the raw, unstable elements. The name on that personnel file was Elda Emma Anderson. She was 44 years old. When she arrived at Los Alamos, the military had a theoretical weapon, but they needed a pure sample of uranium-235 to make it function.
