Dmitri Mendeleev

· archived # 1,458,747
In 1850, a widowed Russian mother traveled 4,000 miles across frozen Siberia with her sixteen-year-old son. She was dying, penniless, and determined. One university rejected them. She kept going. She got him admitted, then died weeks later. Nineteen years later, he gave the world the Periodic Table. Dmitri Mendeleev was the youngest of at least fourteen children born in Tobolsk, a remote Siberian town where winter lasts nine months and Moscow feels like another planet. In 1847, when Dmitri was thirteen, his father went blind and died shortly after. The family income vanished overnight. His mother, Maria Dmitrievna, was left with a house full of children and no means to support them. So she did something extraordinary. She reopened her family's abandoned glass factory and ran it herself—a widow managing industrial production in 1840s Siberia. For three years, she kept the factory running, kept food on the table, kept her youngest son in school. Then the factory burned to the ground. Maria lost everything. But she'd seen something in Dmitri—a hunger for knowledge, a mind that asked questions other children didn't think to ask. She made a decision that would cost her everything she had left. She would take him to St. Petersburg. To the imperial capital, where the universities were, where he could get an education that Siberia couldn't provide. It was 1850. Dmitri was sixteen. And Maria Dmitrievna began one of the most grueling journeys a parent has ever made for a child's future
