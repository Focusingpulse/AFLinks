nixtamalization: A process for the preparation of maize (corn) in which the grain is soaked and cooked in an alkaline solution, usually lime water, and hulled. That grey water in the kettle is made from wood ashes out of her own fireplace, and what it is doing to that corn is the reason her family will have bread when the meal runs low. Hominy. And the chemistry in that kettle was worked out on this continent thousands of years before anybody in these mountains was born. Dried field corn has a tough hull on every kernel and, more importantly, it locks up its niacin in a form the human body cannot get at. A people living mostly on corn without treating it will get pellagra, and in the American South in the early 1900s pellagra killed and disabled an enormous number of people. Soak that same corn in an alkaline solution and everything changes. The hull loosens and floats off. The kernel swells. And the niacin is released into a form the body can actually use. The process has a name, nixtamalization , and it came to Appalachian kitchens from Native peoples who had been doing it since long before contact. Mountain families made the alkali themselves, out of hardwood ashes leached with water, the same lye they made soap with, only weaker. Some used a pinch of baking soda later on. Then the corn got rinsed and rinsed and rinsed, and what came out was hominy, swollen and pearly and mild, to be eaten as it was, fried in grease, or dried and ground into grits. So a woman standing over an ash kettle in a cold yard is running a nutritional process she could not have explained in chemical terms and had absolutely right. She learned it from her mother. Her mother learned it from hers. And somewhere back up that line, somebody learned it from the people who were here first.
