Summary A Friday session by David de Hilster on a single claim, stated in the title: dark matter does not exist. His concern was that even in the dissident community people talk about it as real, often because a model that could explain it would be a prize. The argument His case rests on one comparison, which he said hit him like a ton of bricks. The upper image is a spherical galaxy — the second such reported to have no dark matter, which the mainstream found puzzling. The lower is a spiral like the Milky Way, whose edge stars do not move at the speed Kepler and Newton predict. The difference, in his reading, is in the caption: one is a sphere and one is not. What is actually done in the calculation, he argued, is to estimate the number of stars, estimate an average mass, and put the total at a point in the centre — which makes the gravitational field spherically symmetric. That is exactly right for a spherical galaxy, which is why those come out with no discrepancy and hence no dark matter; and it is wrong for a spiral, whose field is nothing like a sphere. The anomaly is therefore an artefact of the method, and dark matter was invented to cover it. He showed two pieces of supporting work from the society's own proceedings.
