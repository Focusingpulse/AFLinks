Michio Kaku

· archived # 1,460,722
Michio Kaku stood in a cramped Bronx apartment in the early 1960s, soldering copper wires to a two hundred dollar surplus accelerator he built himself, then flipped the switch and split atoms on his mother’s dining table because he believed a kid with no money could still chase the secrets of the universe. He was a teenager with a stack of physics books held together by tape. While classmates played ball, Kaku scavenged junkyards for parts. He tore motors out of abandoned machines. He rewired circuits in the garage. He bought capacitors with lawn mowing money. When he finished his homemade particle accelerator, neighbors said the lights on the entire block flickered every time he powered it on. Kaku smiled and kept working. He wanted to see the invisible. His talent did not stay hidden. Physicist Edward Teller heard about the boy building atom smashers in his garage and offered him a scholarship to Harvard. Kaku arrived with a notebook full of equations written in cramped handwriting. He studied relentlessly. He solved problems on cafeteria napkins. He asked professors questions that pushed far past the syllabus. When he graduated, he left behind a stack of research papers that already bent the edges of theoretical physics. His real breakthrough came with string field theory. Kaku believed the universe was built not from particles but from vibrating lines of energy. He filled blackboards with diagrams.
