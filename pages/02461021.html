Understanding Keely: The Physics of Cause and the Physics of Effects Draft for public circulation and adaptation as the Introduction to Volume 80* Before attempting to reproduce an effect, we must understand the conditions the author said would produce it. After decades of studying John Worrell Keely, I have come to recognize a distinction that helps bring his difficult writings and illustrations into focus. I call it the distinction between the Physics of Cause and the Physics of Effects. It does not answer every question about his work, but it changes the questions we ask—and the meanings we seek. A photograph shows an instrument. A drawing shows its arrangement. Neither, by itself, tells us what Keely believed made it operate. To approach that question, we must investigate his definitions, the relationships expressed in his charts, and the conditions described in his laws. What is the Physics of Effects? The Physics of Effects studies manifested behavior: a wheel rotates, a body becomes warm, a current flows, or two bodies attract or repel. We observe the event and measure its speed, temperature, force, duration, or other properties. We establish relationships between measurable quantities and use them to predict behavior. These investigations are indispensable. An explanation of rotation must ultimately account for actual rotation. An explanation of conduction must account for actual transfer. Here, however, “effects” identifies the level at which our attention begins: what the system does. It does not mean that Newtonian or contemporary physics ignores causes. Conventional physics investigates forces, fields, potentials, and mechanisms as well as motion. The distinction proposed in this article is a way of reading these historical works, rather than a division of all science into two exclusive camps. What is the Physics of Cause?
