Artifical Intelligence

· archived # 1,455,818
In 1955, John McCarthy sat at his desk at Dartmouth College with a problem that didn't technically exist yet: he needed a name for machines that could think. Computers in 1955 were room-sized calculators that crashed constantly and required punch cards just to add numbers. The idea that they could ever "think" seemed absurd. But McCarthy, just 27 years old, believed the human mind could be replicated in code—not its biology, but its logic, its reasoning, its ability to learn. He needed a phrase that captured this impossible dream. So he invented one: "artificial intelligence." The term didn't exist until he typed it in a research proposal. Then he mailed invitations to a small group of researchers—Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, Nathaniel Rochester, and a few others—proposing they meet at Dartmouth in the summer of 1956 to explore whether machines could be made to "use language, form abstractions and concepts, solve kinds of problems now reserved for humans, and improve themselves." That summer workshop became known as the Dartmouth Conference—the official birth of artificial intelligence as a field of study. What none of them realized was that McCarthy had just started a race that would still be running 70 years later, with stakes none of them could imagine. McCarthy wasn't interested in building calculators or business machines. He wanted to understand human intelligence by recreating it.
