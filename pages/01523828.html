Saturday Morning Science Chat for those critical thinkers in science not afraid to take on the establishment. Summary Nobody came into the live room, so Franklin Hu ran what he called a mailbag: reading out the week's email correspondence on the nature of light and answering it, with comments coming in from the stream. Carl was on air; Roger Anderton was watching. It is a one-sided session, but it collects the arguments Hu returns to more compactly than any other. Rydin's questions Roger Rydin had asked the fundamental questions. He had come to think an ether must exist but be uniform everywhere — in which case, how does it pass light? What is disturbed, and what gives back? At best a photon is a wavelet, a short piece of wave, carrying energy and momentum but no mass; individual events like photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering and pair production add up to what we call light waves — but are they really waves? Might light and gravity simply be there , one passing and the other static, without all the supporting machinery? We have invented wave mechanics to help us visualise a process, he wrote, and that does not make it so. Hu's answers, in order: Gravity as a form of light. The parallels are strong — both travel indefinitely, both fall off as one over r ² — and if gravity is a frequency of light, its unlimited range is explained. His image: every pinprick of starlight we can see represents a gravitational source whose field reaches us by exactly the same means. Uniformity. This is where he thought Rydin had created his own difficulty. A perfectly uniform medium could carry nothing, since a wave is a non-uniformity.
