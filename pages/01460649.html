Marion Donovan was born in 1917 in Indiana, into a world where women were expected to follow certain paths: marriage, motherhood, homemaking. Invention was not supposed to be one of them. But Marion’s childhood was different. She lost her mother when she was young. Instead of being sent to relatives or a boarding school, she remained with her father, an engineer whose work shaped early industrial manufacturing. While other children played with dolls and tea sets, Marion spent her time in her father’s workshop, surrounded by tools, blueprints, and the constant rhythm of problem-solving. She learned to look at the world the way an engineer does: nothing was fixed, everything could be improved, and every frustration was really an invitation to redesign. That instinct appeared early. While she was still in grade school, she brought her father an idea for a cleaning product. Most parents might have smiled and told her to focus on school. Her father did not. He helped her think it through, test it, and improve it. That early support taught Marion a lesson she would carry for the rest of her life: ordinary frustrations could become opportunities.
