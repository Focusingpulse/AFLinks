Christian Andreas Doppler

· archived # 1,456,338
In 1842, he proposed that moving objects change the sound and light around them. Scientists called it absurd. Three years later, a train full of trumpet players proved him right. Christian Andreas Doppler was dying. It was 1842, and the 39-year-old Austrian physicist was suffering from tuberculosis—a disease that would kill him within a decade. He was a mathematics professor in Prague, relatively unknown in the scientific world, and he was running out of time to make his mark. So he published a paper with an idea so strange, so counterintuitive, that most scientists dismissed it outright. Doppler proposed that sound and light don't stay constant when their source is moving. If a sound source moves toward you, he argued, the sound waves bunch up—making the pitch higher. If it moves away, the waves stretch out—making the pitch lower. The same principle, he suggested, applied to light. Stars moving toward Earth would appear slightly bluer. Stars moving away would appear slightly redder. This meant you could measure the motion of distant stars just by analyzing their color. The scientific community's response was skeptical at best. The idea that motion could change sound or light seemed to violate common sense. How could a trumpet note change pitch just because the trumpeter was moving? Doppler had the theory. He had the mathematics. But he had no proof. He needed an experiment. And he was too sick, too poor, and too obscure to conduct one himself. Enter Buys Ballot. Christophoru
