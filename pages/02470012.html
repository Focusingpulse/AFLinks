True, it was the “lead” story in most newspapers, but to the average American, occupied with his own problems and diversions, the assassinations were looked upon as just another local squabble in a section of Europe where passions had always been aroused easily. The reader of the Schenectady Gazette picked up his paper on Monday morning June 29, 1914, and read the headline, “Student Kills Heir to Austrian Throne.” He was told how “the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, Archduke Francis Ferdinand, and his morganatic wife, the Duchess of Hohenberg, were shot to death today while driving through the streets of Sarajevo, the Bosnian capital. A youthful eighth grade Servian student fired the shots that ended their lives and added another to the long list of terrible tragedies that have darkened the reign of Emperor Francis Joseph...” If he wanted sidelights on the event, the reader could ponder the beliefs of Nikola Tesla, electrical inventor, who in an interview in New York city said he hoped that “this act will be treated as the deed of irresponsible maniacs, and will end there.
