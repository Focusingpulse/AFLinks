Edward Arthur Milne Born ( 1896-02-14 ) 14 February 1896 Hull, Yorkshire, England Died 21 September 1950 (1950-09-21) (aged 54) Dublin, Ireland Nationality British Known for Kinematic relativity, the cosmological principle, stellar atmospheres Awards Gold Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society (1935) Scientific career Fields Astrophysics, Mathematics, Cosmology Institutions University of Cambridge; University of Manchester; University of Oxford Edward Arthur Milne (14 February 1896 – 21 September 1950) was a British astrophysicist and mathematician, Rouse Ball Professor of Mathematics at Oxford from 1928, who built a complete cosmology out of the special theory of relativity and a symmetry postulate, without using general relativity or curved space at all. He named that postulate the cosmological principle . Milne's first career was in stellar physics. He worked out the theory of radiative transfer in stellar atmospheres, the Milne–Eddington approximation for line formation, the theory of the outer layers of stars, and the escape of atoms from stellar surfaces. He disagreed sharply with Eddington over the internal constitution of stars, arguing for degenerate cores against Eddington's standard model. He was awarded the Gold Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1935 and was its President from 1943 to 1945. Kinematic relativity From 1932 Milne turned to cosmology. His starting point was that the general theory of relativity smuggles in far more structure than the evidence warrants, and that a cosmology should be deduced from as little as possible. He assumed only special relativity, a set of equivalent fundamental observers, and the requirement that the universe look the same to all of them — his cosmological principle. From this alone he derived a linear velocity–distance relation of Hubble's form.
