Introduction It is well known that the intense implosive collapse of bubbles, including acoustic cavitation bubbles, can lead to extremely high compressions and temperatures, and to the generation of light flashes attributed to sonoluminescence (SL). In addition, for suitable conditions and materials, we can also have nuclear emissions (1a). The modeling and analyses of the basic physical phenomena associated with such a process have been discussed in detail elsewhere (1b,2,3). Essentially, the process starts with bubble implosion, during the compression phase of the impressed acoustic pressure field, when the gas/vapor interfacial Mach number ( RCg )  is much less than unity. As the interfacial Mach number approaches unity a compression shock wave is formed in the gas/vapor mixture and this shock wave moves toward the center of the bubble and, in doing so, intensifies.
