Eugene Cernan

· archived # 1,459,012
He floated outside his spacecraft unable to see, unable to breathe properly, his heart rate at 195 beats per minute—and mission control couldn't bring him back in. June 5, 1966. Low Earth orbit. Eugene Cernan opened the hatch of Gemini 9A and prepared for humanity's most ambitious spacewalk to date. The plan: spend two hours outside the spacecraft testing a backpack that would let astronauts maneuver independently in space—a crucial technology for future Moon missions. What actually happened nearly killed him. Cernan was a Navy fighter pilot before he became an astronaut. He'd flown A-4 Skyhawks off aircraft carriers—one of the most dangerous jobs in military aviation. Carrier landings were controlled crashes onto moving decks. Mistakes meant the ocean or fire. That training had taught him one crucial lesson: when systems fail, you execute procedure while your body screams otherwise. In space, every system was about to fail. The moment Cernan left the spacecraft, things went wrong. Moving in zero gravity while wearing a pressurized suit turned out to be exponentially harder than anyone had predicted. Every movement required enormous physical effort. The suit resisted bending. Simple tasks became exhausting. His heart rate spiked to 155, then 180, then 195 beats per minute—far beyond safe limits.
