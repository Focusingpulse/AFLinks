Sympathetic Vibratory Physics

· archived # 2,410,909
Thomas Midgley Jr., an American chemical engineer born in 1889 in Dayton, Ohio, may go down in history as the most dangerously influential man to ever live—though likely without intending harm. Early in his career, while working to improve fuel efficiency, Midgley discovered that adding iodine slightly reduced engine knocking. Unsatisfied, he spent six more years searching for a better solution, eventually developing tetraethyl lead in 1921. Marketed as “ethyl” gasoline by General Motors, this leaded fuel dramatically improved engine performance and was quickly adopted worldwide. However, it released vast amounts of toxic lead into the atmosphere, poisoning ecosystems and people for decades—including Midgley himself, though he never publicly acknowledged the risks. Perhaps burdened by the consequences of his first invention, Midgley turned his focus to safer alternatives for refrigeration, which at the time relied on harmful chemicals like ammonia and sulfur dioxide. In 1928, he introduced chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), specifically Freon—a chemical that seemed revolutionary: non-toxic, non-flammable, and stable. Unfortunately, it would later be discovered that CFCs destroy the ozone layer, allowing dangerous ultraviolet radiation to penetrate the atmosphere. Despite their ban beginning in 1987, the damage they caused remains a cautionary tale of unintended consequences from even well-meaning innovations. In a tragic and ironic twist, Midgley’s life ended by his own hand—unintentionally. After contracting polio at 51 and becoming partially paralyzed, he invented a system of pulleys and ropes to help lift himself from bed. Four years later, in 1944, he became entangled in the very device he had created and died of strangulation. Though Midgley held 171 patents and was known for his intellect and love of music and poetry, his inventions altered the planet more than any single individual—making him, inadvertently, the man who may have harmed Earth the most.
