In 1999, archaeologist Dennis Stanford, of the Smithsonian Institution’s National Museum of Natural History, resurrected and elaborated the idea that the ancestors of North America’s Late Pleistocene Clovis people were Solutreans of the European Upper Paleolithic. Almost all professionals rejected this notion, which was based on notable similarities between the lithic artifacts of the two manifestations. Now, with Exeter University (UK) archaeologist Bruce Bradley, Stanford has put out a major book displaying the hypothesis and arguing for it (Stanford & Bradley 2012, to be reviewed in a later issue), and a certain number of their colleagues are taking this striking suggestion seriously. Most recently, Stanford points especially to lithics recovered from six mid-Atlantic sites, three of which are in the Eastern Shore area of the Delmarva Peninsula to the east of Chesapeake Bay. The implements look very like European ones dating to between 19,000 and 26,000 years ago. Previously, the earliest American tools showing such similarities had been no more than 15,000 years old, much later in time than the Solutrean ones they resembled, and most specialists felt that this time gap was unbridgeable. However, data diminishing the time-gap problem have been accumulating.
