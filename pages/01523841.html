Saturday Morning Science Chats Summary A short session over the Thanksgiving weekend, with Franklin Hu and James Keene the only participants and Keene's connection failing badly in the last quarter hour, which ended the chat early. It was a two-way conversation throughout, and the better for it. Keene's density ranges Keene reported extending an earlier study by parsing the whole range of initial quanta density in his simulator, from absolute vacuum — no one-state bits at all — up to maximum, where every locus is occupied. The ranges he now identifies: above about 0.75 a lepton–quark soup; between roughly 0.66 and 0.75, plasma; and below that, down to about 0.1, the range in which atoms occur. He noted that the higher end of the atomic range appears to favour higher- Z atoms, which he takes as consistent with the astrophysical account of heavy elements forming in large explosions. Hu queried that immediately, and the question was a good one: if all atoms are made of the same protons, neutrons and electrons, why should their bit density differ? Keene did not evade it but did not settle it either; his answer went by way of the simulation's behaviour rather than the chemistry. Run in vacuum mode, anything with momentum leaves the volume and is lost, so the volume cools to what he calls absolute zero — no radiation out, no particle motion — a state unreachable in the laboratory but exact in simulation. Plotting the final density against the initial, he finds that at very high initial densities the final density is near zero: the simulation shows an explosion in every direction, and what is left is almost nothing.
