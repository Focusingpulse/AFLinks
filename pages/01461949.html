Samuel Slater

· archived # 1,461,949
In 1789, a 21-year-old English apprentice named Samuel Slater memorized the closely guarded designs of Britain’s advanced textile machinery, boarded a ship in secret, and smuggled that knowledge to the struggling United States. Britain, then the unquestioned industrial giant of the world, had outlawed the export of both its machines and the skilled workers who knew how to build them. But Slater slipped through, earning in his homeland the furious nickname “Slater the Traitor.” What he carried to America was more valuable than gold: the blueprint of mechanized wealth. Within a few years, Slater built the first successful water-powered cotton spinning mill in the new republic, and with it he lit the fuse of the American Industrial Revolution. Until that moment, the United States was largely an agrarian backwater, dependent on Europe for manufactured goods and lacking the technological means to compete. Slater’s stolen designs changed that overnight. His mills multiplied across New England, giving birth to factories, machine shops, railroads, banks, and eventually the enormous industrial base that would fuel westward expansion, dominate global trade, and outproduce entire empires in times of war. Long before Silicon Valley or Wall Street, America’s climb to superpower status began with an act of intellectual theft—one young man carrying Britain’s forbidden machines in his head.
