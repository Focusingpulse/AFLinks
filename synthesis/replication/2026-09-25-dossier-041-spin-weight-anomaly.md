## The claim (as asserted by the sources)

**"The weight of a rotating object is modified under certain conditions."** This is the headline of the Boudet document, and it is the founding experimental claim of the torsion-field lineage.

Two distinct measurements are in the archive:

1. **Kozyrev (1951–1984, Soviet).** Precision beam-balance weighings of gyroscopes whose rotation speed is held constant by an electric supply, the gyroscope **enclosed in a hermetically sealed box to prevent any disturbance by an air current** (the source's own words). Reported result: **8 mg reduction for a 90 g gyroscope of 4.6 cm diameter spinning at 25 m/s at its periphery**, proportional to the gyroscope's weight and to its peripheral speed — **but only when the axis is vertical and the rotation is counterclockwise; the reduction is zero when it spins clockwise.** The source adds that "these differences are partly due to the Earth's rotation."

2. **Hayasaka & Takeuchi (Tohoku, 1989), published in *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 63, 2701.** Three gyroscopes (rotor masses 140, 175, 176 g), vertical axis, inertial rotation. Reported: **right rotation (spin vector down) causes a weight decrease of the order of milligrams, proportional to frequency between 3,000 and 13,000 rpm; left rotation causes no change.** Note that this is the **opposite sense** to Kozyrev's — the two sources disagree on which direction reduces weight.

**The condition the source itself names, and which the card must carry:** *"weight modifications on rotating objects … are observed only if they are subjected to an additional movement, for example a vibration. The vibrations are produced by a motor acting on the suspension point of the beam."* (Kozyrev 1967.) A rotating object alone is, by the source's own account, not enough.

**Kozyrev's own instrument, described in the source:** to gain sensitivity he used balances "whose beam is a light thin rod, suspended horizontally at its middle by a nylon thread. There is no pan. In their place, the weights are hung directly from the ends of the rod. The balance is made even more sensitive if the beam is asymmetrical."

**The related DePalma experiments (same document, same claim family):** two identical 2.5 cm steel balls launched together in low-pressure space, one spun by a carpenter's router at 18,000–27,000 rpm — *"the rotating ball rises higher and falls back down faster."* And a gyroscope assembly of 3.28 kg dropped 3.2 m with optical timing: *"the gyroscope falls faster when its rotor is turning at 15,000 rpm than when its rotor is stationary."*

**Honest status of the claim — stated up front, and this is the spine of the card:**

- **The claim has been tested and refuted.** Four independent groups (NIST, LBNL, the UK National Physical Laboratory, and a Japanese team) repeated the gyroscope-weighing experiment within two years of the 1989 publication and **all four found nothing**. The NIST and LBNL limits are **two orders of magnitude below** the claimed effect. Quinn & Picard's paper is the most instructive: they *did* see an apparent mass change, and it **disappeared once the friction couple of the spinning rotor and the temperature change were corrected for.** That is a textbook false positive, and it names the two confounds this card must control.
- **The archive still carries the claim as live.** The Boudet document (2017) presents it as established and does not mention the null results. The Li Si-Chen paper (2006) does mention that "others later claimed that the weight of a rotating gyroscope does not change," and **dismisses them** — *"it was pointed out that those gyroscopes whose weight did not change were not being moved, and therefore no effect was seen."* That is a claim about the null experimenters, offered without a citation, and it is exactly the shape of argument the engine exists to test rather than repeat.
- **Kozyrev's own version was never independently replicated.** The 1990–91 nulls tested the Hayasaka–Takeuchi version. Kozyrev's beam-balance work sits in the Soviet literature and was not independently repeated outside it. So the card tests the **claim class**, and it says so.
- **A null is the expected outcome** — and here, unlike the queue's other cards, the null is not merely likely, it is **already in the literature**. That is the point of the card (see "Why it matters").

**Claimants:** N. A. Kozyrev (Soviet, 1951–1984, via Boudet 2017 and Li Si-Chen 2006); H. Hayasaka & S. Takeuchi (Tohoku University, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 63, 2701, 1989); B. DePalma (MIT/Polaroid, 1935–1997)
**Verification status:** **refuted** — four independent null results, 1990–91 (Faller et al. NIST; Nitschke & Wilmarth LBNL; Quinn & Picard NPL; Imanishi et al. Tohoku). The archive's sources do not report this.
**Mainstream position:** no accepted mechanism; the reported effect is attributed to measurement artifact — specifically the spin-down friction couple and temperature drift, which Quinn & Picard demonstrated and then corrected away.

## The protocol — one rotor, one balance, one direction-difference

The testable core: **if a spinning rotor's weight depends on the direction of rotation about a vertical axis, then a rotor weighed at the same speed clockwise and counterclockwise should show a repeatable weight difference larger than the balance's own repeatability.** If it does not, the claim does not reproduce at home scale — which is what the literature says will happen.

The design has one job the other cards did not have: **the confounds are already known and named in the literature, so the protocol must control them explicitly rather than discover them.**

### Materials (~$100–180)

1. **A precision scale, 0.001 g (1 mg) resolution**, capacity ≥ 300 g (~$40–70). The claimed effect is a few milligrams on a ~150 g rotor, so 1 mg resolution is the floor, not a luxury. **A 0.01 g scale cannot see the claim** — say so rather than pretend.
2. **A rotor and a motor that will spin it at a steady, settable speed.** A metal disc (brass or steel) of 90–180 g on a shaft, driven by a small brushless motor and ESC, or a brushed motor with a speed controller (~$30–50). Target speeds ~3,000 and ~8,000 rpm.
3. **A rigid platform** (aluminium or plywood) that carries the motor and rotor and sits on the scale pan, with the **axis vertical**.
4. **A vibration source** — a small unbalanced motor or a piezo disc clamped to the platform, running at the same setting in every arm (~$5). The source names vibration as a *requirement*, so it must be present, and it must be present **identically** in the clockwise and counterclockwise arms.
5. **A tachometer** — a phone strobe/tach app is enough; an optical tach is better (~$0–20).
6. **An enclosure** — a clear plastic box over the whole rig to stop air currents. Kozyrev's own gyroscope was in a sealed box for exactly this reason.
7. **A reference mass** (e.g. a 100 g calibration weight) to check scale drift between arms.
8. **A thermometer** on the rotor or the platform (many scales have one; a $5 probe is fine).
9. **Power leads with a slack service loop** so the leads do not load the pan.

### Design — the parts that make it a measurement

1. **The comparison is clockwise versus counterclockwise, not spinning versus still.** This is the whole design. Vibration, air movement, motor heating, the motor's own magnetic field, and the scale's drift are all **identical** in the two arms; only the sense of rotation differs. A spinning-versus-still comparison would be swamped by all of them at once.
2. **A vibration-off arm, because the source makes it a condition.** The source says the effect appears *only* with an additional movement. So run CW and CCW **with the vibrator off** as well. This is a **discriminating prediction**: if a direction-difference appears with the vibrator off, the source's own stated condition is wrong.
3. **The off arm is the noise floor, not a warm-up.** With the motor energised but the rotor decoupled (or the motor off) and the vibrator on, take the same number of readings. The spread of that arm **is** the rig's repeatability, and every verdict is measured against it.
4. **Steady speed, not spin-down.** Quinn & Picard's artifact came from weighing a rotor *as it spun down* — the friction couple and temperature were changing throughout. Hold the speed constant and let the temperature stabilise before reading.
5. **Temperature is logged, not ignored.** Record the rotor/platform temperature in every arm. Quinn & Picard's apparent effect vanished after temperature correction; if a direction-difference tracks temperature, it is an artifact and the card says so.
6. **Interleave the arms** (off, CW, CCW, off, CW, CCW …) so slow drift cancels, and weigh the reference mass between arms.
7. **Five sessions on different days.** The source itself notes "the effect varies according to the day" — so a single session cannot settle it.
8. **Pre-register** the speeds, the arm order, the number of readings, the thresholds, and the scoring rule before the first reading.

### Endpoints

- **Primary — the direction-difference.** Δ = mean(CW) − mean(CCW) at each speed, in milligrams, with its session-to-session spread.
- **Primary — the signal-to-noise ratio.** |Δ| ÷ the off-arm repeatability (the standard deviation of repeated off-arm means).
- **Secondary — speed dependence.** Whether |Δ| grows between the low and high speed, as both sources claim.
- **Secondary — the vibration condition.** Δ with the vibrator **off** — predicted by the source to be ≈ 0.
- **Control — temperature.** Whether Δ tracks the rotor temperature difference between the CW and CCW arms.

### Pre-registered pass/fail

- **PASS:** a direction-difference **|Δ| ≥ 3× the off-arm repeatability**, of the **same sign in ≥4 of 5 sessions**, **growing with speed**, and **absent (≈0) with the vibrator off** — i.e. the source's own conditions are reproduced. Because the two sources disagree on sign (Kozyrev: counterclockwise reduces; Hayasaka: clockwise reduces), **either sign counts as a pass**; the sign is reported as which lineage it matches.
- **FAIL:** **|Δ| ≤ the off-arm repeatability in ≥4 of 5 sessions** → no direction-dependent weight anomaly at home scale. **This is the expected result and it matches the four published nulls.** It is a complete result, and it earns the Skeptic's Star.
- **VOID:** the off-arm repeatability exceeds the predicted effect size (≈8 mg × rotorMass/90 g × peripheralSpeed/25 m/s) → the rig cannot resolve the claim. **Report the noise floor and the resolution required, not a verdict.** A scale that cannot see the effect has not tested it.
- **ARTIFACT:** Δ tracks the rotor temperature difference between arms, or appears with the vibrator off, or vanishes when the speed is held constant instead of run down → the reading is the known artifact, and the card records it as such.

## What a result means (and what it does not)

- A **pass** means: this rig, on this bench, on these days, found a direction-dependent weight difference that survived the controls. It would be a **replication of a claim four laboratories refuted** — and the first thing to do with it is hunt for the artifact, because Quinn & Picard's paper shows exactly how one is produced. A pass is a reason to look harder, not a reason to believe.
- A **fail** means: at home scale, the direction-difference is below the rig's own noise. **This is the published result and the expected one.** It is a complete, honest, publishable outcome.
- Either way the confounds must be named in the report: **the friction couple of the spinning rotor**, **temperature drift**, **vibration**, **the scale's own repeatability**, and **the fact that a pass at 1 mg resolution cannot be compared to a limit of 0.07 mg**.
