## Source lineage

The Bovis scale is the numeric instrument of French radiesthesia — a graduated dial ("biometro") over which a pendulum swing is read and converted into a number, the *taux vibratoire* in Bovis units (UB). It is used to rate places, water, food, minerals, and people.

Two facts about it come from the corpus itself, not from critics:

1. **It had a physical anchor, and the anchor was cut.** The original scale was tied to a physical unit — **1 UB ≈ 1 ångström, with 6,500 Å = red light.** The corpus records that the human baseline was **quietly rescaled in 2014 from 6,500 to 12,500 UB (+92%) with no instrument change**; dials went 18,000 → 60,000 → 120,000; one popular source now puts Earth's "vibrational level" at **2.39 million UB, climbing 2–3,000 per day**. **ACMOS** — a body inside the tradition — calls a 400,000 UB goji-berry reading *"totalement loufoque"* and warns that dropping the ångström correspondence is where the numbers go insane.
2. **The critique of the drift names a mechanism that is testable.** The corpus's phrasing: *"re-label the ruler and the pendulum will adapt itself."* That is not a rhetorical jab; it is a **falsifiable prediction about the readout**, and the dial-swap control below tests it directly.

The tradition still has a real calibration practice — the **horseshoe magnet pole-flip**, the standard reference against which a reader re-zeroes. This card uses the tradition's own anchor rather than inventing a yardstick for it.

## The claim (as asserted by the source)

That a trained reader, holding a pendulum over a sample or over its carrier disc, can state the sample's rate **as a number on the Bovis scale**, and that this number is a property of the sample. The claim has three separable parts, and the tradition usually states them as one:

- **(a) It tracks the world.** The number is different for samples that are physically different.
- **(b) It is a measurement, not an impression.** The same sample gets the same number from the same reader on different days, and comparable numbers from different readers. This is what separates an instrument from a vocabulary.
- **(c) The scale is stable.** The graduations mean what they meant last year.

**(c) is already known to be false on the tradition's own record** — the 2014 re-scale, with no instrument change. That is not a reason to skip the card; it is the reason the card is interesting. **(a) and (b) have never been tested at all.**

**Honest framing:** This is a *claim* held by a living practice, cited here from the archive's own digests rather than from a peer-reviewed report. The tradition's instrument is a pendulum, and its stated blind spot is that the pendulum's swing cannot be separated from the ideomotor response by the reader alone (Chevreul, 1854 — the Yard's first pendulum validation). The protocol below does not try to see the field; it tries to determine **whether the number behaves like a number.** We hold no brief — the test is the point.

## Why it matters

This is the cheapest decisive test the geobiology lane has, and it settles a question the whole rail leans on. Every downstream Bovis-based claim in the archive — sacred-site ratings, geopathic-stress readings, food and water ratings, the `sources/scout-report-2026-09-19-0000.md` shelf of modern French radiesthesia guides — inherits its authority from a scale nobody has calibrated in public.

And the card is deliberately **not** another hit-rate test. ## Replicability: `home` (kitchen-table scale)

No instrument is required that a household does not already have. The critical piece of apparatus — **the second, silently re-scaled dial** — is a printout. Total build under an hour.

## Apparatus (Bill of Materials)

### Core components
- **1 horseshoe magnet** — the tradition's own calibration reference. A strong ferrite or alnico horseshoe is sufficient (~$5–10); it must have visually distinguishable poles.
- **1 pendulum** (or a bob on a thread) and **1 pair of 70 cm metal rods** — the reader's normal instruments. The reader should use whatever they normally use.
- **12–20 identical opaque containers** with lids — tins, jars, or card boxes, matched in material and shape.
- **Filler of matched mass** — dry sand or rice, so every container weighs the same to within ±5%.
- **5 matched test pairs** — two samples per pair, equal in mass and shape, chosen so the *tradition* predicts a difference. Suggested pairs, all cheap: (i) a magnet vs a matched iron slug; (ii) sea salt vs sugar; (iii) quartz pebble vs glass pebble of the same mass; (iv) copper disc vs aluminium disc of the same mass; (v) spring water vs tap water in identical bottles. **The tradition, not the experimenter, supplies the expected direction** — write the expectation down before session 1.
- **Two printed Bovis dials** — *this is the critical item.* **Dial A:** the standard scale, printed as the tradition prints it. **Dial B:** the *same artwork* with the graduations scaled ×2 and the numerals relabelled accordingly, so that the dial looks normal and the reader is not told anything has changed.

### Measurement equipment
- **A kitchen scale** (±1 g) — to match the containers.
- **A notebook or spreadsheet** — the per-trial log, written as the trial runs.
- **A camera or phone** — to photograph the sealed key and the dials.

### Safety equipment
- Keep the horseshoe magnet away from pacemakers, bank cards, mechanical watches, and any ferrous part of the pendulum rig. Store it away from the containers between sessions.

## Protocol

**Sessions:** **6 sessions over ≥ 3 weeks, at most 2 per week.** Three arms, run in every session.

**Roles.** The **reader** reads. The **coder** is a second person who alone holds the key, randomizes the presentations, and never reads. Rotate the coder across sessions if you can.

**The reader's freedoms (record them, do not constrain them).** The reader uses their own instrument, their own holding posture, and their own standard calibration ritual — including the horseshoe pole-flip if that is what they normally do. Write down what they did, in their words, before session 1. This is the design element that made the Argenton protocol hold up: the tradition sets its own conditions, so a negative is not "you tested it wrong."

### Arm A — The anchor test (the tradition's own physical reference)
**20 trials per session, at least one session run to 20.**

1. The coder flips a coin (or draws a shuffled number) to select one pole of the horseshoe magnet.
2. The magnet is presented behind a screen or in a box, **poles marked only by the coder**, so the reader cannot see which pole faces them. The face may be covered with paper; the poles must not be visually distinguishable.
3. The reader declares **north or south**.
4. The coder scores immediately and records the trial.

*Why this arm:* the pole flip is the tradition's own reference and it is a real, known, flippable physical difference. This is the cleanest available test of whether the reader's instrument resolves *anything*.

### Arm B — The discrimination test (does the quoted number track a real difference?)
**5 pairs, each presented twice in randomized order = 10 readings per session, at least 2 sessions.**

1. The coder loads both members of a pair into identical, mass-matched containers, and seals them. The key says which is which.
2. Containers are presented in a randomized order, blind.
3. The reader **quotes a Bovis number aloud** for each container; the coder writes it down.
4. After all pairs are read, the key is opened and the ranking compared with the expectation written down before session 1.

*Why this arm:* it produces the *number*, not a hit/miss — and the number is the thing the tradition uses downstream.

### Arm C — The ruler test (the arm the tradition has never run)
This is the card's real contribution.

**C1 — Baseline stability.** At the **start and end of every session**, the reader quotes **their own human baseline** in UB, with **no reference given and no object present.** That is 12 readings across 6 sessions. Report the **coefficient of variation** and the raw sequence. A number that behaves like an instrument is stable; a number that drifts with the reader's state is a state variable wearing a unit.

**C2 — The dial swap.** In **sessions 3 and 6**, the reader reads the **same blind sample twice**: once against **Dial A**, once against **Dial B** (the silently ×2 re-scaled dial). Order is randomized by the coder. The reader is told "here is the dial" both times and is told nothing else.

**The decisive calculation:** for each dial-swap trial, compute the ratio
**`R = (UB quoted on Dial B) ÷ (UB quoted on Dial A)`** for the *same* sample.

- If **R ≈ 1** (within ±20%), the quoted number does not follow the graduations — the readout is tracking something independent of the document, and the tradition's scale can be defended.
- If **R ≈ 2** — i.e. the number doubles when the graduations double — then the readout is a function of the ruler and not the sample, and the corpus's stated mechanism (*"re-label the ruler and the pendulum will adapt itself"*) has been caught in the act, on the tradition's own instrument, with the tradition's own reader, at a kitchen table.

**This row needs no theory to interpret.** It is the cheapest decisive result the card can produce, and it is the one that speaks directly to the 2014 re-scale: if numbers track dials, the re-scale did not measure a change in the world, it produced one.

### Controls
- The reader is **blind to container contents at all times.** Only the coder touches the key.
- Presentations are **randomized** — coin flip or a shuffled coded list — and the mechanism is **recorded** (the omission the 2007 Argenton critique named as the OZ's weak point).
- The reader **declares all endpoints in writing before session 1**, and does not change them. The expectation table for Arm B pairs is written and dated before the first reading.
- **Ambient conditions are recorded, not controlled** — date, time of day, room, and anything else the tradition says affects a reading. Recording them is how a null gets its fair chance.
- **A second household repeats the whole thing** before any positive is treated as a result. One reader is one data point.

## Pass / fail criteria (pre-registered)

**PASS (the readout behaves like a measurement):**
- Arm A: **≥ 15 of 20** correct pole identifications (one-sided binomial p ≈ 0.021; a stricter reading would be ≥ 16, p ≈ 0.006); **and**
- Arm B: **≥ 8 of 10** pair orders ranked in the direction the tradition predicts (one-sided p ≈ 0.055); **and**
- Arm C2: the dial-swap ratio **R stays within ±20% of 1** across the swap trials.

→ Report it with the raw numbers, and hand it to a second household immediately. This would be an anomaly deserving maximum scrutiny, not a victory lap.

**FAIL (the claim is retired):**
- Arm A at chance (≤ 10 of 20, or any result not reaching the declared threshold); **or**
- Arm B at chance; **or**
- Arm C2 showing **R tracking the dial's ×2 scaling** (R significantly above 1, approaching 2).

→ Honest negative; the Skeptic's Star applies. A failure of one arm does not require the others to fail, and each should be reported separately.

**THE MOST INTERESTING OUTCOME — report it as such, not as a pass:**
- **Arm A and Arm B pass while Arm C2 fails.** A readout that resolves a real difference *on a ruler that moves with the document* is a strictly harder thing to explain than either a clean pass or a clean fail. If this happens, stop, write it up in full, and do not attempt to interpret it in the same document. The right next step is a second household and a third dial.

**INCONCLUSIVE:**
- fewer than 20 Arm A trials; fewer than 6 sessions; the reader's own method and calibration ritual not recorded; the two dials accidentally printed with the same graduations; the key opened before scoring; or the reader was told about the dial swap in advance.

## Feedback loop

- Log **every** attempt, including abandoned sessions and days the reader "couldn't get a reading" — a reader who cannot read on some days is a datum about the instrument, not a failed experiment.
- Log the reader's own interpretation of any surprising result. The tradition's explanation of a negative is part of the record.
- If a PASS appears, **the second household is the result**, not the first.
- If a FAIL appears, publish it with the same care. A retired claim costs nobody anything and saves the next family the same month.
