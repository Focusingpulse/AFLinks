## The claim (as asserted by the source)

The paper's argument runs: modern monocropping with herbicides, pesticides and mineral fertilizers destroys soil fertility in the humid tropics; the alternative is to **imitate and accelerate natural species succession.** Plants in nature live in consortiums that succeed one another; each consortium creates the physical environment the next requires. Götsch claims to accelerate this by (a) planting species in their natural consortiums at high density, (b) **selective weeding** — removing only maturing grasses, herbs and vines or those substituted by cultivated species, leaving the rest, (c) **pruning / chop-and-drop** — cutting back mature plants entirely or thinning crowns by ≥50% of branchlets and laying the material as mulch, and (d) introducing each species **in the order and at the time it would appear in nature.**

Four claims are separable, and only two are what this card tests:

1. **The core practice (NOT the card's test, and mainstream-supported).** Polyculture, mulch, no-till, no synthetic inputs — the general regenerative-agriculture package. This is widely practiced and independently documented; **the card does not pretend it is contested.**
2. **The order/timing claim (TESTED HERE).** The paper's own distinctive assertion, stated in its conclusion of the planting-order section: *"the critical factor for the establishment and development of a plant is not as much the factor light as the order and timing of its introduction in the natural succession."* Its worked example: manioc (a 1–2 year herbaceous pioneer), corindiba (*Trema micrantha*, a 4–6 year pioneer tree), inga (*Inga* sp., a 20–80 year leguminous tree), and caimito (*Chrysophyllum cainito*, a ~200-year primary-forest fruit tree) — grown together, they **grew vigorously if planted in that order, each when the preceding one was established and in the beginning of its vigorous-growth phase**; planted **simultaneously** they "did fairly well, but the inga and caimito had difficulties establishing themselves." And the reciprocal test: *"a caimito could successfully be introduced beneath fully established manioc, corindiba or inga trees but the reciprocal combinations were not successful"* — corindiba "failed even to germinate" under a fully developed inga or caimito.
3. **The pruning claim (TESTED HERE, as a second arm).** That cutting back a maturing dominant plant rejuvenates the whole community: *"once mature pioneer trees and shrubs were cut back entirely ... the entire plant community was invigorated and burst into new, rapid growth."*
4. **The strong claims (NOT tested here, and named as untested).** That completely degraded soils become "highly productive and biodiverse agroforests after only five years" with no fertilizers; that a maturing dominant consortium **induces senescence in its neighbours** (the paper's own mechanism for why order matters — and the author explicitly flags it: *"the reason for this the subject of further research"*); that the method is "not labor intensive" (one worker per 4–5 ha versus one per 3–4 ha for conventional cocoa). **These are whole-farm, multi-year, and in one case self-declared as unresolved.** A one-season home plot cannot test them, and this card does not pretend to.

**Claimants:** Ernst Götsch (Fazenda Três Colinas, Bahia); the broader syntropic-agriculture school (Agenda Götsch, the ABA-Agroecologia network)
**Verification status:** the core practice (1) is well-established and independently documented; the order/timing claim (2) is asserted by the source from repeated on-farm observation and, as far as the archive shows, **never tested against a simultaneous-planting control**; the pruning claim (3) is asserted and well-attested in the wider practice literature but not isolated from the general polyculture effect; the strong claims (4) are unreplicated and site-specific
**Mainstream position:** polyculture / mulch / no-till are mainstream-supported; the specific "order and timing of introduction determines establishment more than light" claim is a distinctive assertion of this school, plausible to plant ecologists (facilitation and nurse-crop effects are real and documented) but not established as a general rule

## The protocol — three plots, one season, two questions

The testable core: **if the order and timing of introduction matter as the source says, a plot planted in succession order should establish its later-succession species measurably better than an identical plot planted all at once — and a pruned community should regrow measurably more than an unpruned one.**

### Materials (~$0–50)

1. **Seed or starts for three species in three succession roles** (~$0–40). Choose locally-adapted species in your own climate and pick one of each role:
   - a **fast pioneer** (a quick legume or leafy annual — e.g. field pea, cowpea, buckwheat, or a fast clover),
   - a **main crop** (the food you actually want — e.g. a leafy green, a brassica, a tomato, or a root crop),
   - a **later-succession woody or long-lived species** (a small legume shrub or tree — e.g. elder, autumn olive, goumi, Siberian pea-shrub, or a fruit sapling).
   **The later-succession species is the one the source's claim hinges on** — pick one that will visibly struggle or thrive within one season.
2. **A tape measure and stakes/string** — to lay out three equal plots.
3. **A kitchen scale** (~$10 if not owned) — to weigh harvested biomass.
4. **A soil-moisture meter or auger** (optional, ~$10–20) — to check the plots start comparable.
5. **A notebook and a marker** — the pre-registration sheet.

### Design — the parts that make it a measurement

1. **Pick three adjacent, equal plots** on the same ground, same soil, same light, same aspect. Mark them **A (succession order)**, **B (simultaneous)**, **C (control — crop alone)**. Each plot gets the **same three species at the same total density**; only the *timing* differs. Record plot dimensions and a baseline soil-moisture reading. **If the plots differ by more than ~30% in baseline moisture or obvious soil quality, move them or stop** — a baseline mismatch makes the comparison uninterpretable.
2. **Plot C — control:** plant the **main crop alone**, at the density it would have in Plots A and B (i.e. its share of the mix). This is the monoculture baseline.
3. **Plot B — simultaneous:** plant **all three species at the same time**, same density as A.
4. **Plot A — succession order:** plant the **pioneer first.** When it is established and visibly in vigorous growth (roughly 3–5 weeks, or when it has formed a canopy), plant the **main crop beneath/beside it.** When the main crop is established and vigorous, plant the **later-succession species.** This staging is the whole experiment — the intervals are the treatment.
5. **Manage all three plots identically otherwise:** same watering, same mulch handling, **selective weeding only** (remove only maturing grasses/vines, leave the rest) in all three — so that weeding is not the variable. **Do not prune in the first phase.**
6. **Measure at two points:** ~8 weeks and ~16 weeks after the first planting. Per plot, per species: **survival %** (plants alive / planted), **height** (mean of the survivors), and at the end **above-ground biomass** (cut, weigh fresh). Also record **soil-surface cover %** (a quadrat estimate) and the main crop's **yield** (weight of harvested portion).
7. **The pruning arm (second half of the season).** Once the community is established, **split Plot A into two equal halves: A-pruned and A-unpruned.** In A-pruned, cut back the pioneer and any maturing plants (chop-and-drop, material laid as mulch) as the source describes. Leave A-unpruned untouched. **Measure new growth** (length of new shoots, and biomass at the end) in both halves over the following 4 weeks.
8. **Photograph** each plot at each measurement point, from the same angle, with a scale marker.

### Endpoints

- **Primary — establishment of the later-succession species:** survival % and biomass of the woody/long-lived species, **Plot A vs Plot B.** This is the source's own specific prediction.
- **Secondary — total community biomass:** Plot A vs Plot B vs Plot C.
- **Secondary — main-crop yield:** Plot A vs Plot B vs Plot C.
- **Secondary — pruning:** new growth in A-pruned vs A-unpruned over 4 weeks.
- **Secondary — cover:** soil-surface cover % per plot.

### Pre-registered pass/fail

- **PASS (order matters):** the later-succession species' survival in Plot A is **≥ 2× that in Plot B**, **AND** Plot A's total community biomass is **≥ 1.3× Plot B's**, **AND** Plot A's main-crop yield is **≥ Plot B's** (order does not cost yield) — **and the direction repeats in a second planting round or a second season.**
- **PASS (pruning rejuvenates):** over 4 weeks, A-pruned new-shoot growth is **≥ 1.5× A-unpruned**, with no increase in mortality.
- **FAIL:** Plot A ≈ Plot B on every endpoint (the order claim refuted at home scale), or Plot A worse than B (order costs establishment or yield). An honest negative, Skeptic's Star.
- **INCONCLUSIVE:** the three plots differed by >30% at baseline; a frost, drought, flood or pest event hit one plot and not the others; fewer than 5 plants of any species survived in any plot (too few to measure); or the staging intervals collapsed (e.g. the pioneer died before the crop went in).

## Why it matters

This is the **food-domain card** in the queue, and it takes an axis no food card in it has carried. Cards 010 (egg fermentation), 015 (moonlight germination), 020 (Korschelt aether-ray ripening), 026 (electro-culture growth stimulation) and 032 (element-day sowing) are all about a **stimulus applied to a plant or a food** — a vessel, a light, a field, a current, a calendar day. **This one is about the plant *community* — the order and timing in which species are introduced to each other.** The food domain had never carried a growing-system card.

It is also the queue's **second card whose core practice is mainstream-supported rather than contested** (after the contour swale). That is stated as a finding, not hidden: polyculture and mulch are ordinary regenerative agriculture. **What is genuinely contestable — and what the card actually tests — is the author's own distinctive assertion that order and timing determine establishment more than light does.** Presenting the whole method as doubtful would be dishonest; presenting the order claim as settled would be equally dishonest. The card separates them.

## Replicability: `home`

- Build cost: **~$0–50** (seed/starts, a scale if not owned; a moisture meter optional).
- Safety: **none beyond ordinary care** — hand tools, no mains work, no chemicals. **One caution:** verify that any shrub or tree you plant is non-invasive in your region and safe to handle; do not plant a species your local extension lists as noxious.
- Accessibility: one growing season. Three small plots; the staging intervals are the only skill.

## Apparatus (Bill of Materials)

- Seed/starts for three species (pioneer / main crop / later-succession woody).
- Tape measure, stakes, string.
- Kitchen scale; notebook; marker; camera.
- Optional: soil-moisture meter or auger.
- Pruning shears / secateurs for the pruning arm.

## What a result means (and what it does not)

- A **pass** means: on your ground, this season, staging the introductions produced better establishment of the later-succession species and more total biomass than planting everything at once — and pruning rejuvenated the community. Those are real, local, measured results.
- It does **not** mean your soil will be recovered in five years, that no fertilizer will ever be needed, that the "consortium induces senescence" mechanism is real (the author himself calls that unresolved), or that the method is labour-free. Those are the source's strong claims and they are **out of scope at plot scale** — say so in the report.
- A **fail** means order and timing made no measurable difference on your ground, and the mix alone did the work. That is a complete result and earns the Skeptic's Star.
- The confounds to name: **the source's system is humid-tropical** (Bahia: ~1400 mm annual rain, pH 4.2–5.0 oxisols/ultisols); a temperate garden is a different system and the order effect may be a tropical-succession phenomenon. Also: **one season is not a rotation**, **nurse-crop facilitation is a known effect that could explain a pass without the source's mechanism being right**, and **the staging intervals are chosen by the gardener**, so a pass partly measures the gardener's judgement.
