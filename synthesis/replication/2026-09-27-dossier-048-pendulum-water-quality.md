## The claim (as asserted by the source)

That a person using radiesthesia — a pendulum or dowsing rods — can determine the **quality of a water sample** without an instrument, and can do so **reproducibly**, including **detecting pharmaceutical and toxic load** in drinking water after purification steps. The claim is published in the Dutch water sector's own professional press, in an article about utilities using dowsing operationally.

**What is being tested, in one sentence:** can a person using a pendulum (or rods), **blind**, tell a plain water sample from one with a known substance dissolved in it — and rank samples by how much is dissolved — better than chance?

## Honest status of the claim

**The archive holds the claim and no test of it.** The scout recorded the article as `flag — fields: conceptual, testable` and named it a T2 candidate precisely because the claim has an **objective comparator available**: standard water assays. A grep across `living-library/synthesis/quest-queue/` for water-quality-by-dowsing returns nothing — every dowsing card in the queue tests a **spatial location** (005 geopathic mapping, 029 psi-track, 035 blind water-line, 044 plan-reading), and none tests a **property of the water itself**.

Four things the card must say out loud, because they are what a family is actually testing:

1. **The source is a trade journal, not a study.** H2O Water Netwerk is the Dutch water sector's professional magazine. The word *"reproducibly"* in the article is **the claim**, not a finding — no protocol, no n, no blinding, and no statistics are reported. The card exists because that word has never been tested against ground truth.
2. **A home test can only reach the coarse version of the claim.** The source's strongest form is *trace* detection — pharmaceuticals and toxins at microgram or nanogram per litre after purification. A kitchen cannot make or verify that. This card tests the **coarse** version: substances present at grams per litre, where the family *knows* the answer because they put them there. **A FAIL retires only the coarse claim; it does not refute trace detection. A PASS on the coarse version does not validate trace detection either.** Both directions of that sentence matter and both go in the card.
3. **The comparator is what makes this card worth running.** Unlike every dowsing card the queue already holds, the ground truth here is not a hidden object — it is a **known concentration**, and a cheap reference instrument (a TDS meter, chlorine and pH strips) can independently confirm that the samples really differ. If the reference instrument cannot separate them, the run is VOID: a discrimination test on samples that do not differ measures nothing.
4. **The claim's own professional context is the reason it is not trivial.** Dowsing for water *location* is folk practice; dowsing published as an **operational water-quality method in a utility sector's trade press** is an institutional claim, and institutional claims are the ones worth a blind test.

**Under-promise, stated plainly:** the expected outcome is FAIL — a chance-level score. That is a complete result (Skeptic's Star): it would be the first blind test of a claim the archive holds only as a trade-press assertion, and it would tell a family plainly that for water quality they should reach for the meter, not the pendulum. A PASS would be genuinely surprising and would justify a harder, trace-level follow-up.

## Why it matters

- It is the queue's **first card that scores dowsing against a chemical ground truth** rather than a hidden physical location. The dowsing family so far asks *"where is it?"*; this one asks *"what is in it?"*
- It is the queue's **first card whose reference instrument is a lab-equivalent assay** (TDS/chlorine/pH) — the comparator is a second, independent measurement of the same property, which is the strongest form of ground truth the queue has used.
- It is directly **self-reliance relevant**: the practical question is *"can we know our drinking water is safe without a lab?"* Either answer changes what a homestead should do.
- It teaches the transferable lesson: **a claim that names no protocol and no n is a claim about a word, not a phenomenon.** The card converts *"reproducibly"* into a number.

## Replicability: `home`

- Build/obtain cost: **under $60** — a pendulum (any, ~$0–15) or a pair of dowsing rods, 8 identical opaque glass jars with lids (~$10), a TDS meter (~$15), chlorine and pH test strips (~$15), a small bottle of unscented household bleach, table salt, sugar, vinegar, and one base water source (tap or well).
- Safety: **no hazard beyond ordinary kitchen chemistry.** Bleach is used at a few drops per litre and the jars are labelled and never drunk. Do not taste any sample. Keep the chlorine jar out of reach of small children and dispose of it down the drain at the end.
- Accessibility: one afternoon to prepare, three short sessions on different days.

## Apparatus (Bill of Materials)

- **Pendulum or dowsing rods (the claim under test)** — record which, and its material and length. The claim is about the *operator's* skill, not the tool; the tool is recorded so others can rebuild it.
- **8 identical opaque glass jars with lids** — identical is the point: no visible difference between samples.
- **One base water source** — a single batch of tap or well water, drawn at once, so every jar starts identical.
- **Additions (known, recorded):** table salt (NaCl), white sugar, white vinegar, and unscented household bleach. **Four jars get one addition each; four jars get nothing.** The four plain jars are the control.
- **TDS meter** — the reference instrument; gives the true dissolved-solids order.
- **Chlorine and pH test strips** — confirm the chlorine jar and the plain jars really differ.
- **A third party (the referee)** — prepares and codes the jars, holds the sealed key, and does not dowse.
- **Log sheet** — pre-registered columns: date, session, jar code, operator's call (plain / treated), operator's rank, TDS reading, chlorine reading, pH reading.

## Protocol (blind discrimination, pre-registered)

**Phase 1 — establish the operator's own noise floor (session 0).**
1. Fill one jar with base water. Have the referee hand it to the operator **ten times in a row**, blind, and record the operator's rating each time on the pre-registered scale. The spread of those ten readings is the **within-sample scatter**. If the operator cannot give a stable reading on the *same* water, the between-sample test cannot mean anything — record it and treat the run as INCONCLUSIVE rather than pushing on.

**Phase 2 — prepare and code (referee only).**
2. Draw one batch of base water and fill 8 jars. Add salt to one, sugar to one, vinegar to one, and a few drops of bleach to one. Leave four plain. **Record the key, seal it, and hand only the coded jars to the operator.**
3. **Verify the samples really differ** with the reference instrument: TDS reading for every jar, chlorine strip for every jar, pH strip for every jar. Write the true order of dissolved load from the TDS readings. **If the reference instrument cannot separate the treated jars from the plain ones, the run is VOID** — the samples do not differ and there is nothing to discriminate.

**Phase 3 — the blind calls (three sessions on different days).**
4. The operator, blind, gives for each jar: **(a)** a call — *plain* or *treated* — and **(b)** a rank from lowest to highest dissolved content. The operator may also name the substance if they wish; naming is recorded but scored separately and is not required.
5. Repeat with **freshly prepared and freshly coded jars on three separate days**, so each session is an independent 8-trial block.
6. Optionally, a **second person who claims no dowsing skill** runs the same calls as a control operator. (The balanced 4/4 design already fixes chance at 50 %, so this is a robustness arm, not the baseline.)
7. Unseal the key only after all calls for a session are written down.

**Phase 4 — score.**
8. **Primary endpoint:** pooled blind plain/treated hits across the three sessions, against a 50 % chance baseline. **Secondary endpoint:** Spearman rank correlation between the operator's ordering and the TDS-meter ordering, pooled. **Tertiary:** substance-naming accuracy (recorded, not required).

## Pass / fail (pre-registered)

- **PASS:** pooled blind plain/treated hits **≥ 18 of 24** (binomial p ≈ 0.011 one-tailed, chance 0.5) **AND** pooled Spearman rank correlation with the TDS order **≥ 0.6** — the coarse water-quality claim survives a blind test with an objective comparator. A PASS justifies a harder, trace-level follow-up.
- **FAIL:** pooled hits **within chance (9–15 of 24, p > 0.05)** and rank correlation **≈ 0** — no detectable ability to distinguish dissolved content at home scale. **A complete result** (Skeptic's Star), and a finding about the archive's own claim. **This refutes the coarse version only** — it does not refute trace detection.
- **INCONCLUSIVE:** the operator cannot produce a stable reading even on the visible/known positive (distilled vs heavily salted water, key open) — the blind arms are then uninformative, **not** a refutation; fewer than 3 sessions; the operator's within-sample scatter exceeds the between-sample spread; the referee saw the operator's calls before unsealing.
- **VOID:** the reference instrument cannot separate the treated jars from the plain ones (the samples do not differ); the key was opened before the calls were written; the jars were visibly different (fill level, condensation, smell); the operator was told anything about the contents.
- **ARTIFACT:** the operator's calls track a **visible or olfactory cue** (the bleach jar smells; condensation differs) rather than the pendulum — re-run with the jars wiped, filled to the same line, and presented at the same temperature; the operator's calls track **jar order** (a position bias) — randomise jar order per session and re-score; the result disappears when a second, fresh operator runs it.

## Confounds (named in advance)

- **Visible and olfactory cues.** Bleach smells; vinegar smells; salt and sugar raise the fill line if poured carelessly. Wipe the jars, fill to the same mark, and present them at the same temperature. If the operator names the substance, ask *how* they knew — smell is not dowsing.
- **Position bias.** An operator who is uncertain will default to a pattern (alternating, or "the first one is plain"). Randomise jar order every session and score against the key, not the order.
- **The operator's own noise floor.** A pendulum reading that varies more within one sample than between samples cannot discriminate anything. That is why session 0 exists.
- **Sample drift.** Sugar and vinegar are biological; a jar left out changes. Prepare fresh each session and keep the base water identical.
- **The reference instrument is not infallible either.** A TDS meter measures conductivity, not "quality", and does not see sugar well. Record what it can and cannot separate, and say so — the comparator's limits are part of the result.
- **The claim's own word.** *"Reproducibly"* in the source is an assertion. The whole point of the card is that it has never been converted into a number.

## Verdict rules

Pooled blind hits ≥ 18/24 **and** rank correlation ≥ 0.6 → **supports the coarse claim** (and justifies a trace-level follow-up). Pooled hits within chance and rank correlation ≈ 0 → **refutes the coarse claim at home scale**; the trace claim is untouched and is explicitly out of scope. No stable reading even on the known positive → **inconclusive, not a refutation**. Samples that do not differ, or a key opened early → **void, stated as such**.
