## The Claim

The energy-healing traditions — Qi gong, Reiki, therapeutic touch, and their
relatives — hold that a practitioner can **emit a healing influence** that
affects a living body. The strongest form of the claim is not "it feels
soothing" but "something physical leaves the hands and changes matter." Qi
gong practitioners in Japan made exactly that claim about **water**: that
"Qi-imparted water" (気功水) measurably changes its **electrical conductivity**.

**Honest status of the claim:** contested, and this is one of the rare cases
where it was tested *with an instrument* rather than a feeling. The 1993 study
is small, from a fringe-adjacent journal, and — critically — **its own findings
are mixed**:

- 10 senders over 17 days, conductivity measured at **40 ± 1 °C** against controls.
- **Three distinct conductivity curve types** (rising / flat / falling) emerged
  *regardless of an identical Qi-imparting method* — i.e. the senders did not
  produce a consistent effect.
- Effects did **not** correlate with senders' self-reported state of consciousness.
- **Qi did NOT imprint into purified water** — ions/impurities appear to be
  necessary. This is the study's most useful and most testable constraint.
- Initial-exposure effects were larger than later ones.

So the paper does not establish the claim; it establishes a *protocol* and a
*puzzle*. That is exactly what makes it worth a family's bench: the instrument
question is open, and the "ions required" constraint is cheap to test directly.

**Claimant:** Qi gong / energy-healing tradition (as formulated by Sasaki, Sako & Kobayashi 1993)
**Verification status:** unverified; original study internally inconsistent; no independent replication known to the archive
**Mainstream position:** no known physical mechanism; conductivity is a well-understood function of dissolved ions and temperature

## The Protocol — blinded conductivity test

The testable core: **if a person's intent/imposition changes water, a blinded
conductivity measurement at fixed temperature should show it.** If only the
temperature, dissolved CO₂, and handling are changing the readings, it will
show that instead — which is a real, publishable result too.

### Materials (~$30–40)

1. **Conductivity / TDS meter** — handheld EC meter, ~$30–40 (µS/cm readout).
2. **Water, three kinds** — distilled (near-zero ions), tap, and mineral water.
   *Split each single bottle across all cups* so batch identity is controlled.
3. **8 identical glass cups with lids** (lids matter — see confounds).
4. **Thermometer** + a warm-water bath that holds samples at **40 ± 1 °C**.
5. **Labels/codes** + a sealed key (whoever codes the cups does not measure).
6. **Timer** and a log sheet.

### Procedure

1. **Code the cups.** A family member who will *not* measure or send assigns
   each cup a random code (A–H) and seals the key. Nobody else sees it until
   analysis.
2. **Measure the noise floor.** Take 10 repeated EC readings of one sample at
   40 ± 1 °C. The standard deviation is the meter's repeat-measurement noise —
   this is the threshold everything else is judged against.
3. **Baseline.** Measure EC of every cup at 40 ± 1 °C, same operator, same order.
4. **The attempt.** The "sender" attempts to impart Qi to half the cups (the
   sender does not know which half the *measurer* thinks is which — and ideally
   does not know the coding either; a third person can hand them the cups).
   The other half is handled **identically** — same number of handlings, same
   duration of contact, same ambient conditions — but without any attempt.
5. **Re-measure.** Measure EC of every cup again at 40 ± 1 °C, blinded to group.
6. **Repeat 3 sessions** on different days, re-coding cups each session.
7. **Run the ions test.** Repeat the whole thing with distilled water vs tap vs
   mineral. The 1993 paper predicts *no* effect in distilled water — if that
   holds in your kitchen, you have replicated a real (negative) finding.
8. **Analyze.** Compare mean EC change in the attempted group vs the handled-only
   group, against the measured noise floor. Unseal the key *after* the numbers
   are recorded.

### Controls and confounds (document all)

- **Temperature:** conductivity rises ~2 %/°C. The 40 ± 1 °C bath is the entire
  point — measure *in* the bath, not after removing the cup.
- **Dissolved CO₂:** open water absorbs CO₂ from air and its conductivity drifts.
  Keep cups lidded, equal exposure time, same headspace.
- **Handling:** the "sham" cups must get the same handling count as the Qi cups,
  or you are testing warmth and agitation, not intent.
- **Meter drift/calibration:** re-check the noise floor each session.
- **Evaporation:** lids; same session length for all cups.
- **Operator:** one measurer throughout, blinded to group; same order.

## Pass/Fail Criteria (pre-registered)

**PASS (supports the claim):**
- Blinded mean EC change in the attempted group is **≥ 2× the measured meter
  noise floor**, AND
- The direction is **consistent in ≥ 2 of 3 sessions**, AND
- The handled-only control group shows **no** comparable shift

**FAIL (refutes, for this family/bench):**
- No difference between attempted and control groups, OR
- Both groups shift equally (that is handling/temperature, not Qi)

**INCONCLUSIVE:**
- Effect present but below the 2× threshold
- Fewer than 3 sessions completed
- Temperature or CO₂ drift dominated the readings

**Honest note:** the 1993 study's own result was *inconsistent curves across
senders* — so the most likely outcome for a family is FAIL or INCONCLUSIVE, and
that is a genuinely useful outcome: it converts "energy healing" from an
unexamined belief into a measured null on your own bench, with the archive's
own source cited. A clean FAIL earns the Skeptic's Star. A PASS would be a
serious anomaly worth telling the world about — which is why the protocol is
blinded before anyone touches the water.
