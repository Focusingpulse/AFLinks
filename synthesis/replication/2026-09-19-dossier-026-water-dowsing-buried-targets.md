## The claim (as asserted by the sources)

Two claims run together in the tradition, and the dossier keeps them apart because they fail differently.

**1. Groundwater.** A *sourcier* holding a forked stick, an L-rod, or a pendulum can indicate where to dig a well — often with a stated **depth and yield** — better than an uninformed choice would be. The INRS abstract describes the practice fairly: widely used by private individuals "and, in some cases, by municipalities," resting on "intuition and experiential knowledge rather than controlled scientific experimentation."

**2. Discrete buried targets.** Dowsers also claim to detect **pipes and drains** — objects that are discrete, well-defined and therefore *scorable*. This is the claim the controlled literature actually tests, and the one this dossier makes home-replicable. The INRS team says why in its own words: in water-rich regions "groundwater is often ubiquitous. This raises questions about what constitutes a genuine 'detection.'"

**The mechanism row.** Yves Rocard — the French physicist — spent his late career arguing dowsers detect **very weak magnetic field gradients** with the body (the 1963 *Science et Vie* controversy; "Le signal du sourcier," *La Recherche* 124, 1981; *La science et les sourciers*, Bordas, 1989). The standard opposing account is **ideomotor action** — Carpenter 1852; Chevreul before him — in which expectation produces unconscious muscle movement that swings the rod. Neither account predicts the rod will point at water. They predict different things about **metal vs plastic** targets, which is why the apparatus below buries both.

**Honest status of the claim.** This is one of the most thoroughly tested claims in the parapsychology literature, and the record is overwhelmingly null:
- **Costérisant 1937** — ~200 water-supply projects in Puy-de-Dôme and Haute-Loire; "the number of observed successes is effectively zero." His prospective test — dowsers following the underground course of a *visible* spring, checked against where the workmen's picks actually found water — came back negative at Cizière, Lorlanges and Azérat.
- **Jarricot 1959** — medical radiesthesia refuted wholesale by a Lyon laboratory head using statistical analysis of practitioners' own records (the corpus scout reports 129 trials at 55.2% mean error; flagged as summary-sourced, not from the primary page).
- **Munich 1986–88** — 500 applicants screened to 43, 843 trials, water pipe on a randomly moved cart under a false floor; null. The "six dowsers above chance" residue was reproduced from a random-number generator by **Napier's 1999 PHACT simulation** — the expected selection artifact. (See `synthesis/validations/2026-09-19-dowsing-munich-1986-null-campaign.md`.)
- **Kassel 1991 / Enright** — skilled dowsers at chance under blinding.
- **INRS 2025–26** — the first design built to detect a *sub-effect* rather than re-confirm a global null; field season complete, results presented at EGU26 Vienna (May 2026), **numbers not yet published**. (See `synthesis/validations/2026-09-19-dowsing-inrs-2026-controlled-field-study.md`.)
- **Pedro & Schang 1969** is the tradition's positive pole: a respiratory-phase detection mechanism presented to French academies, with a stated classification system and a conducting medium — and **no controlled test anywhere in the note**.

**What makes it a real test rather than a rigged one.** The claim is concrete, mechanical, and falsifiable, and **the chance baseline is arithmetic**: on an N-cell grid with k occupied cells, guessing gives k/N. A family that scores at chance has produced a clean, honest negative on a 90-year-old practice — and knows something true about their own rod. A family that scores *above* chance repeatedly, with the scoring blind, has found something that four decades of controlled trials did not. Both outcomes are informative; a positive would be a genuine surprise and would need to survive scrutiny before it were reported as anything more than one household's result.

## Why it matters

The Yard has a strong dowsing-adjacent card already — **Dossier 005, Blind Geopathic Zone Mapping** — but that tests *inter-rater agreement* (do two people find the same lines?), which is a consistency question. This dossier tests the **hit-rate question**: does the rod point at a hidden thing? That is the claim the public actually cares about, the claim Costérisant, Munich and INRS all tested, and the one that has never been put in the hands of an ordinary household with a scoring sheet.

It also matters because water divination is *not* a fringe side-show in the archive's own subject matter: it is the origin of the French radiesthesia tradition the corpus holds in depth, it is the reason Rocard — a physicist of the French nuclear programme — took the question seriously, and it is where a modern hydrogeology institute chose to spend a field season. If the Aether Force archive is going to carry this tradition, it should carry the test alongside it.

And it is methodically excellent for a family: randomisation, blinding, a pre-computed chance baseline, whole-run scoring instead of best-performer cherry-picking (the Munich lesson), and the difference between *agreement* and *accuracy*.

## Replicability: `home`

- Build cost: **~$25–40** (12–16 identical opaque containers, stakes/string for the grid, a sealed envelope or phone photo for the key, a notebook). Optional second arm: a length of iron and a length of plastic pipe, ~$10.
- Safety: **trivial — digging and water.** No mains power, no chemicals, no heat. Sensible rule: **call before you dig** for any utility locate if the plot is anywhere near buried services, and do not dig deeper than garden depth in an area you do not control.
- Accessibility: two afternoons. Afternoon 1 — an adult buries the containers and seals the key; the dowser must not be present. Afternoon 2 — blind walk and scoring. A child can do the burying and hold the key, which is a genuine job with real information value. Repeatable on subsequent days with the objects left in place, which is the cheapest way to test reproducibility.

## Apparatus (Bill of Materials)

- **12–16 identical opaque containers** — 2 L opaque bottles, sealed PVC canisters, or lidded tins of the same size and shape. Identical matters: a visible size difference between the "wet" and "dry" containers would fake a metal/water effect through the eyes.
- **Sand or soil to fill them**, plus **water**. Half the containers are filled with water and sealed; half are filled with dry sand or left empty — matched for **weight** if the dowser will carry them (they should not) and matched for **shape and size** always.
- **The grid.** A garden bed, a lawn or a field marked into a grid with stakes and string — **4 × 4 cells (16 cells)** is the practical home size, matching the spirit of the INRS 25-cell Battleship layout. Measure and record each cell's dimensions.
- **A sealed key.** An adult writes down which container went into which cell, puts it in a sealed envelope (or photographs the burial with a timestamp), and hands it to a third party. **The dowser must not see the burial and must not see the key until every trial is scored.**
- **A scoring sheet.** One row per trial: date, dowser, rod type, cells indicated, cells occupied (filled from the key *only* after the walk).
- **Optional iron/plastic arm:** one short length of iron pipe and one of plastic pipe, buried at two known cells — for the Rocard magnetic row. This is the row the INRS design exists to decide.
- **Optional second rod type** (wood vs metal) — the instrument row. Cheap, and it tests whether the rod is a passive pointer.
- **A note on "near an apparent spring."** Site the grid **away from visible water, drains, downspouts, soakaways and standpipes**, or the plot is uninterpretable in exactly the way Costérisant described in 1937. Record what is around the plot.

## Protocol (buried grid, blinded, pre-registered cell count)

1. **Fix the number of trials in advance.** Write down how many walks each person will do *before* anyone walks. **The whole run is scored; the best performer in it is not a result.** This is the Munich lesson made operational — six above-average dowsers out of 43 is what pure chance produces.
2. **Choose the random key and seal it.** An adult (not the dowser, not the scorer) places the containers in randomly chosen cells, using a coin, dice, or a shuffled list — **not** the dowser's judgment and not a pattern. Record it. Seal it. Photograph the burial if you like, but do not show the dowser.
3. **Mark the grid clearly** so cell identity is unambiguous, and let the dowser see the *grid* (they need the cell labels) while never seeing the *contents*.
4. **One walk per trial, same route, both directions if you like.** Rod(s) held at waist height, forearms relaxed; the dowser does not push the rods. The dowser states **one cell per trial** — the single cell they believe holds an object — and the scorer writes it down before anything else happens. Allowing multiple guesses per trial changes the arithmetic and must be avoided, or stated up front with the adjusted chance baseline.
5. **Repeat with the same person on different days** (reproducibility), and **with a second person** if available (the novice-vs-experienced row). Each walk is one trial. **Trial counts for each person must be equal**, or the least-tested person will dominate the pool's impression.
6. **Score blind.** The scorer reads the trial records *first*, then opens the key. If the scorer knows the layout while watching the walk, the trial is void — note it and exclude it, do not delete it.
7. **Compute the chance baseline** = (number of occupied cells ÷ 16) × 100 as a per-trial hit probability, and compare the observed hit rate against it. For 16 cells with 4 occupied, chance is 25%; over 40 trials the standard error on the hit rate is about ±6.8 percentage points, so a run needs roughly **±14 points** of deviation (2 SE) to be notable — which is why the trial count is fixed first.
8. **Log every trial including the botched ones.** A walk done while distracted, a trial where the dowser glimpsed the key, an abandoned session — mark them excluded with the reason, do not delete them.
9. **Optional instrument arm:** repeat the whole run with a metal rod and with a wooden rod, same person, same days if possible. If the rod is a passive pointer, instrument type should not matter — any instrument effect is itself a datum.
10. **Optional metal/plastic arm:** bury one iron and one plastic pipe at known cells and add them to the key. If hit rates separate by material, that is the Rocard row, and it is the most interesting possible outcome in this protocol.

## Pass / fail criteria (pre-registered)

**Primary — does the rod point at the hidden thing?**
- **PASS (surprising, needs repetition before it is believed):** hit rate **above chance by more than 2 standard errors**, sustained across a fixed run, with blind scoring. One household's positive is a *lead*, not a finding — say so in the results log, and invite a second household to run the same protocol on the same days.
- **FAIL (the expected and still valuable outcome):** hit rate **within the chance band**, with the scatter of a coin flip. This is a **Skeptic's Star**: a clean negative produced at home, on a claim that has been generating well-siting decisions for centuries. It is worth publishing as loudly as a positive.
- **INCONCLUSIVE:** fewer trials than pre-registered; the key was visible to the dowser; the scorer watched the walk knowing the layout; **the plot sits near visible water or a drain** (the Costérisant confound — the "detection" cannot be separated from the setting); or multiple guesses per trial were allowed without an adjusted baseline.

**Secondary — the sub-rows, if run:**
- **Iron vs plastic separation** — the Rocard magnetic row. Above chance on iron only, sustained, is the single most interesting result this protocol can produce. No separation is a clean answer to a forty-year-old hypothesis.
- **Experienced vs novice** — if experience adds nothing above a trained novice, the "gift" is either learnable procedure or nothing at all. Use **equal trial counts** for each person.
- **Wood vs metal rods** — no instrument effect is what an ideomotor reading predicts. Any effect is a datum.

Report the rows **separately**. A null on the primary with a signal on the iron row is a completely different result from a flat null everywhere, and collapsing them loses the information.

## What would change the verdict

- **Equal-trial-count multi-household runs on the same days.** If two families score above chance independently on the same protocol, no local artefact explains it and the result becomes worth escalating. If neither does, the household null is stronger. This is the "Uber for labs" shape the Yard is building toward.
- **A rod instrumented instead of observed.** A rod with a strain gauge or a phone gyroscope logging its motion would replace "the dowser says the rods crossed here" with a record, and would let a future dossier ask what the rod is actually responding to.
- **The INRS article.** When the EGU26-3985 numbers land (late 2026), the iron-vs-plastic row decides whether a home version of the same test has anything to find. This dossier should be revised with that result: if iron separates there, the iron arm here becomes the priority row; if nothing separates, the honest record says so with the same weight.
- **A positive at home, repeated.** Two independent households above chance would be the first such result in this corpus's dowsing rail in ninety years — and would still be a *lead*, requiring a third, pre-registered run, not a conclusion.
