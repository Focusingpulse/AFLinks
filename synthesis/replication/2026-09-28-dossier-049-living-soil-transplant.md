## The claim (as asserted by the sources)

Two archive claims sit in tension, and the card tests the seam between them.

1. **The transplant claim (CEDRIC):** a living community of soil microbes and root exudates, taken from healthy plants and moved into impoverished soil, **restores** that soil — structure, biodiversity, nutrient density, organic carbon. The implication for a household is the tempting one: *you can buy back a dead bed with a bucket of good soil.*
2. **The management claim (Nagahama):** disease suppression in a real field came from **fifty years** of organic management — the scout's own words are *"ecological niche formation through long-term management."* The implication is the opposite: the benefit is **built**, not transferred, and a one-time transplant may not carry it.

**What is being tested, in one sentence:** does inoculating a poor bed with a living-soil community from a healthy site make it measurably more productive (and less diseased) than the same bed untreated — and if it does, is the effect **biology** (living organisms) or merely **nutrients** (the soil you added)?

## Honest status of the claim

**The archive holds the transplant claim and no home test of it.** A grep across `living-library/synthesis/quest-queue/` for `soil microbiome`, `CEDRIC`, `disease-suppressive`, `bioinocul` or `soil transplant` returns **nothing** — the queue has no soil-biology card at all. Its food cards test germination (015), fermentation (010), ripening (020), sowing *timing* (032), growth stimulation (026), storage (033, 043) and planting *order* (038). **None tests the soil community itself** — which is the one axis both of the freshest soil finds in the archive point at, and which is Gardening's own seed candidate ("Water / soil vitality").

Four things the card must say out loud, because they are what a family is actually testing:

1. **The two sources disagree about the mechanism, and that is the interesting part.** CEDRIC says the community can be *moved*; Nagahama says suppression is *built over decades*. A home transplant test is, in effect, a small vote on which is more true at household scale. The card is designed so that either answer is a result.
2. **"Biology" and "nutrients" are confounded by default, and the card must separate them.** Any bucket of healthy soil you tip into a bed brings **food** as well as **organisms**. An untreated control alone cannot tell you which did the work. So the card carries a **third arm — a killed inoculum** (the same soil, sterilised by boiling or baking) — and the comparison that matters is *live vs killed*, not *inoculated vs nothing*. **If live ≈ killed ≈ both above the untreated control, the effect is nutrients, not biology, and the transplant claim is refuted in its strong form.**
3. **A home test reaches only the coarse version.** CEDRIC's evidence is soil structure, microbial biodiversity and organic carbon measured in a research programme; a kitchen can measure **yield, survival and visible disease**, and at best a crude respiration proxy. **A FAIL retires only the home-scale, coarse claim** — that a single transplant visibly lifts a bed in one season. It does not refute the multi-year restoration claim, which is explicitly out of scope.
4. **The likely result is a null, and the null is the finding.** If the two sources are right that suppression is built over decades, the honest expectation is that one season of transplant does **little**. That is a complete result (Skeptic's Star): it would tell a family plainly that soil health is a **practice, not a purchase** — and it would be the queue's first card to test a *mainstream* soil claim and return a home-scale verdict.

**Under-promise, stated plainly:** the expected outcome is that the live and killed arms differ little and both sit close to the untreated control in a single season. A clear live-vs-killed split would be genuinely surprising and would justify a multi-season follow-up.

## Why it matters

- It is the queue's **first soil-biology card** — every food card so far tests a plant, a seed, a container or a schedule; none tests the **soil community**.
- It is the queue's **first card with a killed-inoculum arm**, the design that separates a *biological* effect from a *nutritional* one. That control is the difference between "soil life matters" as a slogan and as a measurement.
- It is directly **self-reliance relevant**: the practical question is *"can we restore a dead bed cheaply, from a bucket of good soil — or do we have to build it over seasons?"* Either answer changes what a homestead does with its worst ground.
- It teaches the transferable lesson: **when a treatment carries two possible causes, the control that separates them is the whole experiment.** A card that only compares "treated vs untreated" cannot answer the question it asks.

## Replicability: `home`

- Build/obtain cost: **under $50** — three matched containers or three small beds (~$0–30), one bag of poor/sterile growing medium (~$10), seed for one disease-susceptible crop (~$5), a kitchen scale (owned), and a source of healthy soil (free — a forest floor, an old meadow, or a long-untended garden corner).
- Safety: **no hazard.** Boiling/baking soil to make the killed arm is ordinary kitchen work; do it in a throwaway pan, ventilate, and do not reuse the pan for food. Wash hands after handling soil.
- Accessibility: one afternoon to build, one growing season of weekly observation.

## Apparatus (Bill of Materials)

- **Three matched containers or three equal adjacent beds** — same size, same medium, same light and water. Three arms: **A (live inoculum)**, **B (killed inoculum)**, **C (untreated control)**.
- **One poor/sterile medium** — the same batch split three ways, so every arm starts identical. A sterilised potting mix or a leached, lifeless garden soil is the point: a bed that is *already* healthy cannot show a transplant effect.
- **The inoculum source** — a single sample of healthy soil from one site (forest floor, old meadow, or a long-untended garden corner). Record the site, the date, the depth and the appearance. **Take one batch and split it**: half stays live (arm A), half is sterilised (arm B).
- **Sterilisation** — boil the arm-B portion in water for ~10 minutes, or bake it at ~180 °C until steaming, then cool. This kills organisms while leaving most of the mineral and much of the organic content, so arm B is the **nutrient-matched, biology-free** comparator.
- **One disease-susceptible crop** — the same seed, sown the same way, in all three arms (a brassica, a tomato, or a bean are reasonable choices; record the variety).
- **A kitchen scale** — for harvestable yield.
- **A log sheet** — pre-registered columns: date, arm, plant count, survival, visible disease (which symptom, how many plants), height, and final fresh weight per plant.

## Protocol (three-arm transplant, pre-registered)

**Phase 1 — build and pre-register.**
1. Fill the three containers/beds from **one batch** of poor medium. Photograph them side by side before anything is added.
2. Take **one** sample of healthy soil and **split it in two**. Sterilise one half (boil or bake) and let it cool. Keep the other half live and moist.
3. **Inoculate:** mix the **live** half into arm A and the **killed** half into arm B, in the same quantity and the same way. Add nothing to arm C. Water all three identically from here on.
4. Write the **pre-registration sheet** before sowing: the crop, the number of seeds per arm, the sowing date, the watering rule, the endpoints, the thresholds, and the harvest date. Photograph it.

**Phase 2 — grow and observe (one season).**
5. Sow the same number of seeds in each arm on the same day. Record germination count per arm at ~7 and ~14 days.
6. Weekly, record for every arm: number of surviving plants, any **visible disease** (name the symptom), and mean height. Photograph each arm from the same angle each week.
7. Keep water, light and temperature as equal as you can; note any day one arm got something the others did not.

**Phase 3 — harvest and score.**
8. At the pre-registered harvest date, cut each plant at the soil line and weigh the **harvestable** part per plant. Record per-arm total and per-plant mean.
9. **Optional soil-biology proxy:** seal a measured scoop of medium from each arm in a jar with a small CO₂ indicator (or a lime-water trap) for 48 hours and record the change — a crude respiration read on whether arm A's soil is *alive* in a way arm B's is not.
10. Score the pre-registered endpoints: **A vs B** (biology), **A and B vs C** (any effect at all), and per-plant yield means.

## Pass / fail (pre-registered)

- **PASS (biology transfers):** arm A harvestable yield **≥ 1.3× arm B** AND arm A visible-disease incidence **≤ half of arm B's**, with arm B not clearly above arm C — the living community did work the killed soil did not. Repeats in a second round.
- **PASS (effect is nutritional, not biological — a real, different result):** arms A and B are **both ≥ 1.3× arm C** and **A ≈ B** — the transplant helps, but by feeding the bed, not by seeding it with life. Recorded as a positive finding about *what* was transferred.
- **FAIL:** A ≈ B ≈ C on every endpoint — a one-time transplant does not visibly lift a poor bed in one season. **A complete result** (Skeptic's Star): soil health is built over seasons, not bought in a bucket, and the Nagahama "long-term management" reading is the one that survives at home scale. **This refutes the coarse home-scale transplant claim only**; the multi-year restoration claim is out of scope.
- **INCONCLUSIVE:** the three arms differed by >30% at baseline (before inoculation); a pest, frost, drought or flood hit one arm and not the others; fewer than 5 plants survived in any arm; the inoculum source was itself degraded or waterlogged; the killed arm was not actually sterilised (visible mould or regrowth).
- **ARTIFACT:** the result tracks a **fertility** difference rather than biology (A ≈ B, both > C — see the nutritional PASS above); the arms differed in light, water or temperature; the inoculum was visibly different in colour or smell and the operator knew which arm was which; the result disappears when the arms are re-randomised in position.

## Evidence

Photo of the three arms side by side before inoculation + the **pre-registration sheet** (crop, seed counts, sowing and harvest dates, watering rule, endpoints, thresholds) + a photo of the inoculum source site + the live and killed inoculum photographed in equal portions + dated same-angle photos of all three arms at germination and weekly thereafter + the germination counts per arm + the weekly survival, disease and height table per arm + the final per-plant fresh weights and per-arm totals + the results table with the A-vs-B, A-vs-C and B-vs-C ratios shown + the optional respiration-jar readings + the void/artifact checks reported whether or not they void the run.

## Confounds (named in advance)

- **Nutrients vs biology — the central confound.** Any healthy soil brings food as well as organisms. The killed arm is the only thing that separates them; without it the card cannot answer its own question.
- **One season is short.** Soil communities take time to establish. A null in one season is expected and is not evidence that the transplant never works — say so in the card.
- **The inoculum is a sample of one.** A single source site may be unusually rich or poor. Record the site; a second source is a robustness arm, not the baseline.
- **Position and microclimate.** Beds differ in light and drainage by position. Rotate or randomise arm positions between rounds if the design allows.
- **The crop's own disease pressure.** Without a deliberate pathogen challenge, "disease incidence" is whatever the season brings; a disease-free season makes the disease endpoint uninformative, not a pass.
- **The operator knowing the arms.** Inoculated soil may look or smell different. Where possible, have a second person label the arms and keep the key, so the weekly observations are blind.

## Verdict rules

A ≥ 1.3× B with disease halved and B ≈ C → **biology transfers** (supports the transplant claim at home scale). A ≈ B, both ≥ 1.3× C → **the effect is nutritional, not biological** (a positive result about mechanism). A ≈ B ≈ C → **refutes the coarse home-scale transplant claim**; the multi-year restoration claim is untouched and explicitly out of scope. Arms that differed at baseline, or a season that hit one arm only → **inconclusive, not a refutation**.
