## The Claim

Schauberger's agricultural writing contains three separate claims, stated together in one paragraph:

> "…it was shown to be particularly advantageous to mow the grass with **hammer-sharpened hand scythes** rather than being chopped in the normal way. The ancient Estonian farmers used this method of sharpening. Schauberger thought that if the hammering were done **against a hard wooden support instead of, for example, iron**, the mechanical loads generated by the hammering would **form an energy within the scythe that would be released during harvesting, giving the roots and plants energy for growth**. It was important **not to let the hammered scythe remain in the sun, otherwise the charge would dissipate and become 'flat.'**"

Broken into its testable parts:

1. **Tool claim (craft).** A hammer-peened edge ("hammer-sharpened") cuts better than a ground edge ("chopped in the normal way").
2. **Subtle-energy claim (mechanism).** Hammering against a **hardwood** support (not an iron anvil) forms a *charge* stored in the blade.
3. **Sun claim (the weird one, and the sharpest test).** Leaving the hammered blade in the sun **discharges** it — the blade goes "flat."
4. **Plant claim (consequence).** The charge is *released at harvest* and **feeds regrowth** — grass mown with a charged blade should regrow better.

**Honest status of the claim:** unverified, and the source is loose about provenance — "it was shown to be particularly advantageous" names no trial, no date, no instrument. It is an assertion inside a biography, not a report. What makes it worth a family's bench anyway:

- **Claim 3 is cleanly falsifiable.** Same blade type, same steel, same edge preparation, same operator — the *only* difference is one hour of sunlight. If the "charge" is real, the sun blade cuts worse. If nothing is there, the two blades are indistinguishable, and no geometry explanation can rescue the difference because there is no geometric difference.
- **Peening is genuine practice.** Hammer-peening a scythe edge on an anvil (traditionally a "peening jig" or stake anvil) is *the* traditional European method — Schauberger's heterodoxy is the *wooden* support and the charge story, not the hammering. So arm 1 (peened vs ground) is a real craft question with a real mainstream answer (a peened edge is thin and slightly convex; a filed edge is more angular) — which is exactly why arm 1 alone cannot test the fringe claim.
- **Arm 4 is agronomically measurable** — regrowth biomass in matched plots — and it is the claim most likely to be dismissed without a test, because nobody has run it.

**Claimant:** Viktor Schauberger (as reported by Olof Alexandersson, *Living Water*)
**Verification status:** unverified; no independent trial known to the archive; asserted without citation
**Mainstream position:** no known mechanism by which mechanical working stores a charge in steel that affects plant growth; sunlight does not alter a steel edge's cutting geometry. (Steel *does* cold-work — peening hardens and thins the edge — so arm 1 has a conventional explanation. Arms 2–4 do not.)

## The Protocol — blinded blade bench

The testable core: **if a hammered blade carries a charge, an identical blade given an hour of sun should cut worse.** Everything else is secondary. If nothing shows, that is a clean negative on a claim that has been repeated for 90 years without a bench.

### Materials (~$30–90)

1. **Three blades of the same type** — sickles (~$10–20 each) or machetes (~$15–30 each); same maker/model if possible. *(One blade re-edged three ways does not work: re-doing the edge resets the state you are testing.)*
2. **A hammer** — ball-peen or cross-peen.
3. **A hardwood block or stake** — the "hard wooden support" of the claim.
4. **An iron anvil or steel block** — for the optional support comparison.
5. **A fine file or whetstone** — "the normal way."
6. **Grass** — 30+ uniform bundles (same species, same length, cut fresh) **or** a matched strip.
7. **Three matched 1 m² plots** in the same sward, same light/soil, for the regrowth arm.
8. **A timer, a scale**, labels, and a **sealed key**.

### Blade conditions

| Blade | Edge | Support | Storage before test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | hammer-peened | hardwood | dark |
| **B** | hammer-peened | hardwood | **1 h full sun** |
| **C** | filed/whetstoned | — | dark |
| *(D, optional)* | hammer-peened | iron anvil | dark |

### Procedure

1. **Prepare the edges.** Peen A and B on the hardwood support; file C. Photograph all three edges and note the bevel/geometry difference — you will need it for the honest interpretation.
2. **Sun the B blade.** One hour of direct sunlight, laid out flat, then back into the dark. Do not test it hot to the touch — let it reach ambient.
3. **Code the blades.** A family member who will *not* score assigns random labels and seals the key.
4. **Cutting trial (blinded).** 10 uniform grass bundles per blade, in randomized order. The scorer counts **strokes to sever each bundle** (or time to sever). One operator, same technique, same bundle orientation.
5. **Edge retention.** Continue through bundles until the stroke count doubles (or 100 bundles), and record **strokes-to-failure** per blade.
6. **Regrowth arm.** Mow each of the three matched 1 m² plots with one blade (A, B, C). Measure regrowth **height and clipped biomass at 2 and 4 weeks**.
7. **Unseal and analyze.** Compare A vs B (the charge test), A vs C (the craft test), and plot regrowth.

### Controls and confounds (document all)

- **Edge geometry — the big one.** A peened edge and a filed edge differ *shape*, not just "charge." So **A vs C cannot distinguish the fringe claim from ordinary blade geometry.** Say so in the writeup. **A vs B is the discriminating comparison** — identical preparation, identical geometry, sunlight the only variable.
- **Heat.** A sun-warmed blade is not a "flattened" blade. Cool to ambient before testing, and log blade temperature.
- **Rust.** Log humidity and any flash rust on B.
- **Operator technique.** One cutter, one motion, same stance and stroke count discipline; alternate blade order between bundles.
- **Grass variation.** Bundles from one cut, one field, one morning. Weigh them — record bundle mass so you can normalize.
- **Plot variation.** Adjacent plots, same aspect, same soil; photograph all three before mowing.
- **Sun dose.** 1 h is the minimum dose. If the claim is real and 1 h shows nothing, a full-day exposure is the fairer follow-up.

## Pass/Fail Criteria (pre-registered)

**PASS (supports the charge claim):**
- Blade B requires **≥ 15 % more strokes per bundle than blade A** (mean, blinded), AND
- The direction is **consistent in ≥ 2 of 3 sessions**, AND
- Plot A regrows **≥ 15 % more biomass than plot B** at 4 weeks

**FAIL (refutes, for this family/bench):**
- A and B are statistically indistinguishable (within ±5 %), OR
- B cuts *better* than A (the opposite of the prediction) — honest negative, Skeptic's Star

**INCONCLUSIVE:**
- Difference present but below the 15 % threshold
- Blade geometry could not be equalized well enough to trust A vs B
- Fewer than 3 sessions, or grass/bundle variation dominated the readings

**Honest note:** the most likely outcome is FAIL on the charge arms and a **real, explainable difference on A vs C** (peened geometry cuts differently than filed geometry — a genuine craft finding, not a fringe one). That split is the point of the card: it separates the *verifiable craft* from the *unverified charge*, and it gives a family a real skill in the process. A PASS on A vs B would be a serious anomaly worth publishing. A clean FAIL earns the Skeptic's Star.
