## The claim (as asserted by the source)

That a pendulum's behaviour changes **during a solar eclipse** in a way that classical gravity does not predict — either the **azimuth of the plane of oscillation** deviates from its normal Foucault precession (Allais) or the **period** shifts (Saxl & Allen; Jeverdan). Ivanov's framing is broader: that eclipse-day gravimetry is an **open, cheap replication window at every eclipse**, and that the anomaly connects to a kinetic/ether model of gravity.

**What is being tested, in one sentence:** does a pendulum's measured behaviour during a solar eclipse differ from that same pendulum's own baseline on ordinary days, by more than the measurement scatter?

## Honest status of the claim

**Contested, and the weight of modern evidence is null.** This is the honest ledger and it goes in the card:

- The original reports are **large** (Allais's 13.5° azimuth excursion is not a subtle effect) — which is exactly why a home test is worth running: if the effect were real at that size, a careful pendulum should see *something*.
- But **many careful replications found nothing** (Ullakko 1991; Salva 2011), and the 2010 automated-Foucault null is the single most directly comparable modern test.
- The mainstream explanation is **instrumental/environmental**: tilt of the vertical, thermal drift, ground tilt, air-density changes. Duif's review argues those explanations *also* fail — so the question is genuinely open in the literature, not settled.

**Under-promise, stated plainly:** this card cannot settle the Allais effect. It can establish, for one family's pendulum, **whether the eclipse day looks different from the baseline days** — and a null is the expected result and a complete one. The card's real product is the **distributed protocol**: several homes measuring the same event the same way at the same time, which is the only way a small contested effect can ever be resolved.

## Why it matters

- It is the queue's **first card that requires multiple households to measure the same event simultaneously** — every prior card is single-site (or sequential). The Community guild's complement is named *"distributed labs / Uber-for-labs — neighborhood replication nodes"*; this is that node, made concrete.

## Replicability: `home`

- Build cost: **~$50–100** (a heavy bob, a long suspension, a low-friction release, a protractor/azimuth scale, a stopwatch or phone timer).
- Safety: **none.** No mains, no chemicals, no heat. The only hazards are a falling bob (keep the rig clear) and eye safety during the eclipse itself (never look at the sun without certified eclipse glasses — a hard condition carried into the card).
- Accessibility: an afternoon to build + 3–5 baseline sessions; the eclipse arm is scheduled.

## Apparatus (Bill of Materials)

- **Bob** — a dense, symmetric mass (a steel ball, a heavy fishing weight, or a bag of sand), 1–5 kg. Denser is better: it lengthens the period and reduces air-drag sensitivity.
- **Suspension** — a **fine, low-twist line** (braided fishing line or a thin steel wire), 2–3 m long. For the *paraconical* (Allais) geometry, the line meets a **ball-and-socket or knife-edge support** that lets the plane of oscillation rotate freely; a simple fixed pivot gives a Foucault pendulum instead. **Either is acceptable if the family states which one it built** — the two have different claimed signatures (azimuth for Allais, period for Saxl & Allen).
- **Release mechanism** — a burnable thread, a magnetic release, or a mechanical catch that starts the bob from a **fixed, recorded initial plane** without imparting sideways motion.
- **Azimuth reference** — a protractor ring or a marked floor circle around the pendulum, read against a fixed sight line; a phone camera on a tripod pointed at the scale, so the plane's angle can be read from video without an observer standing next to the bob.
- **Timer / period reference** — a stopwatch or a phone; for period work, a photogate or a phone slow-motion video of the bob crossing a mark.
- **Baseline log sheet** — pre-registered columns: date, time (UTC), session length, initial azimuth, azimuth every N minutes, period (mean of ≥20 swings), indoor temperature, and any disturbance.
- **Eclipse glasses (ISO 12312-2)** — required for any family member watching the eclipse. **Never look at the sun without them.** This is a hard condition, not advice.
- **A shared pre-registration sheet** — one per household, with the same protocol, thresholds, and session times, so the pooled result is comparable.

## Protocol (distributed baseline-then-event, pre-registered)

**Phase 1 — build and baseline (immediate, ~3–5 sessions on ordinary days).**
1. Build the pendulum. Record which geometry (paraconical or Foucault), the line length, the bob mass, and the release method.
2. Run **at least 3 baseline sessions** on ordinary days at the **same clock time** as the coming eclipse's local maximum (this matters — you are controlling for time-of-day and thermal drift). Each session: release from the recorded initial plane, log azimuth every 5 minutes for the full session length, and record the period as the mean of ≥20 swings.
3. Compute the **baseline precession rate** (°/h) and the **baseline period** with their scatter. **This is the family's own null.** If the baseline scatter is larger than the effect you are trying to see, the run is **VOID** — report the resolution achieved, not a verdict.

**Phase 2 — the eclipse arm (scheduled; next eclipse visible from your location).**
4. On eclipse day, run the **identical** session at the identical clock time, starting ~1 h before first contact and running ~1 h past last contact. Log azimuth every 5 minutes and the period continuously.
5. **Do not change anything** — same room, same release, same logging interval, same reader if possible. If a change is unavoidable, record it and treat the comparison as confounded (ARTIFACT).
6. **Multi-household pooling:** each participating household runs the same protocol and posts its own baseline and eclipse series with its own scatter. The pooled question is whether the eclipse-day deviation exceeds each site's own baseline scatter, consistently across sites.

**Phase 3 — score.**
7. Compare the eclipse-day precession rate and period against the site's own baseline, in units of the baseline scatter. Pool across households only if the protocols match.

## Pass / fail (pre-registered)

- **PASS:** the eclipse-day **azimuth precession rate** deviates from the site's baseline by **≥ 3× the baseline scatter** in the same direction across **≥ 2 of 3 independent eclipse sessions** (or across **≥ 2 of 3 households** at the same eclipse) — the eclipse day looks different from ordinary days.
- **FAIL:** the eclipse-day series is **within the baseline scatter** at every site and every session — the pendulum does not see the eclipse at your scale. **A complete and valuable result** (Skeptic's Star). This is the expected outcome and matches the modern null literature.
- **INCONCLUSIVE:** fewer than 3 baseline sessions; the baseline scatter exceeds the predicted effect size (the rig cannot resolve it); the eclipse arm was not run at the same clock time; the geometry or release changed between arms.
- **VOID:** the eclipse was not visible from the site; the rig was disturbed mid-session; the reader knew which arm they were in and adjusted the reading; the protocol was changed after the eclipse began.
- **ARTIFACT:** the deviation tracks **indoor temperature** rather than the eclipse (re-run with the room held at constant temperature); or it appears on a **non-eclipse day** at the same clock time (a time-of-day artifact); or it disappears when the observer is removed from the room (an observer/airflow artifact).

## Evidence

Photo of the rig and its geometry (line length, bob mass, release method, azimuth scale) + the **pre-registration sheet** (session times, thresholds, scoring rule, which geometry) + the full baseline series with scatter + the full eclipse-day series + the indoor temperature log for every session + the video of the azimuth scale + the per-household sheets if pooled + the void/artifact checks reported whether or not they void the run + the eclipse-visibility record for each site.

## Confounds (named in advance)

- **Tilt of the vertical / ground tilt** — the leading mainstream explanation; a pendulum is a tilt sensor. Mitigate by running the baseline at the same clock time and by logging temperature.
- **Thermal drift** — a warm room changes the line length and the air density. Hold the room as constant as possible and log temperature.
- **Time-of-day** — the single most important control: the baseline must be at the *same clock time* as the eclipse maximum.
- **Observer / airflow** — a person near the bob adds heat and air movement. Read the scale from video.
- **Small-N** — one eclipse is one data point. The card is honest that a single session cannot settle anything; pooling across households is the point.

## Verdict rules

≥2 independent households reporting the same eclipse-day deviation beyond their own baseline scatter → **supports**. All sites within scatter → **refutes at home scale** (and joins the modern null literature). Mixed → **inconclusive**. Any protocol change after first contact → **void**.
