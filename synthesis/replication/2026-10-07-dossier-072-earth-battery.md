## The claim (as asserted by the source)

The CGRI page describes telluric currents and then states their practical use:

> "Telluric currents are phenomena observed in the mantle of the earth. In September 1862, an experiment was carried out in the Alps of Munich (Lamont, 1862) to examine specifically terrestrial currents. The currents are caused primarily by changes in the outer part of the Earth's magnetic field, and are usually caused by the interactions between the solar wind and the magnetosphere or are results of solar radiation on the ionosphere. … These currents are known to have diurnal characteristics and the general direction of flow is towards the sun."

> "The telluric currents can be used to produce a useful low-voltage current with the help of the earth battery. Such devices were used for the telegraph system in the United States until 1859."

**What is being tested, in one sentence:** does a buried two-electrode "earth battery" produce electrical power **beyond what ordinary galvanic (electrochemical) action predicts**?

## Honest status of the claim

**The archive holds the claim and the ordinary explanation side by side, and they make different predictions.** The mainstream account of an earth battery is **galvanic**: two *dissimilar* metals in a moist electrolyte (soil) form a galvanic cell — the same electrochemistry as a lemon battery — and the soil is the electrolyte. That mechanism is well established and predicts exactly the small voltages earth batteries are known for. The tradition's account is **telluric**: the power comes from Earth currents driven by the solar wind and the magnetosphere, which is why the source describes them as diurnal and directed sunward.

Both explanations predict a small voltage from a buried pair. **They do not predict the same thing**, and the difference is directly testable with a material swap:

| | Galvanic (ordinary) | Telluric (the source's claim) |
|---|---|---|
| **Two identical metals** | no cell → ~no power | power still appears (the Earth supplies it) |
| **Two dissimilar metals** | power appears | power appears |
| **What the output tracks** | electrode material, soil moisture and salinity; fades as the anode corrodes | geomagnetic activity (Kp) and the diurnal cycle; independent of electrode material |

Four things the card must say out loud, because they are what a family is actually testing:

1. **The claim is a claim, not an established result.** The telluric framing comes from the geobiology/radiesthesia lineage. The ordinary explanation — galvanic electrochemistry — is well established and predicts the same small voltages. The card is a test, not an endorsement.
2. **The expected result is the null (galvanic), and that is a complete result.** A clean galvanic outcome is a genuine finding about the method — exactly what the "Skeptic's Star" honours — and it is worth as much as a positive.
3. **The control is the experiment.** The identical-metal pair is the whole design. If power appears with two identical electrodes, galvanic action cannot explain it, and that would be genuinely interesting. If it does not, the effect is chemistry.
4. **A positive would need the output to track geomagnetic activity, not electrode material, and to repeat.** A single day's reading is noise; the strong version is a sustained output from the identical pair that rises and falls with the Earth's magnetic weather.

**Under-promise, stated plainly:** the likely outcome is that the dissimilar pair works and the identical pair does not — i.e., the effect is galvanic, and the 1841 telegraph ran on chemistry, not Earth currents. That is the honest verdict, and the card is designed to reach it cleanly.

## Replicability: `home`

- Build/obtain cost: **~$0–20** — a copper rod or length of copper pipe (~$5–10), a zinc or galvanised-iron rod (~$2–5), a second copper rod for the control pair (~$5), a digital multimeter if not already owned (~$10–20), wire and a log sheet. A load test needs one LED and a resistor (~$1).
- Safety: **low.** No mains voltage, no chemicals, no heat. The only real hazards are a buried-utility strike (call before you dig) and a meter used on the wrong range.
- Accessibility: any household with a patch of soil — garden bed, lawn, or a large planter. A balcony household can run it in two deep pots.

## Apparatus (Bill of Materials)

- **Pair A (dissimilar metals):** one copper rod + one zinc or galvanised-iron rod.
- **Pair B (identical metals — the control):** two identical copper rods.
- **A digital multimeter** (DC volts and DC milliamps).
- **Wire** to reach the meter, and a **log sheet**.
- **A load** — one LED with a series resistor, or a low-power clock.
- **A soil-moisture check** — a hand-squeeze test is enough; record it.
- **A public Kp-index source** — the planetary geomagnetic activity index, for the correlation step.

## Protocol (pre-registered)

**Phase 0 — instrument check (mandatory).**
1. Measure a known AA cell with the meter and confirm it reads ~1.5 V. **A meter that cannot read a known source makes a no-voltage result VOID, not a null.** Confirm both pairs read ~0 V with the rods in air (no soil contact).

**Phase 1 — bury, matched.**
2. Bury both pairs in the **same soil**, about **2 m apart** so they do not interact, at the **same depth (~30 cm)** and with the **same rod spacing within a pair (~1 m)**. Record soil moisture (hand-squeeze) and temperature at burial.

**Phase 2 — measure daily.**
3. At the **same time each day for 14 days**, record for each pair: **open-circuit voltage (Voc)** and **short-circuit current (Isc)**, plus the time of day and the day's soil dampness. Note any rainfall.

**Phase 3 — load test.**
4. Connect each pair to the **same small load** (LED + resistor, or a low-power clock) and record whether it runs and for how long.

**Phase 4 — corrosion check.**
5. Pull the rods, photograph them, and note the corrosion on each — especially the zinc anode of Pair A.

**Phase 5 — correlate.**
6. Compare each pair's daily output against (a) the other pair, (b) the day's **Kp index**, and (c) the time of day. A telluric source predicts a correlation with (b) and (c) and a comparable output from Pair B; a galvanic source predicts a difference between the pairs and a decay that tracks corrosion.

## Pass / fail (pre-registered)

- **PASS (claim survives — surprising):** Pair B (identical electrodes) produces a **sustained current comparable to Pair A** (say **≥ 25 %** of Pair A's Isc), **and** the output of both tracks **geomagnetic activity (Kp)** or a **diurnal cycle** rather than electrode material.
- **PASS (claim refuted — the expected, complete result):** Pair A produces a measurable voltage and current, **Pair B stays near zero** (≤ a few % of Pair A), **and** Pair A's output **falls as its zinc anode corrodes** — the effect is **galvanic** (Skeptic's Star).
- **FAIL:** the two pairs differed in soil, moisture, depth or spacing; the meter's **input impedance / burden voltage** was not accounted for; readings were taken within minutes of burial before the soil–electrode interface stabilised; or the pairs were closer than ~2 m and coupled.
- **VOID:** no measurable voltage on **either** pair (bad contact, bone-dry soil, or a faulty meter), or the Phase 0 instrument check failed. **A VOID is not a null.**
