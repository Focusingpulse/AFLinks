## The claim (as asserted by the sources)

Giorgio Piccardi (1895–1972), professor of physical chemistry at the University of Florence and head of the **Centro Universitario dei Fenomeni Fluttuanti (CUFF)**, proposed that water is not a passive solvent but a **fluctuating, environmentally-coupled system** — and that this is measurable in a beaker.

The measurement is a simple inorganic reaction. A hydrochloric solution of **bismuth(III) chloride** is added to water:

> BiCl₃ + H₂O → **BiOCl↓** + 2 HCl

The insoluble product, **bismuth oxychloride**, forms a white colloid that settles as sediment. Piccardi's claim is not about the precipitate as such — it is about the **rate at which it settles**, and specifically about how that rate changes when the beaker is screened:

1. **The screen effect (the P test).** Two matched sets of beakers are run simultaneously — one set in open air, one under a **thin copper screen** acting as a Faraday cage. After a fixed time, the sediment heights are compared **pair by pair**, and the result is expressed as the **P index: the percentage of pairs in which the screened beaker's sediment is higher than the open beaker's.** Under the null hypothesis (no screen effect) the P index sits at **50%**.
2. **The cosmic-fluctuation claim.** Piccardi reported that the P index is **not** reproducible in the ordinary sense — it drifts and fluctuates, and over long runs was reported to correlate with **solar activity (the ≈11-year sunspot cycle and the ≈22-year Hale cycle), geomagnetic activity, and seasonal/annual components.** Daily P tests were run in Florence by Piccardi from **1951 to 1972** and at the Université Libre de Bruxelles by **Carmen Capel-Boute from 1956 to 1978**; Majorino & Zecca's Fourier analysis of the Florence data found best-fit periods of ≈12 years (5-component expansion) shifting to ≈22.5–24 years (10-component), which they read as confirming the solar-cycle hypothesis. The *Euro Spectra* write-up (1969) states the index was recorded three times a day and published regularly by CUFF.
3. **The shielding sub-claim (the sharpest one).** Brown reports Piccardi's finding that water in a beaker **still responds inside a copper Faraday cage** — i.e. the coupling is *not* ordinary electromagnetic radiation in the usual sense. Piccardi's group distinguished an **F/D/P test family** — F (outside) runs in normal rooms, D (in) runs in a room converted into a Faraday cage, P runs with only *some* tubes shielded. Artemi (2015) notes the P test was the "shielded vs open" comparison.

He generalised this into what is now called **Piccardi's law**: *heterogeneous systems out of equilibrium, if complex enough, respond to any external signal, however low its energy level.* Inorganic colloids in evolution are complex enough; colloids in living organisms more so. For such systems **reproducibility is impossible in principle**, because there is no way to isolate them completely from the rest of the universe — they behave as open systems coupled to a fluctuating environment. The corollary he drew: experimental methodology must treat **time as an irreversible coordinate** bound to surrounding conditions (atmospherics, cosmic rays).

**A supporting result from the same group:** at **38.5 °C the reaction stops responding** to environmental changes (the system reaches equilibrium), which the group read as evidence that **water structure** — not the chemistry — is the fluctuating element.

**Honest status of the claim.** The P test is a genuinely awkward piece of science to assess, and the dossier will not pretend otherwise:
- The original **measurement was qualitative in each pair** — a visual judgement of which of two sediment heights is taller — collapsed into a percentage over ten pairs. Artemi (2015) states plainly: *"the tests were not quantitative measures but qualitative assessments."*
- The group's papers were criticised for **reporting interpolation curves without experimental points**, and it is **not clear whether they used tap, demineralised, or distilled water**. Artemi also notes a "strange coexistence of precision and inaccuracy" — Becker tubes used to confine precipitation to the bottom, alongside unexplained water.
- **Replication attempts have had diverse and contradictory outcomes** (Eichmeier & Büger's German attempt, among others). Mainstream science generally regards the effect as **unreproduced / a statistical artefact**, and his statistical methods were criticised.
- Brown's secondary claims are internally inconsistent: he variously describes the Florence run as "nine and a half years" and "over thirty years," while the documented run is **1951–1972 (≈21 years)**.

**What makes it a real test rather than a rigged one.** The headline claim is that a **thin copper screen changes a colloid's settling rate** — that is a concrete, mechanical, falsifiable proposition, and mainstream physical chemistry predicts it should simply sit at 50%. A family that runs this and lands at 50% has produced a clean negative on a 20-year national research programme. A family that lands away from 50%, repeatedly, has found something the textbooks say is not there. Both outcomes are informative, which is the property this engine asks for.

## Replicability: `home`

- Build cost: **~$30–60** (bismuth oxide or bismuth chloride, dilute hydrochloric acid, 20 identical small glasses or test tubes, fine copper mesh, a ruler).
- Safety: **dilute hydrochloric acid is corrosive.** This is adult-handled, gloves and eye protection, ventilated space, acid diluted by an adult before the run; keep it away from children and store labelled. Once diluted and made into the working solution the hazard is routine, but it is real and the card states it. No heat, no mains power, no pressurised anything.
- Accessibility: **one short session per day** (~20–30 minutes including settle time), run for as many days as the family will sustain — target 10, ideally 30. An adult prepares the solution and pours; a child can record heights and log the numbers; a second person can do the height reading blind to the shield assignment.

## Apparatus (Bill of Materials)

- **Bismuth source** — bismuth oxide (Bi₂O, sold for glazes/pigments) or bismuth chloride (BiCl₃, chemical supplier). The original was "a hydrochloric solution of bismuth(III) chloride," i.e. BiCl₃ dissolved in dilute HCl.
- **Dilute hydrochloric acid** — muriatic acid (hardware-store pool/cleaning acid) diluted well down by an adult, or laboratory-dilute HCl.
- **20 identical small beakers / glasses / test tubes** — same size, same glass, cleaned the same way. Uniformity matters: a diameter difference between the two sets would fake a screen effect.
- **Fine copper mesh or screen** — a "thin copper screen." Enough to cover ten vessels.
- **Optional control screen — stiff cardboard** — covers ten vessels identically to the copper but blocks light without shielding EM. This separates "copper did something electromagnetic" from "copper just shaded the beakers."
- **Ruler or a marked card** for reading sediment height against a fixed line.
- **A consistent water supply** — decide once (tap, filtered, or distilled) and record it, because the sources did not. Tap vs distilled is a real variable, not a detail.
- **Phone** for evidence photos, and a notebook for the daily log.

## Protocol (paired, blinded)

1. **Prepare the stock once** (adult, gloves/eye protection, ventilated). Dissolve the bismuth source in dilute HCl to a fixed, consistent strength. Record exactly what you used. Store labelled.
2. **Same time, same room, every day.** Ambient temperature matters (the group's own 38.5 °C result says the system stops responding when it reaches equilibrium) — run below that, ideally at a consistent indoor temperature. Record room temperature each session.
3. **Twenty vessels, same water.** Fill twenty identical vessels with the **same measured volume of the same water**, from one batch, in one sitting.
4. **Dose in the same order.** Add a fixed measured volume of stock to each vessel, in a fixed sequence, with the same timing. Start the clock on the last addition.
5. **Assign the sets blind.** Before the run, an adult labels the twenty vessels 1–20 and randomly assigns ten to the screened group and ten to the open group, recording the assignment (or sealing it). The person who reads heights at the end should **not** know which group is which.
6. **Screen the assigned ten** with the copper mesh; leave the ten open. Do not move, jostle, or re-light the vessels mid-run; keep the whole tray in one place.
7. **Fixed settle time**, e.g. 30 minutes (pick a time and never vary it — the index depends on the same protocol every day).
8. **Read heights pairwise.** For each of the ten assigned pairs, record which vessel has the **higher sediment** (or "tie"). Ties are recorded and excluded from the pair count, with the denominator noted.
9. **Compute the day's P index** = (pairs where the *screened* vessel was higher ÷ pairs compared) × 100.
10. **Log every day**, including abandoned or botched sessions — mark them excluded, do not delete them.
11. **Optional cardboard-control arm:** run a second, identical ten-pair comparison with cardboard screens in place of copper, on a subset of days. If copper and cardboard behave the same, any "screen effect" is shading, not shielding.
12. **Pull the public data at the end.** Daily **Kp index** (geomagnetic) and **sunspot number** are published openly. Do not look at them before running each day's session — that would defeat the test.

## Pass / fail criteria (pre-registered)

**Claim 1 — the screen effect (primary, best-powered).**
- **PASS:** over the whole run, the mean P index differs from **50% by more than 2 standard errors** (for N compared pairs, SE = √(0.25/N) × 100). At 30 days × 10 pairs = 300 pairs, SE ≈ 2.9%, so a mean outside roughly **44–56%** is a real deviation; at 10 days (100 pairs, SE ≈ 5%) the band is ≈ 40–60%. State the pair count with the result. → A thin copper screen systematically changes the BiOCl settling rate. That is the core Piccardi claim, and it is not supposed to be there.
- **FAIL:** the mean P index sits inside the 2-SE band around 50% with scatter consistent with a coin flip. → **Skeptic's Star** material. This is a genuinely valuable negative: it would mean the P index is measuring nothing at all, at home scale.
- **INCONCLUSIVE:** the run is shorter than 10 days, the height readings were taken with knowledge of the assignment, or the two sets were not truly identical (different vessel sizes, different water, different dose timing).

**Claim 2 — the cosmic-fluctuation claim (secondary, under-powered at home scale).**
- **PASS:** the daily P index correlates with the **Kp index** or **sunspot number** with the predicted relationship, at a level above chance, in the direction reported.
- **FAIL:** no relationship.
- **Be honest about power:** a 30-day series is *marginal* for a correlation test and this arm may simply come back inconclusive. State that up front rather than dressing a null as a refutation — and note that Piccardi's own evidence came from **21 years** of daily runs, which is precisely what a family cannot replicate. A family's real contribution here is a **clean short baseline**, not a verdict on the solar cycle.

**Claim 3 — the shielding sub-claim (optional, sharpest if run).**
- **PASS:** the copper screen and the cardboard control produce *different* P indices. → the screen is doing something electromagnetic, not merely shading.
- **FAIL:** copper and cardboard behave the same. → whatever the effect is, it is not copper-specific shielding.

Report the claims **separately**. A screen effect with no cosmic correlation is still a screen effect.

## What would change the verdict

- An **instrumented readout** replacing the eye comparison — a small camera on a fixed stand photographing all twenty vessels under identical light, with sediment height measured from the image — would remove the biggest objection (qualitative assessment) without changing the chemistry. That is the natural second-stage dossier.
- **Multiple households running the same protocol on the same days** would be the strongest possible test: if the P index correlates *between households* on the same days, no local artefact explains it; if it does not, the index is local noise. This is the "Uber for labs" shape the engine is building toward.
- A clean null at home, repeated by several families, would move the Piccardi effect firmly into the artifact column — and that is worth publishing as loudly as a positive.
