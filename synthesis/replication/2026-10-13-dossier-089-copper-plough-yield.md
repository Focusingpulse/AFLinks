## The claim (as asserted by the source)

**"Agricultural implements made of copper — copper-coated steel, or copper alloys such as phosphor bronze — raise crop yield and quality, where iron and steel implements lower them."** Schauberger's reported numbers, across the corpus:

| Report | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Post-war copper-implement tests | **>30%** yield increase + better quality | implosie.nl (primary) |
| Copper-coated iron plough, "several tests" | **17–35%** productive increase | *Ríos de Vida* |
| Large farm near Salzburg | **50%** production increase | *Ríos de Vida* |
| 1948/49 Salzburg strip tests (copper-coated vs steel) | **up to 40%** higher yield; cereals **6–8 in** taller with fuller ears | Coats, ch. 19.1 |
| One rye experiment | ears with **up to 104 grains** | Coats, ch. 19.1 |
| Kitzbühel potato experiment | tubers **up to 430 g**, **up to 20 eyes** | Coats, ch. 19.1 |
| 1951–52 Chemical Testing Centre (iron / iron+CuSO₄ / copper-only) | "copper obtained the best results" | *Ríos de Vida* |
| Jane Cobbald, own garden (1990s) | larger, better vegetables; flowers more aphid-resistant | Waraxa, *Przekrój* |

**The claimed mechanism** (recorded as the source's, not endorsed): when steel ploughshares are dragged through the earth, "tiny ferroelectric and ferromagnetic currents are generated by the interaction of the hard steel against the earth, which breaks down the nutrient-laden water molecules in the soil in a manner similar to electrolysis"; the soil's potential is discharged, its water's surface tension reduced, and small steel particles are shed as a film of rust that both feeds pathogens and raises the soil's iron content — and **iron-rich soils retain less water than copper-rich soils**. Copper, being "diamagnetic" (bio-magnetic) rather than ferromagnetic, is claimed to replace these destructive effects with beneficial ones and to "activate" the groundwater. On this view the land "should under no circumstances be worked with bare iron or steel, but with implements of wood, copper or copper alloys."

**Provenance note, stated up front:** every figure above is Schauberger's own or his biographers'. The 1948/49 Salzburg tests are described as a **paired strip design** (copper-coated vs steel, alternating strips) — that is a real controlled shape — but **no raw numbers, no table, no error bars, no statistics, and no independent replication** are given anywhere in the corpus. The "fertiliser lobby blocked it" story (the Salzburg Treasury director demanding a share of profits from the nitrogen industry) is part of the mythos, not evidence.

## Honest status of the claim

**Unreplicated; the mechanism is not accepted physics.** The ferroelectric/electrolysis-of-soil-water account has no standing in soil science. Copper is a genuine **micronutrient** (a deficiency causes poor growth) and copper sulphate is a classic **fungicide** — either could produce a real difference in some soils without any "energetic" mechanism, and the archive's own texts do not separate these ordinary explanations from the claimed one. Iron and steel tools have been standard for millennia; a 40% yield penalty from steel would have been noticed.

**What is nevertheless testable** — and this is the whole justification for the card — is the **yield claim itself**, in the source's own paired-strip shape, at garden scale: *does a matched plot worked with a copper tool out-yield the plot worked with a matched steel tool, with everything else identical?* The source supplies the paired design; the card only scales it down and adds the matching the source's own account lacks (tools matched in **shape and mass**, so only the metal differs). The endpoint — **mass of harvest** — is a hard, archivable number, and the whole rig is cheap.

**Under-promise, stated plainly:** the expected result is a **null** — the two plots within garden noise of each other. A home garden has large variance; a 15–20% difference is at the edge of what a single season can resolve, which is why the card requires **≥3 matched pairs** and a **second season** before it calls a positive. A clean positive (copper plots consistently ahead, replicating) would be the surprise and would need an independent family before it is believed. A null is a complete, useful result and earns the Skeptic's Star.

## The apparatus

| Item | Spec | ~Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Copper tool** | A copper or bronze garden tool — a hoe, spade, or hand cultivator — whose soil-contacting part is copper or a copper alloy (bronze/phosphor-bronze). The implosie.nl source names bronze hand tools made in Austria and sold in the Netherlands (kopersporen.nl); any bronze/copper garden tool qualifies. | $25–80 |
| **Steel tool (control)** | A steel tool of the **same type, shape and mass** as the copper one — ideally the same model in steel, or the same tool with the blade swapped. Weigh both; record the difference. | $15–40 |
| **Plots** | ≥3 matched pairs of garden plots (or one bed split into alternating strips, the source's own Salzburg shape). Same soil, same sun, same water, same seed, same planting date. | $0–30 |
| **Seed** | One fast, uniform crop per pair (radish, lettuce, bush beans, or a root crop) from a single seed packet, so the pairs are genetically identical. | $5–15 |
| **Scale** | A kitchen scale (1 g resolution) for the harvest mass. | $0–20 |

**Total: ~$30–100.** Home garden; no lab, no mains wiring, no chemistry.

**Why the matching is the whole card.** The source's own tests compared a copper-coated plough against a steel plough of the same form — so the *shape* was matched and only the metal differed. A home test must do the same: if the copper tool is a different shape, cuts deeper, or is heavier, the yield difference measures the *tool*, not the *metal*. Hence: match the tools in shape and mass, or use one tool with a copper sleeve for the treatment arm and a steel sleeve for the control arm so the physical action is identical.

**The two confounds the design must name (and the card does):**
1. **Copper is a nutrient and a fungicide.** A copper tool sheds copper ions into the soil (as the steel tool sheds iron). In a copper-deficient soil, that alone could raise yield; against a fungal pest, it alone could protect the crop. So the card records the soil's copper status if a soil test is available, and reports any visible disease pressure — because "copper helps" and "copper *energetically* helps" are different claims.
2. **The steel tool sheds rust** (the source's own point). A steel tool left wet between sessions sheds iron oxide into the soil; the card specifies the tools are cleaned and dried identically, so the comparison is of the metal in use, not of rust contamination.

## Protocol (pre-registered)

1. **Prepare.** Choose ≥3 matched pairs of plots (or a bed split into ≥6 alternating strips). Record each plot's position, soil type, sun exposure, and (if available) a soil test including copper and iron. Mark the plots and photograph them before planting.
2. **Match the tools.** Weigh the copper and steel tools; photograph them side by side; record any shape or mass difference. If they differ, either re-match or state the difference as a limitation.
3. **Pre-register.** Before planting, write down: the crop, the seed lot, the planting date, the number of pairs, the tool assignment per plot, the watering schedule, the harvest criterion (e.g. "all roots ≥10 mm", or "all pods at first fill"), the primary endpoint (total harvest mass per plot), and the pass/fail thresholds. Photograph the sheet.
4. **Plant and work.** Sow the same crop, same density, in every plot on the same day. Work each plot with its assigned tool at the same times and the same intensity (e.g. one pass of hoeing per week per plot). Water all plots identically from a single source.
5. **Measure the secondary endpoint (soil water).** Once mid-season, take a matched soil sample from each plot (same depth, same time of day), weigh it wet, dry it fully, and weigh it dry — **gravimetric water content**. The source claims copper-rich soil retains more water; this is a direct, cheap test of that sub-claim.
6. **Harvest.** On the pre-registered criterion, harvest each plot separately and weigh the total (and count the marketable units). Record per plot.
7. **Repeat.** Run the whole thing again the next season (or with a second crop in the same season), to see whether any difference persists.

**Measurable outcome:** total harvest mass per plot (primary); gravimetric soil water content per plot (secondary); the copper/steel tool mass and shape difference; the soil's copper/iron status; any visible disease pressure.

## Pass / fail (pre-registered)

- **PASS (claim survives — the surprise):** the copper-tool plots out-yield their matched steel-tool plots by **≥15%** on the mean across ≥3 pairs, in the same direction in **≥2 of 3** pairs, **and** the difference replicates in a second season — the metal of the tool is doing something the design cannot explain away.
- **PASS (claim refuted — the expected, complete result):** the mean difference is within **±10%** (garden noise), or the copper plots do not consistently lead — the metal of a garden tool does not change the harvest at home scale. (Skeptic's Star.)
- **FAIL (void, not a null):** the pairs differ in soil, sun, water, seed, or planting date; the tools differ in shape or mass beyond a stated tolerance; the tools were not cleaned/dried identically (rust contamination); the crop failed (pest, drought, frost) in one arm only; or the harvest criterion was changed after seeing the plots.
- **INCONCLUSIVE:** one season only; fewer than 3 pairs; no tool-matching; no soil-water measurement; the scorer knew which plot was which while judging quality.

## Artifacts to watch for

- A yield difference that tracks **plot position** (edge effects, sun, drainage) rather than the tool — check by alternating the tool assignment between seasons.
- A difference caused by **copper ion shedding** (a nutrient/fungicide effect) rather than the claimed "energetic" one — check the soil's copper status and any disease pressure.
- A difference caused by **tool mass or shape** (a heavier tool tills deeper) — check the recorded mass difference.
- A difference within the garden's own **run-to-run variance** — establish the variance first by running two steel-tool plots against each other.

## What would change my mind

A pre-registered, matched-pair, two-season garden test in which the copper-tool plots consistently and substantially out-yield the steel-tool plots, **with the tool mass and shape matched and the soil's copper status accounted for**, would move this from "Schauberger's unreplicated claim" to "a real effect worth a controlled agronomic trial." A null bounds the claim at garden scale only — it does not disprove an effect that requires a full-size plough and a whole field.
