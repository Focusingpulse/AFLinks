## The claim (as asserted by the source)

The source's argument runs: rain falling on bare, compacted, sun-heated ground runs off instead of infiltrating; the water table falls; the fix is to **retain water where it falls** so that "no rainwater or wastewater leaves the terrain," after which "all outflowing water should once again be spring water." The named methods are "retention spaces, from checkdams, swales (ditches), terraces, deep plowing along keylines, or through special forms of land use such as reforestation, ecological agriculture, or ... Holistic Management."

Three claims are separable, and only the first is what this card tests:

1. **The mechanism (tested here).** A retention earthwork — a swale dug on contour — captures surface runoff and infiltrates it into the soil instead of letting it leave the plot. This is **mainstream-supported**: it is the core of Keyline, of Brad Lancaster's rainwater-harvesting work, and of ordinary agricultural engineering. **This card is the queue's first whose core mechanism is not contested** — and it says so plainly rather than dressing a known practice as a discovery.
2. **The design claim (tested here, as a second arm).** The source insists the retention space must be **shaped** — "the banks are never built straight or reinforced, but are created in meandering forms, with steep zones and flat zones, so that the water can turn and spin"; the long axis is set to the prevailing wind so waves oxygenate the water; deep and shallow zones are cut to create temperature zones; the dam is built of natural material, ideally clay, with **no plastic liner or cement**. The contestable part is the last one made explicit: **does form change behaviour beyond volume?** Mainstream engineering says a ditch is a ditch — what matters is capacity, infiltration area, and residence time. The Schauberger lineage says the *shape* matters. A straight ditch and a meandering swale of equal volume can be compared directly.
3. **The strong claims (NOT tested here, and named as untested).** "No rainwater or wastewater leaves the terrain"; "all outflowing water should once again be spring water"; a new seepage spring emerging in the first year; and the framing that water is "a living being" that "matures" underground and "absorbs information." These are whole-catchment, multi-year, and in part metaphysical claims. **A home plot cannot test them, and this card does not pretend to.** A pass here says nothing about aquifer recharge or spring re-emergence.

**Claimants:** Bernd Walter Mueller (Tamera Global Ecology Institute), with Sepp Holzer; lineage to Viktor Schauberger, Michal Kravčík, P. A. Yeomans
**Verification status:** mechanism (1) is well-established and independently documented outside this source; the design claim (2) is asserted by the source and, as far as the archive shows, never tested against an equal-volume straight control; the strong claims (3) are unreplicated and site-specific
**Mainstream position:** the mechanism is standard practice; the form claim is not established; the "complete water cycle restored / springs re-emerge" claim is a strong whole-catchment claim usually reported anecdotally

## The protocol — three plots, one wet season, two questions

The testable core: **if a contour swale retains rain, its plot should shed measurably less runoff and infiltrate measurably more water than an untouched control plot. And if the source is right that shape matters, a meandering swale should behave differently from a straight ditch of the same volume.**

### Materials (~$0–100)

1. **A spade and a mattock** — the earthwork is dug by hand (free; the source's own construction is hand-built with natural materials).
2. **A simple rain gauge** (~$5–15) — a straight-sided tin or a purchased gauge, to record rainfall depth per event. **Without this the runoff numbers have no denominator.**
3. **A soil-moisture meter** (~$10–20) or a soil auger and the feel/ribbon method (free) — for the 15 cm and 30 cm readings.
4. **Bottomless tin cans** (~$0) — 3–4 cans, ~10 cm diameter, both ends removed, for the infiltration test.
5. **A measuring tape, stakes, string, a level** (a length of clear tubing filled with water makes a cheap level — free).
6. **Buckets or a length of gutter** (~$0–30) — to collect and measure runoff at the downslope edge of each plot.

### Design — the parts that make it a measurement

1. **Pick three adjacent plots on the same slope**, similar soil, similar vegetation, similar aspect. Mark them **Control** (left untouched), **Swale** (contoured, meandering), **Ditch** (straight, equal volume). Record plot width and slope. **If the three plots differ by more than ~30% in their pre-build infiltration rate, move them or stop** — a baseline mismatch makes the comparison uninterpretable.
2. **Baseline first.** Before digging anything, run the **can infiltration test** at three points in each plot: drive a bottomless can ~5 cm into the soil, pour 250 mL of water into it, and time until the water disappears. Record all nine numbers. Also record a soil-moisture reading at 15 cm and 30 cm in each plot.
3. **Build the Swale** in its plot: a **level ditch on contour** (use the water-tube level), ~2–3 m long, ~0.3 m deep, ~0.3 m wide, with the excavated soil bermed on the downhill side. **Meander the plan shape** and vary the bank profile — a steep zone and a flat zone — per the source's design. No liner. Leave the ends with a small spillway so overflow is controlled, not destructive.
4. **Build the Ditch** in its plot: a **straight, rectangular trench of the same volume** as the Swale (same length, depth, width — measure the excavated volume by counting buckets, and match it). No meander, straight banks, no liner.
5. **Install runoff collectors** at the downslope edge of all three plots: a shaped berm or a length of gutter leading into a bucket, capturing the surface flow leaving each plot. Keep them cleared of leaves.
6. **Run the wet season.** After each rain event of ≥5 mm, record: rainfall depth (gauge), runoff volume collected from each of the three plots (litres), and whether the swale and ditch are holding water. **Aim for at least five comparable events.** Record the water-holding duration in the Swale and the Ditch — how many hours after the rain stops before the standing water is gone (a marked stick reads the depth).
7. **Re-measure infiltration** in all three plots four weeks after the build, using the same can test at the same three points per plot.
8. **Measure the downslope effect**: soil moisture at 15 cm and 30 cm, 1 m downslope of each feature, versus the Control plot at the same relative position.

### Endpoints

- **Primary — runoff:** litres of runoff per mm of rain, per plot, across ≥5 comparable events. Compare Swale and Ditch against Control.
- **Secondary — infiltration:** can-test time (minutes for 250 mL) before vs after, per plot.
- **Secondary — retention:** hours of standing water held in Swale and Ditch after a ≥10 mm rain.
- **Secondary — downslope moisture:** 15 cm and 30 cm readings, feature plot vs Control.
- **The form comparison:** Swale vs Ditch on retention time and infiltration, at **equal excavated volume**.

### Pre-registered pass/fail

- **PASS (retention works here):** over ≥5 comparable rain events, Swale-plot runoff is **< 50% of Control-plot runoff** **AND** Swale post-build infiltration time is **≥ 2× faster than its own pre-build baseline and faster than Control** **AND** the Swale holds water **≥ 6 h after a ≥10 mm rain** **AND** downslope 30 cm moisture is higher than Control's — **and the runoff result repeats in a second month.**
- **PASS (form matters):** at equal excavated volume, the Swale holds water **≥ 1.5× longer than the straight Ditch** and infiltrates measurably differently — i.e. **shape changed behaviour beyond volume.**
- **FAIL:** Swale-plot runoff **≥ Control's**, or infiltration unchanged from baseline — an honest negative (Skeptic's Star). **Or:** Swale and Ditch are indistinguishable on every endpoint at equal volume — the form claim refuted at home scale, which is a complete and useful result.
- **INCONCLUSIVE:** fewer than five rain events, or no measurable rain in the season; the three plots differed by >30% at baseline; the swale was dug off-contour and overflows immediately (a build error, not a result); or the runoff collectors were overtopped.

It is also the queue's **first card whose core mechanism is mainstream-supported rather than contested**. That is stated as a finding, not hidden: the value here is not that the claim is doubtful, it is that the numbers are **yours** — how much rain your ground actually sheds, and whether a weekend's digging changes it. The contested part of the source is carried as the second arm (does form matter beyond volume?), and the source's strong claims are explicitly out of scope.

## Replicability: `home`

- Build cost: **~$0–100** (a rain gauge, a moisture meter, cans, buckets; the digging is labour).
- Safety: **none beyond ordinary care** — hand tools, no mains work, no chemicals. **Call 811 / your utility locating service before digging anywhere a buried line may run.** Do not dig into a slope that could undercut a structure or a road; keep swales away from foundations and septic fields.
- Accessibility: one weekend to build, one wet season to run. The whole protocol is three small plots on one slope.

## Apparatus (Bill of Materials)

- Spade, mattock, wheelbarrow (for the equal-volume check).
- Rain gauge; soil-moisture meter or auger.
- 3–4 bottomless tin cans (~10 cm dia); 250 mL measure; stopwatch or phone timer.
- Measuring tape, stakes, string, water-tube level.
- Buckets or gutter lengths for runoff capture; a marked stick for water depth.
- A pre-registration sheet and a pen.

## What a result means (and what it does not)

- A **pass** means: on your slope, this season, a hand-dug contour swale shed less runoff and infiltrated more water than an untouched plot — and, if the form arm passed, that *shape* changed the behaviour beyond volume. Those are real, local, measured results.
- It does **not** mean the landscape's water cycle is restored, that no water leaves your land, that a spring will emerge, or that an aquifer was recharged. Those are the source's strong claims and they are **out of scope at plot scale** — say so in the report.
- A **fail** means the earthwork did not change the water budget measurably on your ground. That is a complete result and earns the Skeptic's Star.
- The confounds to name: **antecedent soil moisture** (a plot that was already wet sheds differently), **slope and aspect mismatch between plots**, **one season is not a climate**, and **the collector itself changes the flow** (a berm or gutter can pond water that would otherwise have run past).
