## The claim (as asserted by the source)

The source records the tradition and then its own account of what is really happening:

> "in the 1960s so-called living water, produced according to Latyshev by electrolysis, reached us from the former USSR. It is supposed to accumulate around the cathode, while around the anode so-called dead water accumulates. Living water was supposed to have extraordinarily favourable effects on the healing of wounds, the growth of plants, hyperacidity of the stomach, etc. Dead water, on the other hand, disinfected wounds and was recommended for gargling with tonsillitis and influenza."

> "Reality was, however, different, although undeniably good results were achieved. At the cathode, after all, positive ions accumulate, but in the end negative ions supplied by the current source predominate here. At the anode it is the opposite."

> "The problem is that the results are influenced by the salt content. The more salts the water contains, the more precipitates of calcium and magnesium compounds form at the cathode, sometimes brownish from iron hydroxide, and the pH can reach a value of up to 11 … At the anode one can usually smell chlorine, which has disinfectant effects, and so it is no wonder that this water destroys bacteria. At the anode the pH also falls to as low as 3."

> "In addition there is the problem of the electrodes. Stainless steel does indeed serve as a cathode, but as an anode there should be pure carbon, because chromium dissolves out of stainless steel and colours the solution yellow."

**What is being tested, in one sentence:** does passing current through water make **two distinct waters** with properties beyond what ordinary electrochemistry predicts — or is it one water at two pH values, plus dissolved gas and chlorine?

## Honest status of the claim

**This is the queue's unusual case: the source is itself the skeptic.** The Czech text is a psychotronics club's own account of a method it does not endorse. It states the claim, then states the ordinary mechanism (ion accumulation, pH shift to ~11 at the cathode and ~3 at the anode, chlorine from the salt at the anode), then states the practical problems (salt-dependent precipitates, chromium dissolving out of a stainless-steel anode, the solution heating, the shock hazard). So the card is not "test a claim against a critic" — it is **"reproduce the critic's explanation with your own instruments, and see whether anything is left over."**

The two accounts make different predictions, and the difference is measurable at a kitchen table:

| | Ordinary electrochemistry (the source's own account) | "Two distinct waters" (the tradition's framing) |
|---|---|---|
| **What the cathode fraction is** | the same water, now alkaline (pH up to ~11) and hydrogen-rich | a different water — "living water" |
| **What the anode fraction is** | the same water, now acidic (pH down to ~3) with chlorine | a different water — "dead water" |
| **What happens when you recombine them** | acid + base → the pH returns to about where it started | two different waters need not recombine to the original |
| **What a stainless-steel anode does** | chromium dissolves out; the water discolours yellow | no prediction |
| **What a matched-pH control (plain water + baking soda) looks like** | matches the catholyte's pH; differs in dissolved hydrogen | should not match a genuinely different water |

Four things the card must say out loud, because they are what a family is actually testing:

1. **The claim is a claim, not an established result.** The "living water / dead water" framing comes from the Soviet-era popular-medicine tradition. The ordinary explanation — electrolysis, pH, dissolved gas, chlorine — is well established and predicts everything the tradition reports. The card is a test, not an endorsement.
2. **The expected result is the ordinary one, and that is a complete result.** A clean "it is just pH and chlorine" outcome is a genuine finding, exactly what the "Skeptic's Star" honours — and it is worth as much as a positive.
3. **The controls are the experiment.** The **mix-back** (does the pH return?) and the **matched-pH control** (does the catholyte differ from ordinary alkaline water?) are the whole design. Without them the family has measured a pH, not tested a claim.
4. **A positive would need a difference that pH and dissolved hydrogen cannot explain, and it would need to repeat.** A single reading is noise; the strong version is a catholyte that differs from the matched-pH control in a measured property, reproducibly, across three batches.

**Under-promise, stated plainly:** the likely outcome is that the two fractions are alkaline and acidic exactly as ordinary electrochemistry predicts, that mixing them back returns the pH, and that the stainless-steel anode discolours the water — i.e. "living water" is alkaline hydrogen-rich water, and the tradition's good results are the ordinary chemistry. That is the honest verdict, and the card is designed to reach it cleanly.

## Replicability: `home`

- Build/obtain cost: **~$30–120** — a low-voltage DC supply (a 9–12 V battery or a small bench supply, ~$10–20), a glass jar (~$5), two **carbon rods** (~$5–15; gouging rods or carbon arc rods), one stainless-steel rod or spoon (~$2), pH strips or a pH meter (~$5–30), a pinch of salt and some distilled or tap water. An **ORP meter** (~$20–40, or a pool ORP test kit) sharpens the discriminator but is optional — the core design works with pH strips, the mix-back, and the electrode test.
- Safety: **low, with three named conditions.** (1) **Low-voltage DC only — never mains.** (2) **Ventilate:** electrolysing salt water produces **chlorine at the anode** (the source notes the smell); run it outdoors or by an open window and do not breathe the gas. (3) **Do not ingest.** The card tests chemistry; it makes **no drinking-water or health claim**, and the fractions are for external cleaning/disinfection use only. The cell also **warms** — do not seal it, and watch the temperature.
- Accessibility: any kitchen. No mains work, no lab, no special glassware.

## Apparatus (Bill of Materials)

- **A low-voltage DC supply** — a 9–12 V battery, or a small adjustable DC bench supply (set low). **Never mains.**
- **A glass jar** (open, not sealed).
- **Carbon anode** — a carbon/gouging rod. (The control batch uses a **stainless-steel** anode instead.)
- **Stainless-steel cathode** — a stainless rod or spoon.
- **Water** — distilled if available, otherwise tap water; plus **a pinch of salt** (the source notes the salt content drives the result).
- **pH strips or a pH meter.**
- **An ORP meter (optional)** — the sharper discriminator.
- **Baking soda** — for the matched-pH control.
- **A log sheet** and a camera.

## Protocol (pre-registered)

**Phase 0 — instrument check (mandatory).**
1. Confirm the pH strips/meter read a known reference (e.g. distilled water ~7, vinegar ~3). **An uncalibrated meter makes every reading VOID, not a null.** Record the starting water's pH and (if available) ORP.

**Phase 1 — run the cell (carbon anode).**
2. Fill the jar, add a pinch of salt, insert the **carbon anode** and the **stainless cathode**, and run the low-voltage supply for **10–20 minutes** in a ventilated place. Note the smell at the anode, the temperature, and any precipitate or colour.
3. **Separate the two fractions** into two labelled glasses *before* measuring (do not measure the mixed jar). Record **pH** and, if available, **ORP** of each fraction, plus its colour and any sediment.

**Phase 2 — the mix-back.**
4. Recombine **equal volumes** of the two fractions in a third glass and record the pH (and ORP). Ordinary electrochemistry predicts it returns to about the starting pH.

**Phase 3 — the matched-pH control.**
5. Make a control: plain water brought to **the catholyte's pH** with baking soda. Record its pH (matched) and its ORP. If the catholyte is "just alkaline water", the two should match in pH and differ only in dissolved hydrogen.

**Phase 4 — the electrode control.**
6. Run a second batch with a **stainless-steel anode** in place of carbon. Compare the water's colour and the anode's condition (pitting, discolouration, a yellow tint in the water) with the carbon-anode batch.

**Phase 5 — repeat.**
7. Run the whole thing **three times** on different days. A one-off reading is not a result.

## Pass / fail (pre-registered)

- **PASS (claim refuted — the expected, complete result):** the cathode fraction is **alkaline** (pH ≳ 9) and the anode fraction **acidic** (pH ≲ 4) in the direction and rough magnitude ordinary electrochemistry predicts; **the mix-back returns the pH to about the starting value**; the **matched-pH control matches the catholyte's pH** (and differs only in ORP, i.e. dissolved hydrogen); and the **stainless-steel anode discolours the water while the carbon anode does not** — the effect is ordinary electrolysis (Skeptic's Star).
- **PASS (claim survives — surprising):** the catholyte differs from the **matched-pH control** in a measured property that **pH and dissolved hydrogen cannot explain**, reproducibly across **three** batches — e.g. a persistent difference in conductivity at matched pH and matched ORP, or a difference that survives the ORP decaying away.
- **FAIL:** the two fractions were **not separated before measuring** (the jar was measured mixed); the electrodes touched or shorted; the pH/ORP instrument was **uncalibrated**; readings were taken before the current had run **≥ 10 minutes**; or the batches differed in water, salt or electrode area and were compared as if matched.
- **VOID:** no current flowed (bad contact, insufficient voltage); the cell became **hot**; or the supply was inadequate. **A VOID is not a null.**
