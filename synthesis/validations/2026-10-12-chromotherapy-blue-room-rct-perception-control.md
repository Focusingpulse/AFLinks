# Validation — Colour Therapy in a Blue Room: a moderate RCT effect that cannot be traced to the colour

**Domain:** alternative optics / colour therapy trials (environmental chromotherapy)
**Recorded:** 2026-10-12 (Replication Watch, run 11)

## What was claimed

The claim under test is the therapeutic version of the oldest colour idea: **exposure to coloured light changes the body, and can reduce distress.** In its clinical form it has two branches — *active* colour therapy (colouring, mandalas) and *passive* colour therapy (sitting in, or looking at, coloured light). This record is about the **passive/environmental** branch: the claim that colour in the environment is itself therapeutic.

The specific claim tested here is narrower and testable: **that a blue therapeutic environment reduces anxiety in chemotherapy patients more than a standard hospital environment does.**

The rail's existing record in this Yard is the anthroposophical **Metal Colour Light Therapy** series at Havelhöhe (`2026-09-30-colour-therapy-metal-color-light-havelhoehe.md`) — a **single-arm**, self-report, no-control series reporting fatigue and insomnia gains. That record's own honest verdict was that regression to the mean and expectancy were not excluded. This record is the other half of the rail: a **randomized, controlled** trial — and the control study that says the mechanism named in the claim is not the one operating.

## Who tested it, and when

- *"The Effect of Color Therapy on Anxiety in Chemotherapy Patients Attending the Oncology Department: A Randomized Clinical Trial."*
- Published in **SAGE Open Nursing** 2025;11:23779608251396225 (2025-12-11). doi **10.1177/23779608251396225**
- **PMCID: PMC12699003 · PMID: 41394008 · IRCT20130603013568N8** · ethics code IR.KUMS.REC.1400.636
- Data collected 2021–2022; funded by Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (grant 4020144).

**The dismantling control (2020, prior to the trial):**

**Domicele Jonauskaite, Isaline Tremea, Laura Bürki, Cheick N. Diouf & Christine Mohr**, University of Lausanne — *"To see or not to see: Importance of color perception to color therapy,"* **Color Research & Application** 45(3):450–464 (2020). doi **10.1002/col.22490**

## Method, as reported

**The trial (Safari Nezhad et al.).** A **randomized clinical trial** conducted 2021–2022, reported to CONSORT. **300 patients screened → 190 enrolled** (two equal arms), adults undergoing chemotherapy.

- **Intervention:** 20 minutes in a purpose-prepared **blue-themed room**, incorporating blue tones via LED lighting, bed linen, curtains, wall art and artificial flowers — with **no staff interaction** during the session.
- **Control:** 20 minutes in a **standard oncology room** — neutral white/off-white walls, conventional fluorescent lighting, no environmental modification.
- **Outcome:** **Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)** immediately before and after, 21 items, 0–63 scale.
- **Randomisation and concealment** were done properly and are reported in detail: computerised random number generation, managed by an independent statistician, sequence stored in sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, opened only at enrolment. Outcome assessors were blinded.

**The one thing the trial could not do, in the authors' own words:** *"Given the nature of the intervention, participants and care providers could not be blinded to group assignments due to the distinct environmental and procedural differences between intervention and control conditions."* There is **no sham-colour or sham-room arm.**

**The control study (Jonauskaite et al. 2020).** Two experiments that asked the discriminating question directly: is it the colour, or is it the colour-*routine*? Participants completed a commercial relaxation-through-colour routine while looking either at **the accompanying colour disks** or at a **white patch**. **Study 1 (longitudinal): n = 60, three weekly sessions. Study 2 (cross-sectional): n = 63, half the trials completed once.** Both full and **shortened** versions of the routine were tested.

## Result, as reported

**The trial — a moderate, statistically significant effect.**

| Group | BAI before | BAI after | Within-group p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intervention (blue room) | 57.70 ± 10.14 | 50.03 ± 8.49 | p = .001 |
| Control (standard room) | 56.48 ± 5.09 | 55.83 ± 9.15 | p = .406 |

Between-group ANCOVA controlling for baseline: **F(1,188) = 28.157, p = .001, η² = 0.131** — 13.1 % of the variance in anxiety reduction attributed to the intervention. Levene's test confirmed homogeneous variances (F(1,188) = 0.312, p = .577); baseline groups were homogeneous on demographics and disease characteristics (all p > .05). The authors conclude colour therapy is an effective, low-cost, non-invasive complementary method.

**Two things the numbers also say, which the abstract does not.** First, **the baseline BAI means sit at ~57 out of a maximum of 63.** The BAI's own severity bands put 26–63 as "severe," so both groups began near the *ceiling* of the instrument — which compresses headroom in the control arm and makes the control's flat result (56.48 → 55.83) hard to read as a true comparator rather than a floor effect. Second, **the control group's standard deviation roughly doubles** across the 20 minutes (5.09 → 9.15) while its mean barely moves; the intervention group's SD contracts (10.14 → 8.49). Neither is a refutation, but both are the kind of asymmetry a blinded, sham-controlled trial exists to remove.

**The control study — the colour was not required.** Jonauskaite et al. found **stress and anxiety decreased before-to-after the routine in both studies**, with the longitudinal study showing incremental week-by-week decreases. Crucially: *"decreases were the same whether participants (a) physically saw colors or not, and (b) completed the full or shortened version."* Their conclusion — that other factors, and not physical exposure to colour, explain the changes associated with this and probably other colour-therapy routines.

**And within the trial's own literature review, the sign flips with hue.** The authors cite Nazari et al. (2020), who found purple combined with natural sunlight reduced anxiety in ICU patients while **dark blue and green did not** — the opposite of the finding here, in a different population.

## Confidence: medium in the trial's execution; low that it supports a *colour* claim

**For the trial being competent.**

1. **It is randomized, with genuine allocation concealment** — computerised sequence, independent statistician, sequentially numbered sealed envelopes — which is better than much of this literature.
2. **Blinded outcome assessment**, and the unblinding limitation is stated explicitly rather than hidden.
3. **Adequately powered for its stated effect**, with a pre-specified calculation and a reported CONSORT flow.
4. **Homogeneity checks reported** for demographics and baseline scores.

**Against the claim it is offered in support of.**

1. **It cannot be blinded, and the authors say so.** The intervention is "a different room, decorated blue." Expectation, novelty, the act of being moved and attended to, and simply being in a *purpose-built* space are all confounded with colour.
2. **The control is not the right control.** A standard hospital room differs from the blue room in lighting, linen, décor, art, and the fact of having been prepared for a study. The Jonauskaite design — same routine, same room, colour disks versus a white patch — is what isolates colour, and it came back null on colour.
3. **A prior dismantling study sits underneath the claim, and the trial cites it** (reference [12] in the paper). Knowing that colour perception was found inert, the trial still chose a design that cannot distinguish colour from room.
4. **Baseline scores near the instrument ceiling**, with an unexplained variance asymmetry between arms.
5. **The hue is not stable across the literature** — blue helped here, blue was inert in Nazari 2020, purple helped there.
6. **Self-report subjective outcome** only; the protocol names no objective or physiological measure.

## Honest framing

We hold no brief — the test is the point.

**Read together, this rail now says something clean and slightly uncomfortable: two well-intentioned clinical protocols, in two countries, using two different colour instruments, both report real improvement in patients — and neither can attribute the improvement to colour.** The Havelhöhe series had no control at all. This trial has a control, but the control is "a different room," and the one experiment designed to separate colour from everything else around it (Jonauskaite 2020) found the improvement was identical whether the participants saw the colours or a blank white patch.

That does not make the patients' improvement unreal. It makes it **unattributed**. A blue room that reliably reduces a chemotherapy patient's anxiety by seven points on the Beck scale is a perfectly good thing to build, whatever the mechanism — and "the room was designed for me" is a mechanism. What the evidence does not support is the specific claim that it is the *wavelength* doing the work.

The teaching point for the Yard is the one this rail shares with the dowsing and biofield rails: **the tradition's own control, run honestly, tends to dissolve the tradition's own mechanism while leaving some effect standing.** The remaining question is not "does colour therapy work" but "**what, exactly, does the active-ingredient test need to hold fixed**" — and in a room-based intervention, that is nearly everything except the colour.

**What would change this record:** a randomized trial with a **sham room** (same décor, lighting, layout, attention and staff behaviour; neutral or non-therapeutic colour, with participants still unable to tell which arm they were in) and an **objective** secondary outcome. **A null from that design would be as useful as a positive.** And the LUCIA 2.0 waitlist-controlled trial — which holds the instrument fixed and randomises *time* rather than *colour* — will move this rail whichever way it lands.
