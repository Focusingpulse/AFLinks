# Validation — Passive Copper-Dowel Electroculture, No Benefit

**Domain:** electro-culture / magneto-culture
**Recorded:** 2026-09-18 (Replication Watch)

## What was claimed

That inserting copper-wrapped wooden dowels into the soil improves plant growth and yield — the "passive electroculture" practice promoted on social media and now sold as a product in big-box stores for home container gardening. It descends from a three-century electro-culture lineage (Nollet 1747; Lemström 1904; Blackman 1924), in which *active* electric fields and currents at 6–1200 V have sometimes improved crop performance.

## Who replicated it, when

Mya Chier, Aidan Oakey, Michelle L. Budny and Nathan P. Lemoine (Department of Biological Sciences, Marquette University; Milwaukee Public Museum, Wisconsin, USA). Published in *PLOS ONE* 20(8): e0329615, 7 August 2025. Funded by US National Science Foundation grant DEB 1941390.

## Method

Four species — two leafy greens (mustard greens, white Russian kale) and two root vegetables (turnips, beets) — grown in individual one-gallon pots under LED lights at ~25 °C. Each plant randomly assigned to one of three treatments, n = 10 per treatment per species: (1) control, no copper; (2) exposed copper — a 40 cm dowel coiled with copper wire, inserted 10 cm into soil; (3) buried copper — the same dowel fully covered by soil, to separate an electrical effect from a copper-fertilization effect. Weekly measures of height and SPAD leaf greenness; net CO2 assimilation and stomatal conductance via LI-6800; above- and belowground dry biomass after eight weeks. Electrical transmittance of the rods was measured directly with an oscilloscope.

## Result

No consistent evidence that passive electroculture improves growth, photosynthesis or yield. Height and SPAD showed no treatment effect in any species. CO2 assimilation (Anet) was unaffected for kale, mustard greens and turnips; beets showed two isolated, mutually inconsistent low readings traced to a lower-light block artifact. Turnips and beets with *buried* copper produced ~2.5 g more aboveground biomass (p < 0.01) and turnips ~1 g more belowground biomass, but the *exposed* copper treatment — with the same copper in the soil — did not, so the authors attribute it to small sample size rather than copper fertilization or conduction. Direct measurement: a rod transmits only ~2 mV into the soil (~20 mV near a fluorescent light), versus the hundreds of volts used in successful active electro-culture tests. The authors conclude passive copper dowels "would waste both financial and natural resources."

## Confidence: high

Peer-reviewed; pre-stated hypotheses; randomized, replicated arms across four species with n = 10 each; independent of the claim's promoters; NSF-funded with a data-availability statement (figshare 10.6084/m9.figshare.28752290). Limits: container/greenhouse conditions over eight weeks only — it does not test active electric fields, field agriculture, or copper-deficient soils (an explicit reviewer question left open, since most potting mixes already contain copper). Note: an intended *active* electro-culture arm was lost when the electrical probes corroded, so only the passive claim is adjudicated here. This complements the Yard's earlier constant-magnetic-field null (Ivanov & Sokolova 2025) on the active-magnet side.
