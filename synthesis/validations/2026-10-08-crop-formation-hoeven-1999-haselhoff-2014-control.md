# Validation — The Hoeven 1999 Crop Formation and Its Man-Made Control (Netherlands, 2014)

**Domain:** borderland research / plant biophysics (crop-formation node anomalies — pulvinus elongation in flattened cereal)
**Recorded:** 2026-10-08 (Replication Seeder)

## What was claimed

In the summer of 1999, in a barley field at **Hoeven, The Netherlands**, a circular formation appeared. A local man, **Robbert van den Broeke**, reported seeing a luminescent sphere hovering above the field as the circle formed. A biophysical claim followed the sighting: when plant stems sampled from the circle were measured, their **growth nodes (pulvini)** were found lengthened far beyond the values that flattening alone produces — and the lengthening was **not uniform** but varied over the circle's area with a **radial symmetry**, highest at the centre and falling to control levels at the edges ([Haselhoff 1999, 2001a]). Fitted against the radiation intensity of an electromagnetic point source, the distribution matched a source at a height of **h = 4.1 m** with a Pearson coefficient **R = 0.99** on one transect. The **BOL model** ("ball of light") took the shape of the claim: the node lengthening was the signature of an electromagnetic source hovering above the field.

**What is being tested, in one sentence:** does deliberately flattening a circle of the same crop, at the same maturity and diameter, reproduce the *symmetry* of the 1999 node-length distribution — or is the structure a property of something other than flattening?

## Who tested it, when, and where

**Eltjo H. Haselhoff, Robert J. Boerman and Jan-Willem Bobbink** of the **Dutch Centre for Crop Circle Studies** — Haselhoff being one of the two researchers whose 1999 measurements the claim rests on. They created a control circle with the documented makers' method and repeated the 1999 measurements on it. Published in the *Journal of Scientific Exploration* **28**(1):17–33, 2014 (submitted 2013-09-09, accepted 2014-02-21).

The dispute this sits inside is itself in the record: the skeptical critique of the original work is **Grassi, Cocheo & Russo (2005)**, *JSE* 19(2):159–170, written from the original raw data, concluding that the electromagnetic claims were unsupported (sample sizes too small, control criteria never stated up front, and nothing demonstrated beyond a difference between flattened and upright plants — which any flattening produces). **Haselhoff (2007)** rebutted, arguing Grassi's central conclusions followed from reading Levengood's **averages of many stems as single measurements** and from wrong assumed control positions, and that standard t-tests had in fact been run with p < 0.05. The 2014 paper is Haselhoff and coauthors running the *new experiment* both sides had effectively asked for.

## Method — what they actually did

- **The control circle was made by hand.** A wooden board roughly **1.0 × 0.2 m** with a rope attached at both ends — the well-known circlemaker's method: hold the rope, stand on the board, trod the crop down while walking the arc.
- **Matched to the 1999 formation:** the same crop (**barley**, *Hordeum vulgare*), at approximately the same stage of maturity (height ~60 cm), and the **same diameter (9 m)**.
- **Same measurement protocol as 1999**, exactly: the same sampling scheme, the same number of stems per sample at each sampling location, sampling performed **six days after creation**, the same three-month drying time, and the same computer/pattern-recognition program for measuring the penultimate nodes.
- **Controls:** 3 sets of ~27 stems at 3 locations in the standing crop, away from the circle, giving an average node length of **2.4 mm** (the 1999 control average was **2.0 mm** over ~180 stems).
- A sunlight/shadow hypothesis was also tested: a computer model of **cumulative solar energy** at ground level inside the circle over the six days between creation and sampling, using the sun's June azimuth and elevation at Hoeven's latitude.

## Result — what was measured

A man-made circle **does** produce a substantial node increase. It does **not** reproduce the 1999 structure.

| Parameter | Hoeven 1999 | Man-made control circle |
|---|---|---|
| **Average node length, all flattened stems** | **171%** | **111%** |
| **Peak node length** | **214%** | **144%** |
| **Position of the peak** | **Centre of the circle** | **3 m off-centre** |
| **Radial symmetry** | Strong, bell-shaped, falling off in all directions to control levels | **Absent** — none of the 3 transects showed near-perfect centric symmetry |
| **Regional correlation between the two circles** | — | **Pearson R = 0.06** (0.0613) — the points scatter randomly |

- **The elongation alone is not evidence of anything.** The control circle's 144% peak exceeds even the ~120% Levengood's own earlier control study had reported for flattened plants — so "large node lengthening" is a thing treading crop down produces.
- **What did not appear is the structure** — the smooth radial fall-off from the centre, the bell shape, the symmetry. "Whatever mechanism caused the symmetry in the Hoeven node length distribution did not manifest itself in the control circle."
- **The sunlight/shadow account did not survive either.** Node length showed no obvious dependency on accumulated solar energy at any sampling point, in either circle.
- **The authors' own conclusion:** the findings in the 1999 circle "could not be reproduced and hence remain anomalous."

## Confidence: medium (for the control result) — the anomaly itself is unresolved

- **What this settles cleanly:** a man-made flattening circle in matched crop reproduces a substantial node increase but **not the radial structure** the claim rests on. Whatever produced the 1999 symmetry, it is not simply "a circle was flattened here."
- **What limits it, stated by the authors themselves, and it is real:** the experiment was **not blind** — the samplers and analysts knew which formation was which. It could not reproduce six days of unknown weather, soil and humidity from 1999. n is small (one control circle, one 1999 formation, ~25 samples each); a single control cannot establish what "natural" node-length variation inside flattened circles looks like in general.
- **The broader dispute is not closed by this paper.** The 2005 critique and the 2007 rebuttal stand on either side; this experiment added new data but did not adjudicate the statistical argument. And the eyewitness's credibility was separately damaged (van den Broeke's reputation collapsed after 2005 fraud accusations on Dutch television, per the paper's own note) — which the authors say does not change the measurable plant data, and which the record should hold without leaning on either way.
- **The one thing both sides agree needs to exist — a blind replication — still does not.** The 2014 paper is the closest thing to it; see Dossier 073.

## Honest framing

We hold no brief — the test is the point. **Recorded as attempted / measured / reported:** a biophysicist and two coauthors rebuilt the experiment the crop-circle dispute had needed for a decade — they flattened a matched circle of the same barley by hand and ran the identical measurements on it — and the man-made circle reproduced the *node elongation* but not the *radial symmetry* that the anomaly claim depends on. They concluded the 1999 findings could not be reproduced and remain anomalous, and they said out loud that their own test was not blind. Read it as a **replication attempt that split its verdict**: the floor claim (flattened plants differ from upright ones) is reproduced and is therefore uninteresting as evidence; the structural claim (a symmetric radial gradient aligned with the flattened area) was not reproduced and is therefore, on this evidence, not a general property of flattening. It does not license "debunked" — one non-blind control circle does not close a case the authors left open — and it does not license "proven" either. Both words are overstatements on this evidence, which is exactly why the blind version in Dossier 073 is the next step.
