# Validation — The Sub-keV Fusion Plateau: a mainstream result with an independent confirmation, and two incompatible mechanisms

**Domain:** LENR / condensed-matter nuclear science — the fusion-product branch (not the excess-heat branch)
**Recorded:** 2026-10-12 (Replication Watch, run 11)

## What was claimed

The claim under test is that **nuclear fusion in condensed matter is strongly enhanced relative to the bare-nucleus expectation**, because the electronic and structural environment of the reacting nuclei lowers the effective Coulomb barrier (electron screening).

The specific new claim is sharper than that: that the **D–D fusion yield in metal hydrides does not fall exponentially as the bombarding energy drops, but flattens onto a "plateau" — a finite, non-vanishing yield floor — below about 2 keV.**

The prior observation this rests on came from the **Czerski group at Szczecin** (Poland), who reported in 2024 that the ²H(d,p)³H thick-target yield in a **deuterated zirconium (ZrD₂)** target, measured down to a centre-of-mass energy of **0.625 keV**, stopped falling below ~1.25–2.5 keV and flattened. They extracted a screening energy of **340 ± 30 eV** (far above the ~100 eV of defect-free material) and interpreted the plateau as **thermal D–D fusion occurring inside ion tracks** — beam-induced zones of high phonon density where the local temperature momentarily exceeds the melting point.

## Who replicated it, and when

**The new experiment (2026):** Micah E. Karahadian, Matthew Colborne, Arun Persaud, Thomas Schenkel and **Jeremy N. Munday** — **University of California, Davis** with **Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory** (USA).

- Published in **Nature Communications**, 18 July 2026 — **doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74421-1** — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-74421-1 (open access).
- Preprint: **arXiv:2512.06212**, 5 December 2025.

This is the direct follow-up to the same group's *Nature* 2025 work (Chen et al., *Nature* 644:640–645) that showed electrochemical deuterium loading raising the D–D rate by 15–18 % at 15 keV.

**The independent confirmation (2026):** the **Czerski group** — University of Szczecin and Maritime University of Szczecin, in the EU **CleanHME** collaboration — reporting *"Thermal deuteron-deuteron fusion in metallic targets"* (**arXiv:2605.27438**). Having seen the plateau in **Zr**, they repeated the measurement in **Ti and Pd** targets to test whether the effect tracked the materials' different thermal properties. It did: the plateau appears for both, reached at **~3 keV for Zr** and **~2 keV for Ti**.

So the two laboratories reproduced **each other's** plateau — one in Pd and Ti by electrochemistry + ion beam, the other in Zr, Ti and Pd by implanted targets — and **disagree about what it means.**

## Method, as reported (the Davis / LBNL experiment)

A dual-chamber platform, with a **250 µm foil of palladium or titanium acting as the membrane between two environments**:

- **Electrochemical side:** heavy-water electrolyte (6.0 M D₂SO₄ in D₂O), three-electrode geometry, galvanostatic control at 300–700 mA cm⁻², driving deuterium into the metal lattice. The titanium foil was sputter-coated with a ~250 nm palladium layer on the electrolyte face to make deuterium uptake possible at all (Ti forms a passivating oxide in water).
- **Vacuum side:** a microwave-driven ECR ion source delivering a monoenergetic deuteron beam (≈85 % D⁺ / 15 % D₂⁺, ~150 µA, ~1 cm² spot) tunable from **Ecm = 0.25 keV to 6.5 keV**. At these energies the implantation depth runs from **~4 nm (0.25 keV) to ~70 nm (6.5 keV)** — so every beam-induced reaction is confined to a near-surface layer far thinner than the 250 µm separating it from the electrolyte.
- **Detection, in two independent channels:** the **3.02 MeV fusion proton** on a passivated implanted planar silicon diode (with a ~10 µm aluminium foil to stop secondary electrons), and the **2.45 MeV fusion neutron** on an EJ-309 liquid scintillator with pulse-shape discrimination (**>98 % γ rejection**). Because the two D–D branches have nearly equal branching ratios, the channels cross-check one another.

**Controls the authors report:** interleaved background runs with beam off and electrochemistry off, accumulating **>350 h**; the proton-channel background was **0.007 counts per hour** and flat across the window; signal-to-background ran from **>400:1 at Ecm = 3.0 keV** down to a minimum of **15:1 at the lowest energies**; run durations of 12–24 h per low-energy point. Detector geometry, acquisition settings and analysis were held identical across energies and materials.

**The load-bearing control, and the one worth quoting:** *"No excess proton or neutron counts at the energy channels detected in these experiments are observed during electrochemical loading in the absence of an ion beam."* In other words — **electrochemical loading alone produced no detectable fusion products.** Whatever this effect is, it is not the classic Fleischmann–Pons excess-heat signal, and the group says so.

## Result, as reported

- A **pronounced yield plateau — a finite yield floor — below ~2 keV**, in both Pd and Ti, in stark contrast to the exponential suppression the standard model predicts.
- At the lowest energies measured, the fusion enhancement factor relative to bare, unscreened nuclei **approaches or exceeds 10¹⁸**.
- Effective screening potential **Ue rises with electrochemical loading**: Pd **0.8 ± 0.2 keV → 1.7 ± 0.2 keV**; Ti **0.3 ± 0.2 keV → 0.9 ± 0.2 keV**. Loading adds a similar increment (~0.6–0.9 keV) in both metals, but the absolute value stays much larger in Pd.
- **The standard single-parameter screened-tunnelling model (ALR) fails**: it describes the data above ~2.5 keV and then systematically under-predicts below ~2 keV. The authors fit the deviation with a phenomenological **"bi-exponential"** form — explicitly offered as a compact description of a heterogeneous reaction environment, *not* as a physical mechanism.
- The authors' own framing of the limits: reaction rates are low, the system is *"not presently relevant for energy production,"* and the microscopic origin of the rising Ue is *"a key open question."* They propose depth-resolved hydrogen profiling and positron annihilation spectroscopy as the next tests.

**And the divergence.** The Szczecin group's interpretation is a **thermal spike**: the deuteron beam creates ion tracks in which the phonon density is high enough to push local temperatures past melting, and the fusion that follows is *thermal* — with electron screening and a ⁴He threshold resonance contributing. The Davis/LBNL group's framing is **heterogeneous screening** — the beam samples progressively more defective, more deuterium-rich near-surface material at lower energies, and the effective screening potential rises accordingly. **Both fit a plateau. Neither has excluded the other.** The Davis group says so in its own discussion: *"The results of several effects may be lumped into one parameter, the effective screening potential Ue, even though they are physically different mechanisms."*

## Confidence: high that the measurement is real; low that it settles the mechanism

**For the measurement.**

1. **Peer-reviewed in a top-tier journal** (*Nature Communications*), open access, with a full Methods section and a preprint that preceded it.
2. **Two independent laboratories, in two different countries, using two different techniques**, reproducing the same qualitative behaviour across four material systems (Zr, Pd, Ti, and Ti₀.₉₉₃Pd₀.₀₀₇).
3. **Two independent detection channels** (proton and neutron) agreeing.
4. **An explicit negative control on the headline claim:** electrochemical loading alone produced no detectable fusion products — which is exactly the check the field's history demands.
5. **The controls are stated with numbers**, not asserted: background hours, background rates, signal-to-background at each energy, PSD rejection efficiency.

**Against reading more into it.**

1. **The rates are tiny and the effect is not energy-positive.** The authors state plainly that the system is not presently relevant to energy production. An enhancement factor of 10¹⁸ over a vanishingly small number is still a small number.
2. **The mechanism is unresolved, and the two groups disagree.** A plateau with two incompatible explanations is a phenomenon, not an understanding. The plateau's *onset energy* differs too (~2 keV vs ~3 keV depending on group and material), which is exactly where a mechanism test would bite.
3. **"Effective screening potential" is doing a lot of work.** Both groups concede that a single Ue lumps physically different effects — defect trapping, deuterium density, thermal spikes, resonance. A fitted parameter that absorbs everything explains nothing.
4. **The material-specificity is thin.** Pd and Ti are chemically and electronically very different; the plateau appearing in both is offered as evidence of generality, but it also weakens any specific electronic-screening story.
5. **This is not a replication of the excess-heat claim**, and it should not be read as one. It tests a different, harder observable — fusion products rather than heat — under an ion beam the classic claim never had.

## Honest framing

We hold no brief — the test is the point.

**What is worth keeping here is not "LENR works" and not "LENR fails." It is that this is what a *hard* observable looks like, and that two independent laboratories have now converged on a shape while diverging on a cause.** The shape is a floor: below roughly 2 keV, the fusion yield stops falling. The cause is either heterogeneous electron screening in a defect-rich near-surface layer, or thermal fusion inside beam-induced ion tracks — and the two groups, both of whom have done careful work, cannot yet tell them apart. A fitted "effective screening potential" that absorbs both is a description, not an explanation.

The record also carries a methodological lesson the Yard has been accumulating all along: **the observable class decides what can be concluded.** The 1989 claim offered heat and was destroyed for it. This experiment offers fusion products — protons and neutrons, in two independent channels, with a >350-hour background and a control showing that loading alone produces nothing — and it is therefore much harder to dismiss, even though its reaction rates are tiny and it establishes no energy source whatsoever. The field re-entered not by winning the old argument but by accepting a harder one, and this is the hardest one yet.

**What would change this record:** a measurement that separates the two mechanisms — depth-resolved deuterium profiling under beam (SIMS, NRA, ERDA) or open-volume defect spectroscopy (positron annihilation) as the Davis group proposes, or an ion-track prediction that the plateau onset should scale with a material's heat capacity and thermal conductivity. A third laboratory reaching the plateau in a material neither group has tried would also move it. **A clean null — a material that shows no floor — would be just as valuable and should be filed the day it appears.**
