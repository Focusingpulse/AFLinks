# Validation — Chevreul Reproduces the Fortis Pendulum, Then Locates the Cause in the Arm

**Domain:** radiesthesia / dowsing (ideomotor effect)
**Recorded:** 2026-09-17 (Replication Seeder)

## What was claimed

That a pendulum held in a motionless hand oscillates above certain bodies — water, mercury, metals, a living being — and that interposing another body (a glass plate, a cake of resin) between the subject and the pendulum stops the oscillation; withdraw the interposed body and the motion resumes. This is the "Fortis pendulum" or *pendule explorateur* claim, attributed to Fortis and Ch. Amoretti.

## Who replicated it, when

**Michel-Eugène Chevreul (1786–1889)**, the French chemist. In 1812, at M. Deleuze's suggestion, he attempted to reproduce the effects in his own laboratory. He published the experiments in 1833 as a letter to Ampère (*Revue des Deux-Mondes*), and included the arm-support demonstration and the general conclusion in *De la baguette divinatoire* (1854).

## Method

Chevreul first reproduced the claimant's observations. Holding an iron ring on a hemp thread, he saw the oscillations appear above mercury in his pneumatic trough, above an anvil, above animals. He then reproduced the interposition effect: with a glass plate or a resin cake held between subject and pendulum, "the oscillations diminish in amplitude and annihilate themselves entirely. They began again when the intermediate body was withdrawn, and annihilated themselves anew by the interposition of the same body. This succession of phenomena repeated itself a great number of times with a truly remarkable constancy, whether the intermediate body was held by me or by another person."

Having confirmed the claimant's facts, he tested the mechanism. He **supported his right arm — the arm holding the pendulum — on a wooden support that he could advance from the shoulder toward the hand and back**:

- as the support approached the hand, the pendulum's movement **decreased**;
- when the fingers holding the thread were themselves supported, the movement **ceased**;
- reversing the support's travel **restored** the movement, though more slowly for equal distances.

He also recorded the introspective datum: while his eyes followed the pendulum he could feel "a *disposition* or *tendency to movement* which, involuntary as it seemed to me, was all the more satisfied as the pendulum described larger arcs."

## Result

Chevreul concluded that a **muscular movement occurring without his knowledge** determined the phenomenon — that is, the pendulum was moved by the operator, not by the bodies beneath it. This is the first rigorous experimental demonstration of what William Benjamin Carpenter would name the **ideomotor effect** in 1852. His general conclusion also rejected physical explanations for the divining rod on internal-consistency grounds: rod practitioners were divided into two camps over how the rod responds to matter *identical* versus *different* from the target body, and "both cannot be true at the same time; therefore the cause is not physical."

## Confidence: high for the demonstration; note the limits

The corpus holds a full English translation of the experimental chapter and the general conclusion from the 1854 Mallet-Bachelier edition (public domain), so this record traces to a primary text rather than a summary. The arm-support test is mechanically decisive and can be repeated at a bench with a stick and a pendulum. Limits: it is a **single-operator introspective study** — Chevreul is both experimenter and subject — and the 1854 conclusion is a reasoned argument, not a modern blinded trial. The controlled-trial role is filled later in the corpus by the Munich work (Wagner, Betz, Trenkle 1986–88; Enright's 1995/1999 re-analysis, which found the initially "successful" dowsers could not reproduce their own results) and by the INRS Quebec field study whose final results were presented at EGU 2026.
