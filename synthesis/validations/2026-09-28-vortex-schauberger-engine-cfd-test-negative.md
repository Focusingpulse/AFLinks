# Validation — Schauberger's Vortex Engine, Tested by CFD and a Model Boat

**Domain:** vortex / implosion technology (Schauberger lineage)
**Recorded:** 2026-09-28 (Replication Watch)

## What was claimed

**Viktor Schauberger** (1885–1958), the Austrian forest warden whose "implosion technology" is a foundational lineage for a large part of this archive's vortex material, is said to have developed a **vortex engine** around 1943 that could **both levitate and generate energy** — using the friction between vortices and the surrounding medium to force it downward (lift), and using vortex intensification to produce **more rotation out than went in**. The claim sits underneath the "implosion" branch of the water-and-vortex line the Yard carries. This domain has **dossiers but no validation record**; this is its first.

## Who tested it, when

Both tests are reported in a **2026 review** titled *"Investigation of Viktor Schauberger's vortex engine"* and described as an **"Australian Review"**, published on the site of **Infinity Turbine LLC** (a US turbine-design firm), dated **1 August 2026** and stating that the investigation ran **"a year of testing."**

**This is the weak point of the record and it is stated plainly:** the write-up is a **vendor web page**, it **names no investigators and no institution**, it contains **no dates for the experiments themselves**, and **no paper, preprint or dataset** accompanies it. It is filed here as **single-source, unconfirmed** — it earns a record because the domain has none and because a negative with a method is worth more than a claim without one, not because it is strong evidence.

## Method

Two tests, as described:

1. **Propulsion / lift test (physical).** A **model boat** on the water surface was fitted with a **reversed propeller** and a **rear shield** that redirected the flow into a **pair of vortices**. The idea under test was Schauberger's: vortices interacting by friction with the surrounding medium force it backward (water) or downward (air), producing thrust or lift. **Numerous runs** were filmed. In a refinement, a **second shield closed the bottom** of the vortex region, to test whether the motion came from the vortex-friction mechanism or from something simpler.
2. **Energy / extra-rotation test (CFD).** The same vortex configuration was modelled in **CFD** to test whether **adding and removing heat** at different points would intensify the vortices enough to produce **net extra rotation** (i.e. usable energy out). A **blade system** — which Schauberger's engine is said to have had — was then introduced to try to convert the vortex motion into rotation.

## Result — as measured

- **Lift/propulsion: negative, with the cause identified.** Initial model-boat runs showed **forward motion** — up to a **maximum efficiency of 25%** relative to ordinary propeller propulsion — which looked like a positive. On frame-by-frame film analysis, the motion was traced to **water being ejected from underneath the vortex area**, not to vortex–medium friction. Closing the vortex bottom with a **second shield**, and thus blocking that ejection, produced **"virtually no forward motion."** Conclusion: **levitation by vortex–air friction is unlikely.**
- **Extra rotation: negative.** CFD showed that **drag on the rotating wall fell** — but **not** because the vortex intensified. Adding and removing heat **did raise vortex velocity**, yet that extra kinetic energy **could not be converted into rotation** of the system; **no extra rotation was generated.** Introducing blades made the system **unstable** and air-displacing at the required speeds. Conclusion: **a vortex engine of the described type is unlikely to fly or to produce extra rotation or energy.**
- **Stated caveat, in the authors' own words:** without better information on Schauberger's actual engine, "it cannot be conclusively stated without doubt that it is impossible" — but the indications make it **theoretically unlikely**.

## Confidence: low

**Why low.**

- **The source is unstructured.** No investigators named, no institution, no dates, no paper, no numbers beyond the one efficiency figure. A vendor product page is not a laboratory record.
- **The "experiments" may not be the page-owner's own.** The heading reads "Australian Review," which suggests the page is **summarising someone else's work** rather than reporting first-hand measurement. Until the primary is located, treat the 25% figure and the CFD findings as **relayed, not witnessed**.
- **The target is underspecified, and the review admits it.** Schauberger's actual engine geometry and operating parameters are unknown; the tests model an **inferred** configuration. That cuts both ways — it is why the authors hedge — but it also means a null here is a null for *the reviewers' reconstruction* of the engine, not necessarily for Schauberger's own.

**What it does earn.** Two falsifiable claims — vortex friction can lift; heat-driven vortices can output net rotation — were **made testable and failed**, with a **specific physical explanation** for the one apparent positive (bottom ejection). A negative with a mechanism attached is worth carrying.

**The broader literature agrees, in the ordinary way.** Mainstream engineering has built **gravitational water vortex turbines** — a Schauberger-lineage idea, using a bathtub vortex in a basin with a vertical-axis runner — and it works, unremarkably. Measured power coefficients sit around **0.31** for a baseline basin (rising to ~0.42 with optimised basin-wall airfoils, in a 2026 CFD study), and a 2026 experimental-plus-numerical study of the same technology found the flow **"mainly irrotational"** and a potential-flow model accurate to within ~15% at moderate load. That is the shape of the honest answer: **a vortex can do useful work, and the amount of work is ordinary turbine efficiency — not an anomaly.** No mainstream vortex result in this Yard's reading reports net energy gain.

## Honest framing

We hold no brief — the test is the point. Recorded as **attempted / measured / reported**, and the honesty here is mostly about **the source**: a vendor web page describing two tests, with no named experimenters, no date, and no paper, is the weakest form of evidence this Yard accepts. It is filed **low-confidence and single-source** for exactly that reason, and it should be read as "someone reports that these tests were run and came back negative," not as "these tests were independently confirmed."

What makes it worth a record anyway is the **shape of the negative**. Schauberger's two claims were **made concrete enough to test** — vortex friction lifts; heat-driven vortices output net rotation — and both failed, with the one apparent success explained away by a mundane cause the experimenters found by **closing the bottom of the vortex and watching the effect vanish.** That is how a null should be reported: not "we saw nothing," but "we saw something, we found out what it actually was, and we removed it."

And the mainstream literature supplies the honest counterweight without pretending it is a replication: vortex turbines **are** built, they **do** work, and their measured power coefficients are **ordinary**. The Vortex/Implosion pavilion now has its first validation, and its message is the one the whole Yard exists to deliver — **the test is the point, and a vortex that works is not the same as a vortex that gives energy away.**
