# Validation — The Munich Dowsing Experiment, 1986–88

**Domain:** radiesthesia / dowsing (buried-target detection)
**Recorded:** 2026-09-19 (Replication Seeder)

## What was claimed

That a practised dowser can locate a buried target — specifically, a pipe carrying flowing water — at a rate better than chance, by reading the motion of a forked or L-shaped rod held in the hands.

## Who tested it, when

A research group at the **University of Munich**, commissioned by the German federal research ministry, running **1986–1988**. Named investigators in the corpus record: **Hans-Dieter Betz**, **Stefan Wagner**, **Jürgen Trenkle**. Reported budget ~DM 400,000 (~€255,000).

**Applicant pool:** ~500 self-identified dowsers applied; screening reduced this to **43** whom the researchers judged credible enough to test. **843 test trials** were run in total.

## Method

A water-carrying pipe was placed on a **cart under a false floor** in a test hall. On each trial the cart was **moved to a randomly chosen position** by the experimenters, so neither the dowser nor the person recording could know where the target was by ordinary means. The dowser walked the hall and indicated where they believed the pipe to be. Hits and misses were scored against chance.

The design's single question was global: **does dowsing beat chance, overall?**

## Result

**Reported: no effect above chance.** The corpus record states plainly that the Munich tests "did not confirm detection by rod," across the 843 trials examined.

The famous complication: **six of the 43 dowsers scored above chance** in the published analysis, and those six carried the study's afterlife for years. **Tom Napier's 1999 computer simulation** (PHACT) subsequently generated the same pattern — "six virtual dowsers" performing above chance — **from a random-number generator alone**, with no dowsing in it. That is the expected residue of selecting the best performers out of 43 on a finite number of trials, not a signal.

A further dispute sits on top of that: **J. T. Enright** (1995, 1999) argued that non-standard statistical handling had manufactured even the apparent residue; **Betz rebutted**. The SPR's Psi Encyclopedia carries the full exchange; secondary sources describe the overall results as "close to chance," with some nuance reported on individual performers.

A separate **Kassel (1991)** controlled trial, analysed by Enright, also found skilled dowsers at chance under blinded conditions.

## Confidence: high on the null; high on the simulation; medium on the dispute's residue

- The study is large, prospective, government-commissioned, and its method is documented well enough to be criticised in detail — which it was, in print, by both sides.
- The Napier simulation is the cleanest available answer to the "but six of them did better" objection: the same count falls out of pure randomness at that sample size.
- The limits are real and should be stated: the dowsers were **self-selected volunteers**, not a random sample of practitioners; and the design tested **one flat question** — global chance — so it was structurally blind to sub-effects (e.g. whether a *metal* pipe separates from a *plastic* one, the magnetic hypothesis). That blindness is exactly what the 2025–26 INRS design was built to repair (see the companion validation in this folder).

## Honest framing

We hold no brief — the test is the point. This is a **reported null from a large controlled trial**, not a proof that no one anywhere has ever found anything. What it establishes is narrower and firmer: at this scale, under blinding, with pre-screened volunteers, the effect was not there. The six-dowser coda is the more useful lesson than the null itself — it is the clearest case in the corpus of a striking-looking result dissolving when the selection is modelled. Any home test in the Replication Yard needs the same discipline: fix the number of attempts in advance and score the whole run, not the best performer in it.
