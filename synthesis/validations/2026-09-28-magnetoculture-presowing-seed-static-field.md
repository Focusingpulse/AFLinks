# Validation — Pre-Sowing Static Magnetic Field Seed Treatment and Crop Yield

**Domain:** electro-culture / magneto-culture (dry-seed exposure to a static magnetic field)
**Recorded:** 2026-09-28 (Replication Watch)

## What was claimed

That exposing **dry seeds to a static magnetic field (SMF) before sowing** — a physical, non-chemical "seed invigoration" — raises germination, early vigour and, ultimately, **field yield**. The claim is decades old (commercial magnetic seed treaters were being sold by the mid-1970s), and the underlying idea — that a static field can leave a lasting mark on living tissue — is the same idea behind the magnetized-water and electro-culture lines already in this Yard.

This is a **different sub-claim** from the records already here. Magneto-culture in this Yard has so far meant **magnetized irrigation water** (`2026-09-18-magnetized-water-caraway-yield.md`) and **in-growth electric fields** (`2026-09-16-electroculture-constant-field-null-wheat-mustard.md`, `2026-09-25-electroculture-hvef-tomato-shanxi.md`). Treating the *seed* before it is ever planted is a separate protocol, and this record is its first entry.

## Who tested it, when

- **Field trial, 2026 (positive).** **J. L. Ristić-Djurović** and colleagues at the **Institute of Physics Belgrade** (National Institute of the Republic of Serbia, University of Belgrade), with the **Institute for Biological Research "Siniša Stanković"** and the **Institute of Meat Hygiene and Technology**, Belgrade, Serbia. Published in *European Physical Journal Special Topics*, published online **21 December 2025**.
- **Field trial, 1975/76 (null).** **Harley J. Otto, Joni Ford, W. E. Leuschen, L. J. Smith and Dennis D. Warnes**, **Minnesota Agricultural Experiment Station**, trials at **Waseca, Lamberton, Morris and Crookston**.
- **Lab trial, 2026 (positive, germination only).** Soybean germination study, *Scientific Reports* (2026).

## Method

**Belgrade (the new test).** Cabbage (*Brassica oleracea* var. *capitata* f. *alba*). Seeds exposed **one hour** pre-sowing to a **highly homogeneous static field** at **50, 100 and 350 mT**, then followed **from sowing to harvest** in an agricultural field — assessed at the **transplant stage (~2 months)** and the **mature stage (~3.5 months)**. The authors' stated reason for the field setting is that the majority of this literature is **laboratory-only**; their point is to test whether the effect survives contact with a real crop season. Multiple field strengths, unexposed controls, quantified yield and biomass endpoints.

**Minnesota (the historical test).** Wheat, oats, barley, soybeans and corn treated with an **"Enagizer" magnetic seed treater** exactly per the manufacturer's instructions (energy meter at maximum), planted within four days of treatment, across **four locations**. Design was a **randomised block, split-plot**, with **treated and untreated seed of the same lot always located together** — a design built to kill location and lot effects — with **four replications** per trial. Yields were harvested from the two centre rows of each plot.

**Soybean lab (2026).** Four cultivars, **250 and 500 mT for 3 and 12 min**; germination and early growth only — controlled laboratory conditions.

## Result — as measured

- **Belgrade, field (positive, modest).** At the **mature stage**, seed treatment with **350 mT and 100 mT** produced statistically significant increases in plant weight and head weight. At **350 mT**: plant weight **+22.7%**, head weight **+24.8%** vs untreated control. At **100 mT**: **+16.9%** and **+18.3%**. At the **transplant stage**, **100 mT** gave **+21.5%** leaves and **+30.7%** plant weight. The authors call 1 h at **350 mT** the most effective of the three treatments.
- **Minnesota, field (null).** **"No significant advantage for magnetic seed treatment."** Side-by-side yields, treated vs untreated (bu/ac): wheat **40.6 / 40.7**, barley **56.9 / 57.9**, oats **78.1 / 78.7**, soybeans **43.3 / 42.6**, corn **114.4 / 116.0**. Location- and variety-specific tables likewise showed no consistent benefit.
- **Soybean, lab (positive, germination only).** Seed Vigour Index up **77.9%** (cv. Adessa) and **74.4%** (cv. Adelfia) at **500 mT for 3 min**; **shorter exposures (3 min) beat longer (12 min)** across all genotypes; response was **genotype-dependent**.

## Confidence: medium that the 2026 cabbage field trial measured what it reports; low that the general claim holds

**Why medium on the specific result.** Peer-reviewed journal; **field** rather than chamber conditions; three field strengths; quantified, statistically tested yield and biomass endpoints. That is the strongest form this claim has yet been given.

**Why the general claim stays low.**

- **One site, one season, one cultivar.** The Belgrade gain is a single field experiment. There is no second site and no second season in the record, so it cannot yet separate "magnetic seed treatment works" from "this field, this year, this cultivar."
- **The historical field evidence is null, not mixed.** The 1975 Minnesota trial is the closest thing this literature has to a **large multi-crop, multi-location field test** — five crops, four sites, four replications, treated and untreated seed of the same lot adjacent — and it found **nothing**. A single-site positive does not retire a multi-site null; it has to out-replicate it.
- **The effect sizes across the modern literature are wildly dispersed.** ~**25%** here (field, cabbage); ~**78%** on a lab germination index (soybean); and reported field gains up to roughly **200%** in a 2026 safflower priming study. A claim whose effect size wanders by an order of magnitude across labs usually signals an **uncontrolled variable**, not a strong effect. Dispersed magnitude is itself the finding.
- **No mechanism.** There is no accepted account of how a **one-hour static field exposure** alters a plant's growth **months later**. The dose–response is explicitly **non-linear** in this literature (both here and in the soybean study, *shorter* exposure outperformed longer), which means a home replicator guessing at field strength and duration has no principled place to stand.
- **The treated-seed handling is uniform.** All of these trials treat seed the same day or within a few days and sow soon after; none of them test how long the putative mark persists.

## Honest framing

We hold no brief — the test is the point. Recorded as **attempted / measured / reported**: a Belgrade group did the useful thing and moved the pre-sowing magnetic-field claim out of the growth chamber and into a cabbage field, and measured a **real, modest, statistically significant gain** — about a quarter more head weight at 350 mT — under the conditions it describes. That is a genuine positive and it goes in the Yard as one.

It is filed **low** on the general claim, and the reason is not scepticism about the measurement; it is the **standing historical null**. The single most rigorous field test of this claim that has been run — five crops, four Minnesota locations, four replications, treated and untreated seed of the same lot side by side — found **no advantage at all**, and it has never been out-replicated. So the honest summary is not "magnetic seed treatment works" and not "it doesn't." It is: **one site, one season, one cultivar now shows a quarter more yield; the best multi-site field trial on record shows zero.** The next useful experiment is not another positive; it is a **second site, second season, same protocol** — and the control every replication in this line should carry is **sham-treated seed** (same handling, field switched off), not untreated seed, because handling and sowing date are confounded with treatment in most of this literature.

The transferable lesson this Yard keeps meeting: **a dispersed effect size is a claim about the experiment, not about the world.** When a field trial reports ~25% and a lab reports ~78% and another field trial reports ~200%, the interesting number is not any of them — it is the spread.
