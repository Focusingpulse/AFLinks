## The honest framing (the spine of the card)

The card tests a claim, not a tradition. Nobody is being asked to prove that magnets heal anything. They are being asked one narrow question: **does a 20 mT static field on your feet and ears change your blood pressure more than an identical-looking piece of rubber does?**

Three things the card keeps in front of the family:

1. **The source's own control arm fell too.** Systolic pressure dropped 6.8 mmHg with the magnets and 3.7 mmHg with the sham. Sitting still for twenty-five minutes lowers blood pressure. The claim is the *gap between two falls*, which is why the card is **paired** — the same person on a magnet day and a sham day — and why the report is a **difference of differences**, never a before-and-after.
2. **The source supplies the blinding, and the card must not lose it.** The sham is the same rubber with the magnet removed; an assistant coded the arm; neither the reader nor the subject knew. A home version that lets the reader see which pads are in use has thrown away the one thing that made the source's result worth replicating.
3. **The geomagnetic claim is a second, separate question.** The source says the size of the response depends on the day's magnetosphere. Logging the daily index costs nothing and turns a two-arm test into a three-way one — but a family that finds no magnet effect and no geomagnetic relationship has answered both questions cleanly.

**Under-promise, stated plainly:** the likely outcome is that **the magnet day and the sham day fall about the same**, that the difference sits inside the cuff's own noise floor, and that a 20 mT sheet of rubber does nothing measurable to a blood pressure. That is the mainstream prediction and the card is built to reach it cleanly. The one thing the card establishes whatever the result is the family's own **measured cuff noise floor** — the number below which no blood-pressure difference in that house means anything — which is worth having whether or not anything moves.
