## The honest framing (the spine of the card)

The card tests a claim, not a tradition. The magnetized-water lineage is not being asked to prove anything — it is being asked what it says the treated water does to a plant, and that answer is countable in a garden in one season.

Three things the card keeps in front of the family:

1. **Both accounts predict a garden — that is why the controls are necessary.** "The magnets changed the water" and "the beds differ for ordinary reasons" both put a grown plant in front of you. Only the **matched beds**, the **matched water volume**, the **sham treater**, and the **treater swap** separate them.
2. **The expected result is the null, and that is a complete result.** The card says so before the family starts. The report's own plants material is commercial-cultivation testimony gathered across several treater types, and its own authors ask for a more unified agronomic approach; the review's own authors list standardisation, mechanism and long-term validation as open. A clean "the beds performed the same" outcome is a genuine finding — exactly what the "Skeptic's Star" honours.
3. **A positive would be genuinely interesting and would need to repeat.** A magnetized bed that out-yields its matched control across two crops, with the sham performing like the control and the effect following the treater when swapped, would be a real anomaly — and it would justify the taller follow-up (a pre-registered multi-season run with matched nutrient analysis, which is what the Immokalee trial's nutrient finding would predict).

**Under-promise, stated plainly:** the likely outcome is that the two beds are indistinguishable, and that any difference seen in the first season is explained by position, water volume, or weather. That is the honest verdict, and the card is designed to reach it cleanly.
