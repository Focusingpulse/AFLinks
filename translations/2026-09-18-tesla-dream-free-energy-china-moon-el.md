China wants to make Tesla's dream of free energy a reality

Nikola Tesla envisioned a world where electrical energy could be transmitted wirelessly, without cables.

His vision never became a reality on Earth, but Chinese scientists believe it may find application in a very different place: the Moon.

Researchers from the Harbin Institute of Technology propose a new system that could send energy to lunar vehicles via laser beams, allowing them to explore regions of the Moon where sunlight never reaches.

The plan concerns mainly the South Pole of the Moon, an area considered particularly important for future missions. There, some peaks receive almost continuous sunlight, while nearby craters lie in permanent darkness and most likely contain valuable frozen water.

The problem is that the rovers that would explore these dark craters cannot use solar panels, while their batteries may not be sufficient for long missions.

The solution the scientists propose is a laser-based energy transmission system. Stations placed in illuminated areas would collect solar energy and convert it into laser beams, which would send energy to the lunar vehicles. There, special receivers would convert the light back into electrical energy.

To design the network, the researchers used data from NASA's Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA), an instrument that precisely maps the Moon's surface. With this data they studied the area around Shackleton crater, one of the most important targets for future missions.

The simulations showed that such a network could allow the rovers to travel greater distances without needing large, heavy batteries. The system managed to transmit energy over a distance of about 5 kilometers — enough to support the operation of vehicles within the permanently dark regions.

The scientists are also examining the possibility that the same laser beams could be used not only for energy, but also for laser communications, simultaneously enabling the transfer of data from the lunar missions.

The proposal comes at a time when NASA, with the Artemis program, and China, with the Chang'e missions, are pursuing a more permanent presence on the Moon, with the South Pole as a key target — because of the frozen water and the particular lighting conditions there, they say.

If this technology becomes a reality, the Moon may be the first place where Nikola Tesla's idea of wireless energy transmission passes from theory into practice. Perhaps, in the end, the future of cable-free energy will begin not on Earth, but on the surface of the Moon.
