# Goethe's Scientific Works - Chunk 136

Response to a question

You see, truth has the power to evoke and kindle, in feeling, beauty and, in willing, goodness (morality). A world permeated by truth ("The truth shall make you free") would therefore be different and better. If only those who claim to fight for a "better world" without fighting for truth would understand this!

Certainly, speaking of truth to someone immersed, like all of us, in error and falsehood, is a bit like explaining the physiology of the eyes, as nearsighted or farsighted, to other nearsighted or farsighted people. But that is not what matters! What matters rather is realizing that our eyes could and should see otherwise, and that it is not true-as the obstructionists would have us believe-that they cannot be, by nature, anything but nearsighted or farsighted.

Steiner writes: "The most widespread opinion on this matter is that the concept is only a means, belonging to consciousness, by which consciousness takes possession of the data of reality. The essence of reality would lie in the in-itself of things themselves; so that even if we were able to truly reach the foundation of things, we could nevertheless take possession only of a conceptual image of it and never of the foundation itself. Here, therefore, two completely separate worlds are presupposed: the objective external world, which carries within itself its essence, the foundations of its existence, and the subjective-ideal inner world, which would be a conceptual image of the external world. The latter is completely indifferent to objectivity, is not required by it, exists only for the knowing human being. The congruence of these two worlds would be the epistemological ideal of this fundamental opinion" (p. 105).

The two "presupposed" worlds are, of course, the "external" (objective) world of perceptual images and the "internal" (subjective) world of representations.

The third, the "external of the internal" world of concepts, insofar as it is reduced to a formal world, is in fact inserted and understood within the ("internal") world of representations, and thereby subjectivized.

"The congruence of these two worlds," says Steiner, "would be the epistemological ideal of this fundamental opinion." This ideal can, however, be realized by pursuing not the "congruence" of the "external" (physical) world with the "internal" (psychic) one, but rather the "congruence," in the "internal" (psychic) world, of the "external" (physical) world with the "external of the internal" (spiritual) world; this latter-as we have said-is nothing other than the "internal of the external" world, and therefore the spiritual essence of the physical world.

The conceptual image, Steiner further says, "is completely indifferent to objectivity, is not required by it, exists only for the knowing human being." But even "the knowing human being" is part of the world (of "objectivity") and is "world." Therefore, if we consider that the world has arrived at creating a being through which it can become conscious of itself, it is indeed difficult to think that human knowing is "completely indifferent to objectivity."
