# André Simoneton — Radiovitality: Food Radiations and Human Waves

*English dossier by Forge (translation-qc agent), 2026-09-15. Composite translation of French sources: Trédaniel author page, mrbienetre.fr, Inexploré, with psi-gamma.com for the scale's lineage. Original books (Radiovitalité des aliments 1949; Radiations des aliments, ondes humaines et santé 1971/1990) are out of print — this dossier assembles the fetchable record.*

## The man

André Simoneton was born in Paris in 1893 and died in Lagny-sur-Marne in 1983, at age 90. His formation is a striking detail of the lineage this corpus documents: a young electrical engineer in August 1914, he was recruited by **Captain Ferrié** — the founder of French military radio (T.S.F., télégraphie sans fil) — who was seeking young mobilized engineers to initiate them into the new science of wireless telegraphy. Simoneton's radiesthesia of food is thus not folk tradition but radio engineering turned onto the living: the same man who learned to measure electromagnetic waves at the birth of French radio later claimed to measure the waves of foods and human bodies.

He spent his career in the agri-food industry, and Trédaniel presents him as "a true pioneer" of the study of the intrinsic quality of foods and their impact on health — "as a citizen wary of the practices of the agri-food industrialists." His two books:

- **Radiovitalité des aliments — hypothèses sur la vie et la santé** (1949)
- **Radiations des aliments, ondes humaines et santé** (1971; 3rd revised edition, Courrier du livre, 1990, 295 p., collection "Méthodes et techniques de la vie saine", ISBN 978-2-7029-0058-1 — currently out of print at Trédaniel)

## The doctrine: radiovitality

The premise: everything in nature emits waves. Every particle vibrates; therefore every thing "radiates." Simoneton took up the work of **André Bovis** (1871–1947), the Nice dowser who built the biometer — a graduated rule with a witness cup, first used to grade the quality of cheese and wine casks — and the associated scale on which a healthy person reads about 6,500 (in "Bovis units" or, per Simoneton, angstroms).

In the 1930s, Simoneton — an expert in electromagnetism — fell ill, and by his own account recovered his vitality by eating foods measured at the highest vibratory frequencies on the Bovis scale. He then undertook systematic studies of food vitality and its impact on human energy levels, coining the term **radiovitalité** (radio-vitality).

The human reference points, as he stated them:

- Human cells vibrate at a wavelength between **6,200 and 7,000 Å**
- A healthy person maintains **6,500 Å or more**
- A person whose vibratory rate falls below 6,200 Å has a greater chance of declaring illness
- Eating foods at 6,500 Å and above raises one's vibratory rate; eating below it lowers it

## The four food categories

Simoneton measured foods' vibratory frequencies by the radiesthetic method (pendulum/biotensor — a method with no scientific basis by current standards, as even his sympathetic modern commentators note) and classified them into four categories:

1. **Superior foods — 6,500 to 10,000 Å.** Fresh, ripe fruits and vegetables. These sit above the 6,500 Å reference of good health and raise the consumer's vibratory rate.
2. **Support foods — 4,000 to 6,000 Å.** To be eaten in moderation; to be avoided in case of illness.
3. **Inferior foods — 1,000 to 3,000 Å.** Their frequency sits below that of a living human body; described as "empty of vitamins," to be avoided by anyone not in optimal health. Cooked meats, refined products (white pasta, white sugar) fall here.
4. **Dead foods — 0 Å.** Emitting no life radiation at all; presented by Simoneton as responsible for a large share of modern disease.

The governing variable is **freshness and maturity**: the fresher and closer to natural ripeness a food is, the higher its wavelength. The same food migrates between categories with processing — a ripe organic fruit reads high; the same fruit canned or refined reads low or dead.

## The lineage: Bovis → Simoneton → Merz

The Bovis scale itself is attributed to André Bovis through his grandson Jacques Bovis (the self-designated "geobiologist" who publicized it). Simoneton developed the concept (1949) and introduced the term radio-vitality, extending the scale from places (cosmo-telluric energy) to **foods and the human body**. Simoneton's scale was in turn developed into the "modern Bovis scale" by the Swiss geobiologist **Blanche Merz** (1919–2002) — former Vaud cantonal parliament member, founder of the Institut de recherches en géobiologie at Chardonne in 1979 — whose books (Hauts-Lieux Cosmo-Télluriques 1983, translated as Points of Cosmic Energy, 1987) carried the scale through French- and German-speaking Europe.

In modern dowsing practice the chain is seamless: 6,500 Bovis units is the "neutral" reading of a healthy person; religious buildings read 8,000–9,000, privileged sites 13,000 and above; Hartmann knots and underground water streams read around 3,000; pork meat 2,000–4,500; computers and mobile phones 2,000–3,000. The unit itself has no physical definition — the "measurement" is the dowser's declared number.

## Why this dossier matters for the corpus

Simoneton is the **food-radiations branch of the French radiesthesia tradition** this library documents: Bovis (biometer) → Simoneton (radiovitality, foods) → Merz (modern Bovis scale, places). He is also the tradition's clearest case of an engineer migrating from radio science to subtle-energy claims — the Ferrié/TSF formation makes him a data point for Sandra's "bioenergy has layers beyond plain electricity" thesis: here the claim is precisely that *life* has a measurable wavelength, and that food quality is a wavelength question, not a calorie question.

Trédaniel's own framing points at the falsifiable seam: Simoneton "anticipated measurement methods since developed by emerging scientific disciplines — biophotonics and photobiology" (polyphenol and pesticide measurement in cereals and fruit; optical systems like biochips, infrared sensors, fluorometric techniques). The modern instrumentation exists; what was never done is the controlled comparison — Simoneton's angstrom readings versus, say, delayed-luminescence biophoton measurements on the same foods. That experiment is the natural next claim in this lane.
