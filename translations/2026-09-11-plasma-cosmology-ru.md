Plasma Cosmology — Plasma cosmology

A non-standard model of the universe; emphasizes the role of ionized gases

Hannes Alfvén proposed that laboratory scaling results could be extrapolated to the scale of the Universe. A scaling jump of 10^9 times was required to extrapolate to the magnetosphere, a second jump to extrapolate to galactic conditions, and a third jump to extrapolate to the Hubble distance.

Plasma cosmology is a non-standard cosmology whose central postulate is that the dynamics of ionized gases and plasma plays an important, if not dominant, role in the physics of the Universe beyond the Solar System. In contrast, current observations and models from cosmologists and astrophysicists explain the formation, development, and evolution of astronomical bodies and large-scale structures in the Universe as being under the influence of gravity (including Albert Einstein's formulation of general relativity) and baryonic physics.

Some theoretical concepts about plasma cosmology originated with Hannes Alfvén, who preliminarily proposed using plasma scaling to extrapolate the results of laboratory experiments and observations of plasma physics, scaling them by many orders of magnitude up to the largest observable objects in the Universe (see box).

Cosmologists and astrophysicists who have evaluated plasma cosmology reject it because it does not match observations of astrophysical phenomena, nor modern cosmological theory. Since the mid-1990s, very few papers supporting plasma cosmology have appeared in the literature.

The term "plasma universe" is sometimes used as a synonym for plasma cosmology, as an alternative description of plasma in the Universe.

Alfvén–Klein Cosmology

In the 1960s, the theory of plasma cosmology was presented by Alfvén, a plasma expert who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1970 for his work on magnetohydrodynamics. In 1971, Swedish theoretical physicist Oskar Klein extended previous proposals and developed the Alfvén–Klein model of the Universe, or "metagalaxy," an earlier term used to designate the empirically accessible part of the Universe, rather than the entire Universe, including parts beyond our particle horizon. In this Alfvén–Klein cosmology, sometimes called Klein–Alfvén cosmology, the Universe consists of equal amounts of matter and antimatter, with the boundaries between matter and antimatter regions delineated by cosmic electromagnetic fields formed by double layers—thin regions containing two parallel layers with opposite electric charge. The interaction between these boundary regions would generate radiation, and this would lead to plasma formation. Alfvén introduced the term "ambi-plasma" to denote plasma consisting of matter and antimatter, and thus double layers are formed by ambi-plasma. According to Alfvén, such ambi-plasma would be relatively long-lived because the constituent particles and antiparticles would be too hot and have too low density to rapidly annihilate each other. Double layers would reflect clouds of the opposite type, but merge clouds of the same type, creating increasingly large regions of matter and antimatter. The idea of ambi-plasma was further developed in the forms of heavy ambi-plasma (proton-antiproton) and light ambi-plasma (electron-positron).

Alfvén–Klein cosmology was proposed partly to explain the observed baryon asymmetry in the Universe, starting from an initial condition of exact symmetry between matter and antimatter. According to Alfvén and Klein, ambi-plasma naturally forms pockets of matter and antimatter that expand outward as annihilation between matter and antimatter occurs in the double layer at the boundaries. They concluded that we must simply live in one of the pockets that consisted mainly of baryons rather than antibaryons, which explains baryon asymmetry. The pockets or bubbles of matter or antimatter would expand due to annihilation at the boundaries, which Alfvén saw as a possible explanation for the observed expansion of the Universe, which would be simply a local phase of a much more extensive history. Alfvén postulated that the Universe has always existed due to arguments from causality and rejection of ex nihilo models, such as the Big Bang, as a hidden form of creationism. The explosive double layer was also proposed by Alfvén as a possible mechanism for generating cosmic rays, X-ray bursts, and gamma-ray bursts.

In 1993, theoretical cosmologist Jim Peebles criticized Alfvén–Klein cosmology, writing that "there is no way that the results can be reconciled with the isotropy of the cosmic microwave background radiation and the X-ray background." In his book, he also showed that Alfvén's models do not predict Hubble's law, the abundance of light elements, or the existence of the cosmic microwave background. Another difficulty with the ambi-plasma model is that matter-antimatter annihilation results in the production of high-energy photons, which are not observed in the predicted quantities. Although it is possible that the local cell where "matter dominates" is simply larger than the observable Universe, this assumption is not observationally testable.

Plasma Cosmology and the Study of Galaxies

From the 1960s to the 1980s, Hannes Alfvén argued that plasma plays an important, if not dominant, role in the Universe because electromagnetic forces are much more important than gravity when acting on interplanetary and interstellar charged particles. He also suggested that they could contribute to the compression of interstellar clouds and might even constitute the main compression mechanism initiating star formation. The current standard view is that magnetic fields can prevent collapse, that large-scale Birkeland currents have not been observed, and that the length scale for charge neutrality is predicted to be much smaller than the relevant cosmological scales.

In the 1980s and 1990s, Alfvén and Anthony Peratt, a plasma physicist at Los Alamos National Laboratory, outlined a program they called the "plasma universe." In the plasma universe proposals, various plasma physics phenomena were linked to astrophysical observations and used to explain contemporary mysteries and problems in astrophysics from the 1980s and 1990s. In various forums, Peratt presented what he characterized as an alternative to the mainstream models applied in astrophysics and cosmology.

For example, Peratt suggested that the mainstream approach to galactic dynamics, based on gravitational modeling of stars and gas in galaxies with the addition of dark matter, neglects a possibly important contribution from plasma physics. He mentions laboratory experiments by Winston H. Bostick in the 1950s, in which plasma discharges resembling galaxies were created. Peratt conducted computer simulations of colliding plasma clouds, which he reported also simulated galaxy shapes. Peratt suggested that galaxies formed from the merging of plasma filaments in a z-pinch, starting 300,000 light years apart and carrying Birkeland currents of 10^18 amperes. Peratt also reported simulations he conducted showing jets of material emerging from a central buffer region, which he compared to quasars and active galactic nuclei arising without supermassive black holes. Peratt proposed a galaxy evolution sequence: "transition of double radio galaxies to radio quasars to radio-quiet QSOs with peculiar and Seyfert galaxies, ending in spiral galaxies." He also reported that flat galactic rotation curves were modeled without using dark matter. At the same time, Eric Lerner, an independent plasma researcher and supporter of Peratt's ideas, proposed a plasma model of quasars based on a dense plasma focus.

Comparison with Mainstream Astrophysics

Standard astronomical modeling and theories attempt to incorporate all known physics in descriptions and explanations of observed phenomena, with gravity playing a dominant role at the largest scales, as well as in celestial mechanics and dynamics. To this end, both Keplerian orbits and Albert Einstein's general relativity are typically used as foundational frameworks for modeling astrophysical systems and structure formation, while high-energy astronomy and particle physics in cosmology additionally appeal to electromagnetic processes, including plasma physics and radiation transport, to explain relatively small energetic processes observed in X-rays and gamma rays. Due to overall charge neutrality, plasma physics does not provide very long-range interactions in astrophysics, even while most matter in the Universe is plasma. (See "Astrophysical Plasma" for more.)

Proponents of plasma cosmology claim that electrodynamics is as important as gravity in explaining the structure of the Universe, and suggest that it provides an alternative explanation for galaxy evolution and the initial collapse of interstellar clouds. In particular, it is claimed that plasma cosmology provides an alternative explanation for the flat rotation curves of spiral galaxies and eliminates the need for dark matter in galaxies and the need for supermassive black holes at galaxy centers to power quasars and active galactic nuclei. However, theoretical analysis shows that "many scenarios for generating seed magnetic fields that rely on the survival and stability of currents at early times [are disfavored by the universe]," i.e., Birkeland currents of the necessary magnitude (10^18 amperes over megaparsec scales) for galaxy formation do not exist. Furthermore, many of the problems that were mysterious in the 1980s and 1990s, including discrepancies related to the cosmic microwave background and the nature of quasars, have been solved with more evidence that in detail provides distance and timescales for the Universe.

Some of the places where plasma cosmology proponents most diverge from standard explanations include the need for their models to produce light elements without Big Bang nucleosynthesis, which in the context of Alfvén–Klein cosmology has been shown to produce excessive amounts of X-rays and gamma rays beyond what is observed. Plasma cosmology proponents have advanced additional proposals to explain the abundance of light elements, but the associated problems have not been fully resolved. In 1995, Eric Lerner published his alternative explanation of the cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR). He claimed that his model explains the fidelity of the relic radiation spectrum to the black-body spectrum and the low level of detected anisotropy, even though the 1:10^5 level of isotropy is not explained with such precision by any alternative models. Furthermore, the sensitivity and resolution of CMB anisotropy measurement have been significantly improved by WMAP and the Planck satellite, and the signal statistics so matched the predictions of the Big Bang model that the relic radiation was declared the chief confirmation of the Big Bang model to the detriment of alternatives. The acoustic peaks in the early Universe match with high precision the predictions of the Big Bang model, and to date, there has never been an attempt to explain the detailed anisotropy spectrum within plasma cosmology or any other alternative cosmological model.
