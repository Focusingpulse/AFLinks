## The Abbé Mermet

*From ebookesoterique.com, "L'Abbé Mermet":*

Many priests have studied and practiced radiesthesia: Father Bourdoux, a former missionary; Brother Padey; Father Marie-Bernard; Father Werle; Father Bouly — and above all Father Mermet, perhaps the most celebrated of them all.

Abbé Alexis Mermet, born November 12, 1866 at Les Ollières (Haute-Savoie) and died September 7, 1937 at Jussy (canton of Geneva), was a French Catholic priest, active in Haute-Savoie before carrying his work on to Switzerland. He is particularly renowned and widely recognized as a dowser (sourcier) and radiesthesist, fields in which he acquired notable popularity and genuine expertise.

Son and grandson of a dowser, Abbé Mermet in turn practiced this discipline with passion and great mastery, thus carrying on a remarkable family tradition.

His renown in this field dates back to his participation in a dowsing competition organized as part of the Second Congress of Experimental Psychology held in Paris in March 1913. At the close of that congress, he took part in a fascinating radiesthesia experiment whose extraordinary results did not fail to astonish the eminent Armand Viré.

After the First World War, he was regularly called upon to search for springs, mineral deposits, and even missing persons. He invented "télé-radiesthésie" (remote radiesthesia), intended to apply to searches at great distance. This innovative technique, though received with skepticism by some, attracted growing interest among esotericism enthusiasts and scientists curious about unexplained phenomena.

Relying on objects connected to a target or a person, télé-radiesthésie was explored for varied uses, such as locating natural resources or detecting human activity at a distance. Its potential applications, though controversial, continue to fuel discussion and experimentation around the human capacities that might transcend conventional limits.

In 1932 he published a book setting out his methods: *Comment j'opère pour découvrir les sources et tous autres objets cachés de près ou de loin* ("How I Operate to Discover Springs and All Other Hidden Objects, Near or Far"). This book went through several reprints. Another notable contribution of his work is the emphasis he placed on the importance of mental concentration and intuition in the practice of his art.

Mermet held that success in detecting springs or hidden objects did not rest exclusively on the tools used, such as the pendulum, but also on the operator's state of mind and personal connection with his environment. This novel idea influenced many practitioners and researchers who adopted a more holistic, introspective approach to radiesthesia.

Abbé Mermet served as president of the Association des Amis de la Radiesthésie (AAR, Association of the Friends of Radiesthesia). This association played an essential role in promoting and spreading knowledge of radiesthesia, bringing together enthusiasts and researchers who shared their experiences and their work in this field. Another remarkable side of Abbé Mermet was his ability to inspire and pass on his knowledge.

Beyond his activities at the AAR and the publication of his book, he gave lectures that helped popularize radiesthesia and spark keen interest in the discipline. His teachings remain a reference for amateurs and experts in radiesthesia throughout the world.

With the permission of his superiors, this priest carried out prospecting surveys of every kind: water, minerals, medical diagnoses, searches for missing persons, on site or at a distance.

He was even invited to assist, in Rome, with excavations undertaken under the supervision of a prelate, Monsignor Belvederi, a member of the Pontifical Society of Archaeology.

### His works

- *Comment j'opère* ("How I Operate") — his classic manual, several editions, translated into English.

## A Conference by Abbé Mermet on the Pendulum

*From voyance-officielle.com, "L'Abbé Mermet « Prince des souciers »":*

Mr. Delâtre, secretary-general of the "Amis de la Radiesthésie," presents to the audience Father Mermet, who has just been elected president of the Society. With his healthy, good-natured face and his eyes shining with intelligence behind tortoiseshell spectacles, Abbé Mermet is likable at first sight. He wins over his audience at once. He begins with a tribute to the Society of the Friends of Radiesthesia, founded by Abbé Bouly, parish priest of Hardelot (Pas-de-Calais), with successive presidents the Viscount Henry de France and Mr. Armand Viré. The Society now numbers more than a thousand members, spread across 26 countries. He recalls the first public experiments attempted at the Jardin des Plantes in 1913. The pendulum was scarcely in use then; he was the only one to have one — and it was... his watch hanging from its chain! "In that distant time, the learned doctors called us quacks — we have come a long way since then!"

### Who can become a dowser?

In that era, the word "sourcellerie" (dowser's craft), which made the public smile a little, was replaced by the word "radiesthesia," which has far more style. The study of the vibratory manifestations of bodies now interests the general public, and according to Abbé Mermet, there are far more dowsers — pardon, radiesthesists — than one believes. Out of 100 people, 60 succeed on the first try at working a rod or a pendulum, much to their own surprise. Another 20 need a few exercises, and the last die-hards, the least gifted, will also succeed provided they are well advised. "Ladies and gentlemen, you are all dowsers. If you want to test your aptitude, ask an 'initiate' to examine you. With one hand he will lightly touch your solar plexus, with the other he will hold his pendulum. If the pendulum gives between 18 and 40 oscillations, you are fit for radiesthesia. Certain moral qualities are also needed, much judgment and prudence, for an apprentice dowser can be as harmful as an apprentice driver."

### The pendulum says far more than a simple YES or NO

Abbé Mermet is not absolutely a partisan of the wave-capture apparatuses. The pendulum and the rod, skillfully employed, are the best instruments. The pendulum above all, with the infinite variety of its movements, speaks a very precise language to those who know how to hear it. Yes, at first glance one thinks the pendulum answers YES or NO, or else "I don't know," but that is wrong. One can work with the pendulum and obtain unlimited answers to questions that call for something other than YES or NO. A few examples: it allows one to make incursions into the ground without drilling. As the operator's "mental ray" descends through the successive layers of the soil, the oscillations of the pendulum report on their nature. A particularly interesting detail: it is known that the City of Paris lacks drinking water, and that millions are about to be spent bringing water from the valleys of the Loire. Well, Abbé Mermet says he has discovered, in the suburbs, less than 30 kilometers from the walls of Paris and at a depth of some 800 meters, an excellent spring yielding a million liters a minute. More than enough to quench the thirst of all Parisians, who will gladly give up their Seine water flavored with bleach or ozone. In the same vein, a fairly deep borehole would, it is said, reach an important pocket of oil that would allow France to do without foreign imports for many years. Think of the billions saved! A word to our financiers! With his charming good nature, smiling at finding all this perfectly natural, Abbé Mermet recounts the astonishing results he obtained in his searches for gold, coal, and various deposits, and in his medical diagnoses. The pendulum stirs in a special manner in the presence of a diseased organ. The wavelength of the illness is added to that of the same organ in good health, and this makes it possible to locate, with great precision, the seat of the malady, its severity, and often its origin.

### Examples of the pendulum's power

"Monsieur l'abbé," asked a young man, despairing over the onset of baldness, "couldn't you make my hair grow back?" The pendulum begins to dance, and the abbé, very sure of himself, replies: "Certainly, my good friend — treat your kidneys, they are not at all well!" Two months later the young man returned with a full head of hair. Without knowing medicine, Abbé Mermet works marvelous cures, whose beneficiaries are sometimes doctors themselves. One of them had a child in the most pitiful state. The poor baby could tolerate no food, and medicines had no effect: "Thanks to the oscillations of my pendulum in the presence of the little patient, I was able to find the exact proportion of water and milk that suited his fragile stomach. After a few weeks, the child had become splendid." People often set traps for him: "Monsieur l'abbé, am I ill?" — "No, you are in fine fettle!" — "Surely I have nothing too much... or too little?" — "Ah, yes, you are missing something: your appendix!" — "It's true, Monsieur l'abbé, I was operated on ten years ago!" The conference ends amid enthusiasm. The next day, the Geneva express carries the prince of dowsers back to his little presbytery at Jussy.

## The "Prince of Dowsers"

*From guerisseur-radiesthesiste.fr (Jean-Claude Maes), "Abbé Mermet 'Prince des Sourciers'":*

Son and grandson of dowsers renowned in Savoie, Alexis Mermet was initiated from childhood into the art of the dowser's craft.

Ordained a priest in 1890, he soon settled in Protestant French-speaking Switzerland, where, with the encouragement of his superiors, he founded several Catholic parishes which he enabled to benefit from the resources his radiesthesia brought him. First curé of Le Landeron, then curé of Saint-Prex from 1922 to 1933, he spent the end of his life at Jussy, near Geneva.

The breadth of his knowledge and the importance of his radiesthetic successes earned Abbé Mermet the amply deserved sobriquet "Prince of Dowsers." His reputation was known worldwide; his field surveys were of quite remarkable precision. It was he who, through 150 meters of solid rock, revealed the existence of a whole series of galleries at the Gouffre de Lacave caves, in the Lot.

One would never finish listing his successes in extremely varied fields: discoveries of mineral-water springs, of metals, of missing persons, and so on. He was among the first to practice remote prospecting, with excellent results.

A practitioner above all, grand theories made him smile. "In radiesthesia, some find objects, others discover explanations," he would say. "Far more convenient!" Printed in thousands of copies, his book *Comment j'opère* ("How I Operate") recounts the multiplicity of his experiments over the years and the way he went about his work. Translated into English, it made the famous abbé's methods known well beyond the borders of Europe.

A pioneer of radiesthesia, Abbé Mermet will surely remain in its history, as depicted in an article in *Le Pèlerin* published at the time of his death: "Who has not heard tell of this astonishing man? His art bordered on the prodigious. Anyone who never saw him pass by, at a great stride, cassock flying, eye triumphant and his dowser's pendulum swung at arm's length like a censer, searching for springs, game, fish, treasure... has seen nothing. From one end of Europe to the other people called on him to find water, iron, oil... It is in part thanks to him that the dowser's mysterious art is beginning to interest official science."

## Abbé Mermet and Medical Radiesthesia

*From bien-etre-et-formation-ermitage.fr, "La radiesthésie médicale" (2021):*

From the 1930s onward, particularly in France, a prodigious enthusiasm developed for medical radiesthesia — the use of the pendulum as an aid to medical diagnosis and to the choice of treatments. This enthusiasm found its source in the great figures of radiesthesia, principally Abbé Bouly, the inventor of the term, and Abbé Mermet, considered the prince of dowsers. Doctors and pharmacists likewise seized upon the subject, becoming genuine experts in this revisited science of the interwar years.

### Radiesthetic diagnosis

The applications of radiesthesia to medicine (and to the veterinary art) go back to the beginning of the twentieth century with the work of Abbé Alexis Mermet (1866–1937), considered the prince of dowsers. He proposed establishing a radiesthetic diagnosis, and he described its creation shortly before his death: "More than thirty years ago, around 1905–1906, the idea came to me one day that it must be possible to use the pendulum as an auxiliary to medicine. I set to work, observing the radiations of the human body and of the various organs. I had no difficulty noticing that diseased organs no longer gave the same number of radiations as healthy organs. I had found it — I had created pendular diagnosis! And from then on, observation after observation, in clinics, in infirmaries, as an auxiliary to doctors, veterinarians, and herbalists, I was able to establish rules that became the foundation of radiesthetic diagnosis. I taught the science of the pendulum to doctors, veterinarians, and pharmacists, and so on, who took it up and practiced with success." (from his *Abrégé de ma méthode* ["Abridgment of My Method"], published 1938.)

What, then, is his method, which yields a diagnosis as precise as a doctor's or a veterinarian's? The abbé supplies the operating procedure, again in his abridgment: "To examine a patient, one moves the pendulum all around the body, at a distance of 15 to 20 centimeters. As soon as it passes in front of a diseased organ, it undergoes a repulsion; it moves away from the body, tracing in the air, in a vertical plane, a loop that closes on the diseased point — and this loop is all the more developed as the organ is more affected. To locate the diseased point, one must establish its depth in the organism. To do this, one places the index finger of the left hand at the spot below which the diseased organ lies; then one tunes one's receptivity by choosing as the unit of measurement the centimeter or the millimeter. The number of oscillations of the pendulum corresponds to the depth of the diseased focus, calculated in centimeters or millimeters. The pendulum can render the doctor other services still: it makes it possible to discover illnesses while they are forming or exist in a latent state, long before ordinary diagnosis could know their symptoms. In addition, it is of great use in the selection of remedies. Place the pendulum between a diseased organ and a remedy: if the remedy is very good, the pendulum rotates in the direct direction; if it is good, it oscillates more or less strongly between the organ and the remedy; it remains motionless if the remedy is neutral; and if the remedy is bad, it turns in the indirect direction."

### The AAR and the medical corps

It was an infatuation with this new discipline, with the creation in 1929 of the Association des Amis de la Radiesthésie, which brought together a great number of scientists of renown: Édouard Branly, Arsène d'Arsonval, Foveau de Courmelles, Armand Viré, Georges Claude, Georges Lakhovsky... Among the roughly 2,000 members of this association was a hundred-odd doctors, who created a medical section. Among the famous physicians, let us cite Dr. Albert Leprince, who published *Pendule et médecins. Sourciers et malades* (1933) and *Radiesthésie médicale. Applications de l'art du sourcier à la médecine humaine* (1936); Dr. Jules Regnault, director of the journal *Côte d'Azur médical*; and Dr. Alfred Roux, who published *Autour de la radiesthésie* (1938).

The pharmacists were not to be outdone — among them Louis Eugène Maupy, pharmacist and holder of the "Abbé Chaupitre" specialty brand; A. Soulier, author of *Spécification des toxines de la tuberculose et du cancer* (1932), who proposed a radiesthetic diagnosis of the urine; and Gédéon Meillère, member of the Académie de Médecine. Note the personality of Gabriel Lesourd (1890–1976), who after great hesitations over the use of the pendulum threw himself into the discipline and even joined the association's board of directors. He wrote two books on the discipline: *Méthode radiesthésique de recherche des maladies et imprégnations microbiennes* (1934) and *Vie, maladies, radiations* (1936). He carried out urine diagnoses and treatment selection for his clients at his Biological Laboratory of Medical Radiesthesia. This produced a line of herbal teas — the Tisanes Lesourd, still marketed today. He also created the Lesourd pendulum of black glass, adapted to biological research, as well as a journal: *La Science nouvelle, revue générale de la radiesthésie et des sciences psychiques*.

This rise of radiesthesia in the medical world, successful as it was, fairly soon subsided. A number of doctors and pharmacists opposed the approach and accused their colleagues of charlatanism, forcing them to practice within a narrower frame. The Second World War would put an end to its use within the official body of medicine and pharmacy.
