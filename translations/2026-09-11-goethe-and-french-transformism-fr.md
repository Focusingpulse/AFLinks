Dominique Massonnaud (Université de Haute-Alsace)

The "transformist" historians of nature have often been approached in a
perspective that today demands epistemological rigor; their
work falls at the very end of the eighteenth century and in the first half of the nineteenth century: to
cite the major figures, Goethe (1749-1832) in Germany; in France: Jean-Baptiste
Lamarck (1744-1829), who directed the invertebrate collection at the Museum from
1790 onward, Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire (1772-1844), appointed by Daubenton to the Museum of Natural
History in 1793 and the first holder of the chair of Vertebrate Zoology, and Antoine
Serres1 (1786-1868), professor of comparative anatomy at the Museum and physician at the University of
Montpellier.


---


There weighs, it seems to me, over this period — the first half of the nineteenth century — a recurring
reading that sees in these figures mere "precursors," whereas "modernity," the genuinely
effective, valued discoveries and renewals, would take place in the second
half of the century2, as indicated by a text by Quatrefages published in 1892: Darwin and his
French precursors. This a priori, recurrent throughout the twentieth century, thus has "modernity" begin
with Flaubert in literature, with Manet in painting, with Darwin in biology. As far as
Goethe is concerned, his death in 1832 places him in this period in which the "transformist3"
historians of nature are often presented as "pre-evolutionists" — a retroactive
reading of the history of science against which Georges Canguilhem has nevertheless warned
us: one finds it, for example, in the title of a chapter of René Berthelot's Science et philosophie chez
Goethe (1932): "The evolutionism of continuity in Goethe." The aim here is therefore not to seek the afterward — here, Darwin — in the before, but to grasp the specificity of a
moment of thought, by drawing as close as possible to the context in which it appears, by
seeking what it brings and precisely modifies, at that moment.

In this "Goethean moment4," these "transformists" are therefore not yet
"biologists" but "historians of nature" in the discourse of the period. Lamarck is credited with the first use of the word "biology" in his Philosophie anatomique (1809); the term
is in fact a borrowing from German: the word was coined in 1802 by the naturalist, specialist
in histology, Gottfried Reinhold Treviranus (1776-1837) in Biologie oder die Philosophie
der lebenden Natur (1802-1822). The transformists' method is distinctive and free of

the first publication, in February 1844, of Auguste Comte's Discours sur l'esprit positif. In that
text, published in the year of Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire's death, Comte takes over
from the transformists the motifs of the "germ" and the "embryo" in order to think through an idea of the progress of the
scientific and human spirit: here again, grasping transformism in the 1820s-1840s
requires shedding what later grafts itself onto it; moreover, as Canguilhem
also indicates in one of his articles on Bachelard:

The same words are not the same concepts; one must reconstruct the synthesis in which
      the concept is inserted — that is, both the conceptual context and the directing intention
      of the experiments or observations5.
       The hold of Darwin's later recognition made it possible, over the course of the nineteenth
century, to valorize those who were seen as "precursors," right up into the 1930s. The phenomenon is highly visible if one observes the history of Balzac criticism
devoted to analyzing Balzac's relation to the historians of nature of his time: Brunetière
asserts, in an analysis of the "Avant-propos" of La Comédie humaine — in Evolution de
la pensée lyrique en France au XIXe siècle — that the reference to Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire belongs to
that moment when "everyone began to hope that Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire's views would be
confirmed," for "the human mind was about to take a great step [...] It was the idea of evolution
that was entering science6." In the first half of the twentieth century, the relation of
Balzac to Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire was still effectively privileged; the link was valorizing:
it was regularly mentioned that Geoffroy was the dedicatee of Le Père Goriot in 1835.

Conversely, in the 1960s, Cuvier was revalorized in biological scholarship.
Yet Cuvier was, in the language of the period, a "fixist" and not a transformist: the adaptation of
organisms to their way of life seemed so well-suited that the idea of a modification, even
a gradual one, of their plan of organization was, in his view, inconceivable. Nevertheless, Cuvier was
valorized because his conception broke with the traditional idea of the "chain of beings," a
chain grasped as linear and continuous by the classical episteme7: he had demonstrated the diversity
of the organizational plans of bodies, according to species, whereas Geoffroy supposed
a continuity among all possible living forms8. Cuvier was thus revalorized in those
years 1950-60. It is amusing to note that Balzac was then made into an apostle of Cuvier9,
by recalling instead his tribute at the beginning of La Peau de chagrin in 1831. Today
this postwar period is regarded by biologists — by Stephen Jay Gould, for
example — as a moment of "rigidification10" of evolutionary thought. Indeed, this

2
valorization by literary criticism meets very directly a somewhat hasty reading of
Michel Foucault: in Les Mots et les Choses (1966), where Cuvier may seem to become the "true"
precursor, "in depth," of Darwin, just as Ricardo would be of Marx11.

Relative to these effects of successive valorizations linked to the evolution of
biology, we first have Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire and then Cuvier; but in 1985, the movement
seems to reverse once again: the journal Natural History publishes an article entitled
"Geoffroy and the homeo-box12." Work at the molecular level and the deciphering of the genome
have indeed given rise to recent revalorizations of the work of Étienne Geoffroy
Saint-Hilaire, which the observations of phylogenesis13 have met: in fact, an article published in
Nature in March 1996 rehabilitates one of his theses: it had seemed entirely fanciful, but
it is confirmed by molecular genetics — namely, the presence of a gene common
to insects and mammals that regulates the head/tail organization, and more
broadly the front/rear arrangement of organisms. One then reads that:

Functional studies now seem to confirm, as the first
      Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire suggested in 1822, that there was an inversion of the dorso-ventral axis in the course of
      animal evolution14.

Work in this field therefore demands a double vigilance: the task is to present elements
belonging to a history of science by returning directly to the texts themselves and not
to the "popularized" accounts that were subsequently produced, while also being attentive to
the History of this history.

Goethe and transformism

Goethe's scientific works constituted a reference throughout the nineteenth century;
publications regularly recalled his output in this domain. In 1837, Charles
Martins published Goethe's Œuvres d'histoire naturelle: comprenant divers mémoires d'anatomie comparée de botanique et de géologie [Cherbulliez], which went through sixteen reprints
up to 1857. One can then mention articles by Ernest Faivre, in 1862, in the Revue
contemporaine, followed by the publication of the Œuvres scientifiques de Goethe, commentées et
appréciées par Ernest Faivre, professor at the Faculty of Sciences of Lyon, published by Hachette
in 1862. Indeed, in an issue of the Revue d'Histoire des Sciences devoted to Étienne
Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, an article asserted that a:

         Summary of the history of comparative anatomy confirms, in agreement with the opinion of their
         authors, that the works of Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire and those of Goethe introduced a
         new method in biology, and it is not impossible that it may once again serve as a
         guide in future research15.

3
       To grasp what transformism is, the Goethean notion of "metamorphosis"
seems directly called for, and on this point I refer to the work of Anne Gaëlle
Weber16. I will therefore begin instead from another Goethean concept, central to his work, that of
"Morphology17."


---


Goethe gives the name "Morphology" to the discipline he wants to help create and
which must encompass botany and osteology; the central notion of "metamorphosis" enables
him to link the two domains. Starting from "morphology" has the advantage of emphasizing
the fact that Goethe's work takes up the idea of the unity of plan in the organization of the animal
kingdom but adds to it a decisive importance of the "type," which allows him to undertake
work in comparative anatomy. As he indicates:

Comparative anatomy has prepared the ground for a general concept of organic natures. It
      leads us from form to form and [...] we rise above them all, so as to see their
      characteristics in an ideal image18.

If one sometimes reads that the idea of a "unity of composition" to which all
organisms of the animal world would be subject is quite archaic or naïve, it is important to
observe that Goethe's approach, as we may understand it here, does not make it an initial belief, but
an a posteriori model founded on a prior work of observation. Goethe is the author, in 1792, of
De l'expérience considérée comme médiatrice entre l'objet et le sujet (On Experiment as Mediator between Object and Subject). As Nicolas Class has indicated, Goethe's path led him to elaborate precisely and progressively the notion
of Urphänomen, given in the Theory of Colours as the "sensible and
intellectual intuition of the law within the phenomenon19." Observations in comparative anatomy are
essential for combining sensation and reason. The approach is spelled out:

I consider all phenomena as independent of one another by forcibly
      isolating them; then, I consider them as correlates and reunite them in a decisive life. I often
      proceed in this way with nature, but even with the most recent history of the world stirring around
      us, this way of looking at things is fruitful20.

The methodical principle is thus of an experimental order. One finds this same approach in France
in Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, who likewise practiced comparative anatomy from the
Egypt campaign onward: the work thus gave rise to a memoir on electric fishes, on the
Torpille ray, for example21. He writes in his Principes de philosophie zoologique, discussed at the
Academy of Sciences in March 1830: "so many animals that I held to be different, and that, by
imposing a specific name on them, I treated as distinct, differed nevertheless only by
a few slight attributes22." On the methodological level, he effectively asserts: "The aim of
research in comparative anatomy is the knowledge of the laws of organization of animals

4
with precision great enough to be able to elaborate and formulate laws23." The links between
Goethe's works and those of Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, the leading figure of French
transformism, are underscored in the French edition of Goethe's Œuvres scientifiques published by
Hachette in 186224, where one reads:


---


Geoffroy [...] recognizes in birds a genuine dental system; he points out analogies
      between the bones of the skull and the limbs in mammals, birds, reptiles and fish;
      he demonstrates that the skull is an assemblage of vertebrae, and that the fetus, before reaching
      its complete development, passes through a succession of transitory forms that recall the definitive
      forms of more perfect animals; finally, he indicates the possibility of producing, under certain
      conditions, anomalies and monstrosities.

Indeed, a first highly decisive piece of work by Goethe was devoted to the observation, in comparative
anatomy, of the intermaxillary bone: this bone of the upper jaw is present in vertebrate
animals with bony skulls that have incisors. At the end of the eighteenth century, the common
discourse concerning its absence in humans served to ground the difference between humanity
and animality; in this context, it had also been made a decisive element for
justifying a singularity of man, who alone would be endowed with language. Goethe observes that this bone
is indeed present in primates, where it is fused to the maxilla but three distinct sutures are
then visible; by contrast, it seems absent when one observes the skulls of adult men.
Goethe's approach to analyzing the living does not then burden itself with a difference between man and
animal that would suffice to explain everything. Goethe's inquiry takes into account these sutures
visible in primates in order to go looking for a comparable phenomenon in humans: by
observing the skulls of young children. What is more, he extends his observations to embryos
as well as to a hydrocephalic skull. This allows him to affirm the presence of this bone in the human
being.

Here, on the epistemological level, two points must be noted: the recourse to
embryology, and the work on what was regarded as a "failure of nature" —
this hydrocephalic skull, commonly thought to belong to the realm of anomaly, but which Goethe uses
to establish general principles. He thereby accomplishes a remarkable scientific
gesture. What belongs to anatomical deviation, the "monster," becomes revealing of the general
law. In the work of Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire in France, the approach in this direction is
particularly developed: one often reads that he is the founder of teratology25. He had
worked in 1819 on deformations of the human skull and on acephalic monsters, as
one can read in a Mémoire of 1821. He also developed a classification of
anomalies between 1825 and 1827. For example, Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire proposes in 1825 a

Mémoire sur les Aspalasomes. These are unitary monsters, characterized by the abnormal
conformation of their trunk and limbs; he then brings to light the genera "Hypognathus"
and "Heteradelphus," in order to observe the variations of organization of what are
commonly called Siamese twins. The Heteradelphus designates two brothers, one of whom develops while the other does not, the second remaining a kind of permanent embryo that participates in the common life but
in a parasitic mode.

The general laws can therefore be efficiently observed with the "monster," which takes on
a meaningful function: it "shows" and reveals the natural nomos. Irregularity is no longer
contrary to some supposed general will of nature; rather, it will make it possible to deepen
the knowledge of the mechanisms of the living. As Canguilhem writes, this "intelligence of
the anomaly" constitutes a major epistemological leap. He adds: "one may grant that
the intelligence of the anomaly is that by which biology has distinguished itself from mechanics26." This
position is foundational for "the experience of life" and for the productivity of "negative
values" and, as such, is central to Canguilhem's work on the normal and the
pathological. In this sense, the transformist work on monsters can thus refute the
claims of Judith Schlanger, who makes of transformism a Newtonianism applied to
biology27.

Homology — the role of the environment

To continue speaking of Goethe's work, the observations contained in The
Metamorphosis of Plants enabled him to carry out effective work on what is today called
homology and, in particular, serial homology. The idea is that the organs — or their elements — within one and the same organism are formed from unique elements that
recombine differently, having the same forms under different functions28. Beyond
the work on botany, this principle of serial homology would be invoked by Goethe to
work once more in osteology, with the vertebral theory of the skull.

While the Italian journey had allowed the idea of the Urpflanze to begin taking shape,
it also allowed — starting from the observation of a sheep's skull "in the dunes of the Lido
that separate the Venetian lagoon from the Adriatic Sea" in 1790 — the subsequent proposal (published in 1820) that the bones of the skull are derived from the vertebrae. This work can be
situated within metamerism29 as it is conceived today: it is a mode
of organization of the body that allows one to see in it a succession of segments, called metameres:
one observes the repetition, all along the body from front to rear, of a structure


---


6
fundamentally identical to itself. This proposition of Goethe's allows him to grasp the
progressive character of transformations: in what he calls "successive
metamorphosis." Thus, what today belongs to serial homology — within one and the same organism — found
developments in France, in the work of Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire — in particular for
another kind of homology: special homology, between species. Geoffroy is the one who develops
what he calls the "theory of analogues": as Jean Rostand indicated, he laid down its
outlines as early as 1796 in the memoir he devoted to the makis, and then presented it in a highly
elaborated form in the Principes de Philosophie zoologique in 183030.

From 1806 onward, Geoffroy worked on the skeletons of fishes and then on the bones of young
birds in order to compare them. One observes a proximity of approaches: Étienne Geoffroy Saint-
Hilaire indicated in 1807 that he had hoped "to discover in the skull of fishes the same
parts as in that of the other vertebrate animals31." As Laurent Goulven indicates:

The comparison of the skulls of Fishes and of Mammals showed him that the former had
      more pieces than the latter, which was directly contrary to his principle that 'nature
      works constantly with the same materials,' with 'the same elements, in the same number'32.

Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire then studied "the bones of the human skull at an age closer
to the time of their formation," which allowed him to establish that "fishes, in their
first age" were "in the same conditions, relative to their development, as the
fetuses of mammals33." The observational work then makes it possible to develop the theory. In
1824, a Mémoire by Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire affirms the presence of a dental system in
birds; he also proposes elements for establishing a point of passage between vertebrates
and annelids: only Ampère and the physician Hallé would follow him on this point, as his
son Isidore indicates in the book he devotes to his father's work34. Étienne Geoffroy Saint-
Hilaire, in 1822, in the second volume of his Philosophie anatomique: Monstruosités humaines35,
lays down a law given as the "elective affinities of organic elements" and brings to
light the reciprocal suitability of the different parts, allowing the fusion of two similar
organs. On the level of methods, Goethe and Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire have in common
the practice of observing, with comparative anatomy, resemblances instead of the manifest or
habitually emphasized differences. They then note inequalities of development that make it possible
to ground a law of compensation. Goethe formulates it thus: "nature's budget being fixed,
too considerable a sum allotted to one expenditure demands an economy elsewhere36." Geoffroy
likewise indicates that if one organ or anatomical element develops, the others are
reduced.

       Another remarkable point in this transformist work is the influence of the environment on
the transformation of species. This additional element links Goethe's work to that of
Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire. From his Italian journey (1786-88) onward, Goethe had differentiated himself
from Linnaeus in botany: by taking into account the type of a plant but also the various
climatic conditions that may influence its growth. Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire is
the first to use the term "milieu" (environment) in the singular in French — from 1831 onward — to designate
and analyze the "ensemble of circumstances that surround and influence a living being37." The
word appears in the plural in the natural history writings of the eighteenth century, as well as in
Lamarck, where the expressions "milieux environnans" (surrounding environments) or "milieux ambians" (ambient environments) designate the
"ensemble of actions exerted from the outside upon a living being38." Étienne Geoffroy Saint-
Hilaire has the particularity of giving the environment a determining role in the transformation of
species. Thus, he indicates that according to the modifications that may undergo:

The molecular arrangement of the organic tissues. The son, being born henceforth under other
      influences than the father, cannot resemble him in every respect [...] the possibility of such
      experiments [gives] a presentiment of the new destinies of the Natural History of the globe.

He asserts that: "Every organized body obeys its virtual development, which it draws from its
original essence; but at the same time, it develops only in the manner prescribed by its
ambient environment39." Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire indeed wrote a text entitled Influences
des milieux ambiants pour modifier les formes animales40 after conducting experimental
work between 1825 and 1828 on hen's eggs in the course of incubation. The aim was
to obtain anatomical variations by modifying the chemical composition of the air breathed by the
embryo. A manuscript by Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, found only in 1970, relates the
procedures and the results of the observations. As Jean-Louis Fischer indicates: "The
techniques employed by Geoffroy were taken up by other researchers until these last
years, most often without knowing that it was Geoffroy who had inaugurated them41."

The last article of the "great Goethe"

The last point I will address concerns the debates that set the
historians of nature against one another in this period, and in particular the famous quarrel of 1830, which would give Goethe
the occasion to write his two last articles. As Isidore Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire writes, in
chapter X of the history of his father's works, Goethe had not had, for The Metamorphosis
of Plants, the luck that Étienne had — that of the quarrels with Cuvier: in 1825 over the
crocodiles of Caen42, and then in 1830. The transformist idea of extending the unity of the plan of
composition to all classes was the object of the March 1830 debate at the Academy of Sciences.


---


8
It took place on the occasion of the discussion of a memoir presented by Laurencet and Pierre Stanislas
Meyranx (1792-1832), showing an anatomical analogy between cephalopods — the octopus,
the cuttlefish, the squid — and vertebrates. From this work, Geoffroy deduced — beyond the
hypotheses proposed by the two authors of the memoir — the proven existence of a
single branch that would make it possible to place mollusks,
arthropods and vertebrates on the same plane. Cuvier responds violently: he considers that Geoffroy
extrapolates abusively, with regard to the observations presented. On the symbolic level, as on the
scientific level, the transformist model as Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire conceives it thus
posits the possibility of passages by affirming the porosity of taxonomic categories.
      When Goethe (1749-1832) presents the controversy that took place between Cuvier and
Geoffroy, in an article of 1830 and then again in his last article in 1832, the author
of The Metamorphosis of Plants (1790) asserts that "the question in dispute is a European one and
of a scope that goes beyond the circle of natural history." Goethe's figure was then central
in the French sphere: one recalls that Balzac would immediately place La Comédie humaine
under the authority of the "great Goethe" in his foreword of 1842, and there makes explicit
mention of "the last article that the great Goethe wrote." This last text therefore deserves
our attention. The article appeared in France in La Revue encyclopédique, a quarterly review with a wide
readership, to which Flourens — one of the founders of experimental neuroscience —
but also Benjamin Constant and later Pierre Leroux contributed. It is found under the title "Les Naturalistes
français" in volume V of Paris ou le livre des cent-un (1831-1834), published by Ladvocat; it
was also reissued in volume form together with Goethe's natural history
works in 1837 in Martins's translation at Cherbulliez43 — to remain within the French sphere.

It is often said that Goethe forcefully defends Geoffroy's scientific thought in this
text. He does indeed endorse his theses and thereby contributes to forging opinion: Geoffroy becomes
the emblem of an optimism opening the way to rational possibilities of understanding the
universe. A synthetic vision of the history of humanity is at stake: Geoffroy is presented by
Goethe as the French champion of the work of German Naturphilosophie. He is then
the man of the naturalist synthesis of ideas, Cuvier the man of analysis: the man of facts.
As Darwin would say, generously evoking his predecessors, the facts proved the
ideas right. Indeed, from the outset, in these commentaries that praise Geoffroy's Philosophie
zoologique, the aim is to oppose two methodological choices, but also to regret that the two
naturalists could not work better together. Cuvier accumulates observations of detail


---


9
      [He] never tires of distinguishing, of describing exactly what is before his eyes, and of thus extending
      his empire over an immense surface; Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire studies in silence the analogies
      of beings and their mysterious affinities: the former starts from isolated existences to arrive at a whole
      that he presupposes, without ever thinking he might have an intuition of it; the latter carries within his inner
      self the image of that whole, and lives in the conviction that the isolated beings can little by little be deduced from it.

Goethe forcefully underscores the interaction of the two scholars' works, an interaction of which neither
is sufficiently aware:
      Cuvier does not believe in the possibility of presentiment, in the foresight of the part within the whole.
      To want to know and to distinguish what one can neither see with the eyes nor touch with the hands
      seems to him an exorbitant pretension.

In this second article, Cuvier is characterized as "a systematic arranger, who holds
to particular facts." Geoffroy is the continuator of Buffon, but does not confine himself,
like the latter, "to actual nature, existing and complete; he studies it in its germ, its
development, its future." Geoffroy's method as thus presented is therefore a "historical"
method that takes into account duration and the modifications of species. Methodologically it
joins the principles of Herder, such as Quinet (1803-1875) reads them in the introduction
to the Ideen sur la philosophie de l'histoire et de l'humanité (Ideas for a Philosophy of History, 1827): the explanation of the great by
the small, the grasp of the whole by the part, the singular intuition that makes it possible to reconstruct
causal chains, linking imagination and reason. The presence of diachrony is possible within
an analytic research: such is, according to Goethe, the great contribution of Étienne Geoffroy
Saint-Hilaire's thought. The 1832 article closes as it had begun, lamenting that Cuvier and
Geoffroy do not collaborate, because:

Separating and reuniting are two founding acts of the understanding; or rather one is obliged, whether
      one wishes it or not, to go from the particular to the general, and from the general to the particular. The more these
      intellectual functions — which I compare to inhalation and
      exhalation — are carried out with energy, the more the scientific life of the world will flourish44.
Goethe's last article thus proposes a horizon: one that would conjoin the methods,
developing the study of facts without omitting the broader aims and the general plan, in a
permanent circulation from one to the other. Moreover, this article by the author of Faust draws an
analogy between the organization of a text and the scientific model of the organization of the living. It
specifies the use of the term "material" and then that of "composition," devalued because it is
"still a vicious term borrowed from mechanics45." Goethe goes into this point in very precise
detail and then compares the French and German languages. He distinguishes the rhetorical and
traditional use of the term "composition" and its naturalist use:


---


10
      The organs are not combined, are not brought together like objects finished and completed
      separately; they develop out of one another, modifying themselves, to form an entity that necessarily
      tends to constitute a whole.

He also spells out what the term "Plan" covers, a term that:

Serves to express that the materials are arranged according to an order combined in advance; but this word
      instantly recalls the layout of a house or a town, whose arrangement, however admirable
      it may be, cannot be compared to that of an organized being.

Goethe notes, however, that these considerations do not apply to poetry or art; rather, he
proposes for natural history a textual arrangement that would borrow from the vitalist model of
the organization of the living. These two articles by Goethe reappear in French in 1837, in a
work that also takes up the naturalist's studies — on geology or the metamorphosis
of plants — thus making explicit and exemplifying the method he advocates: known to Balzac,
it seems to me to have been eminently suggestive in giving the construction of the whole
Balzacian corpus a methodical warrant, suggesting an epistemological transposition into the field
of social history, of the analysis of mores. The reading of this last article of the great Goethe
is striking in this respect when one returns to it:

One sees how the mathematical demonstration, which with a series of elements produces a thousand
      combinations, differs from the kind of demonstration that a skilled orator knows how to deduce from his
      arguments. Arguments may have very partial relations, but an ingenious orator
      gifted with imagination forces them to converge toward a common point, and plays his audience with
      appearances of good and evil, of false and true. But the one who proceeds conscientiously [...]
      strives carefully to elaborate the isolated experiments, in order to arrive at considerations of a
      higher order. These will be [...] coordinated together as they develop,
      and grouped so as to form, like mathematical formulas, an unshakable edifice, in
      its parts and in its whole.

Goethe thus proposes and endorses an idea of arrangement that makes it possible to leave the
rhetorical and demonstrative order, escaping the compositional model. The method, founded on
patient and careful coordination, is laid down programmatically; it is a matter of a:

First work in which these materials will be coordinated in series, without arranging them in a
      systematic manner; each can then group them in his own way so as to form a whole more or less
      approachable and accessible to the intelligence.

This insistence on a principle of organization, a method founded on coordination, naturally resonates
for any reader of Balzac: beyond the word "coordination" taken up by Balzac, it seems
one can read here a commentary on the reading paths opened by La Comédie humaine46.

       I will add, by way of conclusion, that Goethe's figure is thus central in the French
sphere. The "Essai sur Goethe et le second Faust" which appears at the head of the translation of Faust
by Henri Blaze, at Charpentier, in 1840, is significant: the author of the preface brings out the
difficulty of Goethe's text and its radical singularity:

This man gathers into the same epic Helen and Faust, Paris and Wagner, the modern Kabists and
      Vulcanists, the "ideas" of Plato and the "matrices" of Paracelsus: the
      powerful attitude of this singular emperor who holds together the ancient world and the new world, who
      now weighs them gravely, now amuses himself by clashing them together, playing still, in his fancy, with
      the thousand sonorous sparks that can spring from them; there is in all this something that
      astonishes and terrifies you. By what secret of genius can so many diverse elements combine
      harmoniously47?

This Goethe-moment is thus a particular moment: that of transformism, which opens up other
paths and stirs significant resonances. One may recall that before meeting him,
George Sand corresponded with Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire — the exchange of letters falls
between June 1835 and June 1839 — and she planned to write about his work48. Indeed, she
writes a text: "Soi pour soi – la science" (Self for self — science), devoted to questions of comparative anatomy.

While Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire interrogates the pertinence and the foundations of a
difference between man and animal, one may also recall a short novel by the young
Flaubert, dated October 8, 1837, Quidquid volueris, which puts on stage a singular character,
a musician, solitary, one of those young men in black who resemble us; and this character
turns out to be the fruit of the loves of an ape and a woman49. If transformism establishes a porosity
between animal species, one can also observe that, in this period, the strict generic
frontiers of the classical age come undone. The grotesque mingles with the sublime, and people begin to write
poems in prose. On the level of cultural history, this Goethean moment is therefore perhaps
also that of a literary transformism.
