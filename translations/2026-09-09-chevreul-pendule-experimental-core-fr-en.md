# CHAPTER IV.

## M. CHEVREUL'S RESEARCHES ON THE SO-CALLED EXPLORER PENDULUM.

172.

The curiosity I have always had to know the facts within the history of human faculties led me to take advantage of the friendship of the respectable M. Deleuze to initiate me into animal magnetism, and to study its phenomena on every occasion that presented itself to me from 1810 to 1813. One day—it was in 1812—M. Deleuze spoke to me in my laboratory about the pendulum of Fortis and Ch. Amoretti, and suggested the desire to try to reproduce its effects.

This I did immediately. In communicating the results of my trials to M. Œrstedt, who was then in Paris overseeing the printing of the French translation of his *Researches on the Identity of Chemical and Electrical Forces*, and with whom I had frequent conversations, he confessed that, despite the regard he had for Ritter's ideas, his opinion was not yet fixed on the cause of the movement of Fortis's pendulum.

Various motives which it is superfluous to give led me to postpone the publication of my experiments until the year 1833, when they appeared in the form of a Letter addressed to M. Ampère. M. Œrstedt, after examining it in Copenhagen, wrote to me that he completely shared my way of seeing. In 1846 [1], two former students of the École Polytechnique, MM. Desplaces and Chabert, and M. Robert having communicated to the Academy of Sciences some experiments on Fortis's pendulum, I took the liberty of recalling mine, and in the *Compte rendu* of the session of December 14, 1846, an extract from the Letter from the *Revue des Deux-Mondes* was inserted. Finally, when this year there was talk of *turning tables*, I saw with satisfaction the welcome given to my ideas by several French and foreign journals. And this satisfaction increased further through the literary researches I undertook on the divining rod and the explorer pendulum. By reproducing textual passages from my Letter, one will see the analogies of the facts described therein with those offered by authors whose works I was unaware of in 1812 at the time of my experiments, and in 1833 at the time of their publication. But if the analogy of the facts is evident, the interpretation I gave differs absolutely from those to which they had previously been subjected.

**Letter from M. E. Chevreul to M. Ampère on a particular class of muscular movements; L'Hay, near Paris, March 23, 1833 (*Revue des Deux-Mondes*, issue of May 1st).**

173.

If I were not interested as an experimenter in my readers knowing perfectly the Letter that was inserted in 1833 in the *Revue des Deux-Mondes*, I would now reproduce only a fragment, the rest being scattered with development in Parts IV and V of the work. But if a textual and complete reproduction has the disadvantage of giving rise to repetitions, it has the great advantage, in my opinion, of offering proof that my current opinions are those I expressed twenty years ago. Be that as it may, I will distinguish the fragment that forms an essential part of the work from the citation that will follow, the purpose of which is to spare those of my readers who would like to consult the whole of my Letter the trouble of searching for it in the *Revue des Deux-Mondes*.

174.

"My dear friend,

"You ask me for a description of the experiments I did in 1812 to know whether it is true, as several people had assured me, *that a pendulum formed of a heavy body and a flexible thread oscillates when one holds it in the hand above certain bodies, although the arm is immobile.* You think these experiments have some importance; in yielding to the reasons you gave me for publishing them, let me be permitted to say that it took all the faith I have in your enlightenment to decide me to put before the public facts of a genre so different from those with which I have entertained it until now. Be that as it may, I will, following your desire, expose my observations; I will present them in the order in which I made them.

"The pendulum I used was an iron ring suspended from a hemp thread; it had been arranged by a person who vividly desired that I verify for myself the phenomenon that manifested when she placed it above water, a block of metal, or a living being—a phenomenon of which she made me a witness. I confess it was not without surprise that I saw it reproduce itself when, having taken the pendulum thread in my right hand myself, I had placed the latter above the mercury in my pneumato-chemical trough, above an anvil, above several animals, etc. I concluded from my experiments that if, as I was assured, there were only a certain number of bodies capable of determining the oscillations of the pendulum, it could happen that by interposing other bodies between the former and the moving pendulum, the latter would stop. Despite my presumption, my astonishment was great when, after taking in my left hand a glass plate, a cake of resin, etc., and having placed one of these bodies between mercury and the pendulum that was oscillating above it, I saw the oscillations diminish in amplitude and annihilate themselves entirely. They began again when the intermediate body was withdrawn, and annihilated themselves anew by the interposition of the same body. This succession of phenomena repeated itself a great number of times with a truly remarkable constancy, whether the intermediate body was held by me or by another person. The more these effects seemed extraordinary to me, the more I felt the need to verify whether they were really foreign to all muscular movement of the arm, as had been affirmed to me in the most positive manner.

"This led me to support the right arm holding the pendulum on a wooden support that I could advance at will from the shoulder to the hand and bring back from the hand toward the shoulder. I soon noticed that, in the first circumstance, the movement of the pendulum decreased all the more as the support approached closer to the hand, and that it ceased when the fingers holding the thread were themselves supported, whereas in the second circumstance, the contrary effect took place; however, for equal distances from the support to the thread, the movement was slower than before. I thought from this that it was very probable that a muscular movement occurring without my knowledge determined the phenomenon, and I should all the more take this opinion into consideration as I had a memory—vague, to be true—of having been in a *quite particular state* when my eyes followed the oscillations described by the pendulum I held in my hand.

"I redid my experiments, the arm perfectly free, and convinced myself that the memory I just spoke of was not an illusion of my mind, for I felt very well that at the same time my eyes followed the oscillating pendulum, there was in me a *disposition* or *tendency to movement* which, involuntary as it seemed to me, was all the more satisfied as the pendulum described larger arcs; from then on I thought that..."

## GENERAL CONCLUSION RELATIVE TO THE DIVINING ROD.

154.

How did mysterious ideas attach themselves to the divining rod?

I have said (§37), in showing that in the most remote antiquity it was a sign of authority, of power, of command, finally an instrument of divination.

It is as such that it was employed to search for metals toward the 15th century, and for underground waters in the 17th.

The explanations given for its movement fall into two very different categories:

The first comprises those that attach this movement to the *moral world*, to a spiritual cause, which may be God, angels, the demon, or again the spirit of man;

The second comprises explanations that attach to the *physical world*.

1° To properties with which matter is endowed. Peripatetics put forward occult properties known under the names of *empathies* and *antipathies*.

2° To *corpuscles*, to *vapors*, to *subtle matter*. This opinion was held by Cartesians.

3° To an imponderable fluid, such as *electricity, electro-magnetism, electro-organism*, etc. This is the opinion of several of our contemporaries.

155.

Critical examination of the most remarkable writings to which the divining rod gave rise has led me to reject every cause from the physical world according to the considerations expressed by Father Malebranche, the Abbé de Rancé, the Abbé Pirot, Father Ménestrier and above all Father Lebrun.

Indeed, phenomena emanating from a cause of the physical world reproduce themselves in a constant manner and as *fatal* in the same circumstances. Well, we have seen the partisans of the rod divided into two camps when it comes to recognizing with it the specific nature of a hidden body: some claim that its movement is augmented by contact with matter *identical* to that which determined the movement, while contact with matter *different* from that which determined the movement produces no effect; others claim that the movement is stopped by contact with matter *different* from that which determined it. Now, if the cause of the movement were physical, one of these two opinions would be true; but both cannot be true at the same time; therefore the cause is not physical.

## TRANSLATOR'S NOTE

Michel-Eugène Chevreul (1786–1889), the great French chemist, was 68 when he published this work in 1854. His experiments on the pendulum, begun in 1812 and published in 1833, constitute one of the founding documents of experimental psychology — specifically, the first rigorous demonstration of what would later be called the **ideomotor effect**: unconscious muscular movements produced by expectation and attention.

The arm-support experiment translated above is the crucial demonstration. When Chevreul supported his arm on a wooden block and moved the support progressively from shoulder to hand, the pendulum's oscillations decreased and finally stopped when the fingers themselves were immobilized. This proved that the movement came from the arm, not from the pendulum or any external force.

Chevreul's conclusion — that the divining rod and pendulum move by unconscious muscular action, not by any physical cause — became the standard scientific explanation for dowsing and related phenomena. His work influenced William Benjamin Carpenter, who coined the term "ideomotor" in 1852, and through him the entire subsequent tradition of experimental psychology.

The translation preserves Chevreul's precise, methodical prose. His style is that of a careful experimentalist: he describes what he did, what he observed, and what he concluded, with minimal speculation. The famous passage about the "quite particular state" and the "disposition or tendency to movement" is among the first introspective accounts of what we now call attentional set or motor readiness.

**Why this matters for the corpus:** Chevreul's work is the *falsification half* of the radiesthesia lane. The French tradition (Chaumery/de Bélizal, Turenne, Servranx, etc.) documents what practitioners claim; Chevreul documents why those claims cannot be attributed to physical causes. Both halves belong in a complete archive.
