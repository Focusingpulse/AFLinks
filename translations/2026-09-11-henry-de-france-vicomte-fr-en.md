# Vicomte Henry de France (1872–1947): The Aristocrat of Radiesthesia

Henry de France (16 March 1872, Abbeville, Somme – 1947, Château d'Arry, Somme), Vicomte, known as *Henry de France père* to distinguish him from his son of the same name (also a radiesthésiste), was a French landowner, dowser and radiesthésiste — the second president of the Association des Amis de la Radiesthésie (AAR), founder of the movement's first monthly journal, and one of the great popularizers of the discipline between the wars.

## First Encounter with the Rod (1924)

He told the story of his beginnings as a dowser himself, in one of his books:

> "One day I had gone to visit a cousin of mine who lived in my neighborhood. I found him walking in one of the paths of his park, holding in both hands a kind of small wooden fork. He explained to me that this singular instrument lifted when one crossed an underground stream of water. Just in case, I tried to see whether the same phenomenon would reproduce itself in my hands. The rod lifted feebly, but quite distinctly."

That was in 1924. Encouraged by this first trial, he ran further experiments, then had the good fortune to meet Abbé Bouly, who completed his initiation into radiesthesia.

In 1926, an international psychic congress was held in Paris, including a section on water-diviners. His detailed report of it was the first major article Henry de France wrote on radiesthesia.

## The Water Prospecting Specialist

A licentiate in history and geography, he had solid geological knowledge which, joined to his gifts as a radiesthésiste, quickly made him a renowned specialist in water prospecting. His broad culture and his ease of literary composition let him write numerous works: *Souvenirs d'un sourcier* (Memoirs of a Dowser), *Radiesthésie agricole* (Agricultural Radiesthesia), and above all *Le Sourcier moderne* (The Modern Dowser) — in its time a true synthesis of the procedures used by ancient and modern radiesthésistes.

## The AAR Presidency

The Association des Amis de la Radiesthésie had been created by Abbé Bouly on 29 December 1929 in Lille, with its seat at 91 rue de l'Hôpital Militaire. The inaugural meeting was held at the Lille hippodrome on 29 January 1930 before no fewer than 500 people; by the end of 1930 the association counted more than 160 members, and within a few years nearly 2,000.

Very busy with his many activities, Abbé Bouly left his place as president to Vicomte Henry de France (the father). De France found the word *radiesthésie* perfect, as he explained:

> "The word proposed by Abbé Bouly, meaning perception of radiations, has the quality of encompassing everything dowsers deal with — water, ores, diseases, and even plenty of other things… As for 'Friends,' it's a find: it lets us appeal to a great many people who are not dowsers at all, but who take an interest, more or less closely, in our art."

He was succeeded by Armand Viré, director of the underground biology laboratory of the Muséum d'Histoire Naturelle — a dynamic figure who organized many conferences, outings and experiments, including the 1934 discovery of unknown chambers in the caves of Lacave in the company of Abbé Mermet. In 1935 it was Abbé Mermet who took over the association, at the height of his fame after the publication of his major work *Comment j'opère* (How I Operate).

The founding members of the association included great names of French science: Édouard Branly, inventor of wireless telegraphy; Arsène d'Arsonval, founder of electrotherapy and member of the Institut; Deslandres, member of the Académie des sciences; Dr. Foveau de Courmelle, pioneer of chromotherapy and dean of the radiologists; and Dr. Gédéon Meillère, president of the Académie de médecine in 1932. Contemporary accounts note how much reference was made to Branly's wireless in the books of the period — radiesthesia was compared to this invisible current/wave, with its notions of antenna, receiver, capacitor, frame…

## The Chronique des Sourciers (1930–1940)

The enthusiasm for the discipline pushed Henry de France to launch a monthly journal bearing the association's spirit — the first monthly review devoted to radiesthesia: *La Chronique des Sourciers* (The Dowsers' Chronicle), created in 1930. It ceased publication in 1940. His editorial committee was composed, for the most part, of former students of the École Polytechnique.

## Agricultural Radiesthesia

De France defined the field plainly: "Agricultural Radiesthesia is the application of the dowser's art to Agriculture." It comprises:

- the study of the soil with the dowser's instruments: identifying its water resources, its composition, and the fertilizers it may need;
- then the study of the plant: assessing its vitality, greater or lesser, and the accord between plant and terrain;
- finally the same practices applied to the animal — both its health and the foods that suit it.

## Tool Improvements

Several refinements that eased the use of rod and pendulum are owed to him:

- He was the first to use rods formed of two flat blades of whalebone or steel — models that let the operator better perceive the effect of unconscious movements on the rod.
- He also advised winding the free end of the pendulum's suspension thread around a small stick held between thumb and forefinger, making it easier to adjust the thread's length.

## The Two-Origin Theory

Henry de France held a nuanced position on what moved the dowser's instruments — notable for its honesty about the limits of the theory:

- Successes in **map work** (recherches sur cartes) could, in his view, be explained only by **intuition**.
- In **field prospecting**, by contrast, he supposed that a **physical effect**, probably of electromagnetic or radioactive origin, could directly provoke the dowser's unconscious movements.

He was thus a partisan of attributing two distinct origins to the unconscious movements of the dowser — a deliberate split between the psychic and the physical readings of the discipline, at a time when most writers collapsed them into one.

## Le Sourcier moderne

His practical manual opens with a bold analogy: researchers have found proof that in ancient times the rod was used by miners; from then on, it ought to become for the geologist what the telescope is for the astronomer and the microscope for the biologist. Not only does one locate ores and metals with its help, but also faults and cavities — hence the unexpected and fruitful uses made of it by archaeologists.

He announced an application "still more extraordinary, recently attempted with success": the rod now serving to scrutinize the state of plants, animals and men, and to diagnose their diseases. "Recent and rigorously controlled experiments leave no doubt on this point."

The art of the rod and its companion research instrument, the pendulum, was to become indispensable to farmers, architects, archaeologists, engineers, officers, geologists, physicians and veterinarians alike. His little manual gave the essential notions for forming a good operator, after a short historical study and a survey of the scientific explanations available for the movements of rod and pendulum. His training method was built on a sequence of fundamental experiments performed on electricity first — what is practiced on electricity is later reproduced on water, on metals and ores, on plants, on animals, and on man himself: always the same procedures and the same laws. The electrical experiments had the double advantage of anchoring the practice to its scientific starting point and of giving the trainee a simple means of directing his work and checking his results.

In the preface he thanked the journals that had accepted his articles over the years — *Les Études*, *La Vie à la Campagne*, *Le Chasseur Français*, *L'Intérêt Français*, *La Somme* — and above all "MM. the abbés Bouly, Mermet, Ferran for their collaboration from the first hour." Successive editions were kept up to date with the incessant discoveries of researchers of all nations.

## Death and Legacy

His death, in 1947 at the Château d'Arry, left a great void in the world of radiesthesia, where he was appreciated as much for his great talent as a radiesthésiste as for his human qualities.

The association he had steered through its golden years went dormant during the Second World War; only 125 members returned in 1946. It regained financial stability but was attacked again — trials were brought against medical radiesthésistes, and the association never again exceeded 500 members. The rift between the partisans of radiesthesia and the scientific and medical corps was, by then, consummated. Under Bruno Allione (president until his death in 2006), the AAR renewed itself in the 21st century, adding geobiology and radionics to its original specialty and building ties with the Italian radiesthesia community.

## Sources' Points of Disagreement

- The ermitage.fr history and the AAR's own history page date his succession of Bouly to the association's earliest years (presidents were elected for one-year terms); the biographical sketch by Jean-Claude Maes dates it "at the same time" as the 1930 founding of the Chronique des Sourciers. Both agree he presided over the AAR and later over four international congresses.
- The spelling of his first name varies: "Henri" on ebookesoterique.com, "Henry" in his own works and most accounts. His son, also a radiesthésiste, bore the same name — hence "Henry de France, père."

## Historical Note

Henry de France stands in the French radiesthesia tradition alongside Abbé Bouly (who named the discipline), Abbé Mermet (its most famous practitioner), and the Chaumery–de Belizal school (its theorists of form-waves). His specific contributions were institutional and methodological: the first journal of the movement, the first manual that trained beginners through controlled electrical experiments, the extension of the dowser's art to agriculture, and the two-origin theory — an early attempt to separate the intuitive from the physical in radiesthetic practice. The Radio-Canada archives record that his method was later transmitted to future dowsers across North America, and that he explained the rod's indicative tremble by the capacity of intuition.
