# Alexis-Timothée Bouly (1865–1958): The Priest Who Named Radiesthesia

Alexis-Timothée Bouly (11 December 1865, Condette – 29 January 1958, Condette), known as l'abbé Bouly, was a French priest and radiesthésiste, known for practicing water divining and herbalism — and for having invented the very word *radiesthésie* that named the discipline.

## Origins and Clerical Career

Alexis-Timothée Bouly was born on 11 December 1865 in Condette, in the Pas-de-Calais: the son of François-Alexis Bouly, a cartwright (who became deputy mayor of Condette in 1889 and held the post until his death in 1909), and of Euphrasine Fauquembergue, a linen maid. His birth record spells his middle name "Thimothée"; today it is written "Timothée" — from the Latin *timeo* ("I fear") and the Greek *theós* ("God"): "he who fears God."

A gifted pupil, he studied at the communal school of Condette, then at the petit séminaire of Boulogne-sur-Mer (1879–1886), and finally the grand séminaire of Arras. He was ordained a priest on 13 July 1890, and taught for four years at the Institution Saint-Vaast in Béthune (teaching English and German at the school, recently founded in 1887 by Dean Cornet). In 1894 the diocese sent him to the Sorbonne to study modern letters, which is where he mastered English and German. He taught in parallel at the collège Saint-Stanislas of Boulogne-sur-Mer, becoming its head (supérieur) in 1900.

In January 1909 he was appointed priest of Wirwignes, at the church of Saint-Quentin — a post he held only eight months. On 1 August 1910 he was entrusted with the newly created parish of Hardelot-Plage, thanks to the support of John Whitley, the British businessman who had founded the seaside resort on the Côte d'Opale and had the new church built by the architect Louis Marie Cordonnier (who drew up the town plan and built many of its villas). Sited on the axis of the tennis courts, the church was placed under the patronage of Saint Augustine of Canterbury. Bouly would remain priest of Hardelot until his death.

Being the priest of a seaside resort left long idle seasons once the summer visitors were gone. The abbé had abundant time for study: he gathered herbs, and he took an interest in the sciences.

## The Dowser's Gift (1913)

In April 1913, Bouly met the de Lapparent family — whose patriarch, Albert de Lapparent (d. 1908), had been a geologist, member of the Académie des sciences, and president of the Société Géologique de France. Bouly discovered in himself a dowser's gift: "The rod turned better with me than with him." He decided to develop the aptitude rationally — to understand it and extend it. As he later put it: "Only through relentless work and constant practice can one achieve a result."

His reasoning, as the departmental archives record it: since every body — animal, vegetable, mineral — is nothing but an accumulation of energy whose vibrations spread throughout the universe, it must be possible to grasp and identify those radiations with a dowser's rod. After working on water-supply problems, he taught himself to search for cavities, for metals, and eventually for microbes.

He took part in the discovery and drilling of a great many wells, and acquired a wide reputation in the field. Notable finds:

- **Boulogne-sur-Mer**: at three meters' depth, he located the basin of the Saint-Jean baptistery of the former cathedral, for Monsignor Lejeune.
- **Roubaix**: he located water sources for the town's spinning mills.
- **The Canary Islands**: called over by the islands' government during a severe drought, he found several freshwater sources that allowed the planting of early crops and bananas — vital to the local economy. Every year thereafter, the Canaries sent crates of bananas to the abbé.
- **Lens (1923)**: the city asked his help for the reconstruction of the church of Saint-Léger, entirely destroyed by bombing on 19 January 1916. He averted a disaster by revealing hidden cavities filled with rubble beneath the planned foundations.
- **Royan (October 1923)**: consulted by Mayor Métadier to find a forgotten tin mine. The local heritage museum preserves a letter about the episode: the "prospector with the rod" first used the whalebone rib of an old umbrella, then a hazel wand, and asked only for reimbursement of his expenses and, occasionally, a donation to his charities.
- **Morzine (1940)**: he helped Messieurs Baud and Althuser locate a tin deposit in the region (Haute-Savoie).

**Wartime service.** In 1918 the Ministry of War called on him to locate and defuse unexploded shells on the battlefields of the Marne, the Aisne, and Artois. For these services to the Nation he was decorated with the Légion d'honneur in 1950.

## The Word "Radiesthésie" and the Founding of the AAR

Between 1925 and 1930, Bouly developed another competence: the search for microbes and human radiations — the beginnings of what historian Hervé Guillemain calls *radiesthésie médicale*, medical radiesthesia. Guillemain situates the move precisely: medical radiesthesia transposed onto the human body the search for the hidden and for fluids that water diviners had pursued for centuries; it extended the era's fascination with sound and light waves, at the very moment when wireless telegraphy and radioconduction were making waves material in everyday space.

It was through exchanges with his friend Abbé Louis Bayard (born and died at Hocquinghem, 1861–1948; professor at the petit séminaire of Boulogne, then from 1894 at the Facultés Catholiques of Lille) that Bouly coined the word *radiesthésie*: from the Latin *radius* ("rod," "ray of light") and the Greek-derived suffix *-esthésie* ("sensation," "perception") — the perception of radiations through the dowser's rod.

The term made its public appearance with the creation of the **Association française et internationale des amis de la radiesthésie** in Lille on **29 December 1929**, founded at the initiative of Léon Delattre and Théo Gravez — two friends who attended Bouly's lectures and asked him for an association to study and develop radiesthesia. Headquartered at 91 rue de l'Hôpital-Militaire in Lille, the association counted in its honorary committee some of the most prestigious names of French science:

- **Édouard Branly**, inventor of wireless telegraphy's coherer;
- **Henri Deslandres**, director of the Paris and Meudon observatories;
- **Arsène d'Arsonval**, founder of electrotherapy;
- **Dr. François Foveau de Courmelles**, pioneer of chromotherapy and radiography;
- **Dr. Gédéon Meillère**, who would preside over the Académie de médecine in 1932.

As founding president, Bouly delivered the inaugural lecture on 29 January 1930 before more than 500 people at the Lille hippodrome, with the viscount Henry de France presiding over the session; de France succeeded him at the head of the association a few weeks later. The association published a regular bulletin from 1930 on. Bouly was one of the movement's keystones: he multiplied contacts with reputed scientists, in the Catholic faculties of the north and at the Académie des sciences. Guillemain's verdict: he was in sum the founder of a new discipline — hugely popular, largely carried by amateurs, sitting at the crossroads of several scientific fields, generating new practices, gestures, and machines, and creating a wholly new network of study and sociability.

In one lecture Bouly declared: "The science of waves is the science of the future. In twenty years, it will have shed its light on domains where present-day investigation is extremely limited."

## The Healer of Hardelot

From 1930 on, while continuing his pastoral duties, Bouly refocused his activity on water-finding and medical diagnosis. He became a kind of healer who sought out microbes by radiesthesia. Thousands of people came from France, England, and Belgium to consult him at his home in Hardelot; his gifts as radiesthésiste and herbalist were said to help them. He gave lectures on his practice throughout France and abroad, and sold potions under his own brand. He devoted the financial ease this brought him to relieving poverty: he had two buildings constructed at the bottom of his property on the rue de la Marne to host, each summer, frail young Parisian girls convalescing by the sea.

Interested in local history and heritage, he created a small museum tracing the history of the Boulonnais. In 1934 he bought the **château d'Hardelot** — its last private owner — where he installed his immense library and his naturalist's collections. He later bequeathed the château to a charitable work, the sisters of Sainte-Agnès of Arras. He also acquired Les Tourelles in Condette for the sisters of Saint-Maur, the château des Dunes for the Pères Blancs (White Fathers), and properties on the rue de la Marne in Condette; local lore had it that he had found a treasure with his rod. A room in the château d'Hardelot still pays him homage, reconstructing his study.

## Death and Legacy

The abbé Bouly died on 29 January 1958 in Condette, aged 92. (Note: the Wirwignes parish page once gives 1956 as the year of death, but its own text elsewhere, the Wikipedia article, the departmental archives, and his age at death all confirm 1958.)

His figure has resisted time:

- **Professor Tournesol**: in a book devoted to Hergé's famous scientist (*Le Tournesol illustré*, Casterman 1994), Albert Algoud presents Abbé Mermet and Abbé Bouly as the inspirations for the radiesthetic experiments of Tintin's professor — Hergé's interest in the radiesthésistes of that era is well documented, and Guillemain opens his study with the same observation: Tournesol, who appears in the bande dessinée from 1943, owes much to them.
- **A bronze by the sculptor Pierre Stenne**, sealed on a stone plinth in the park of the château d'Hardelot, evokes his exceptional life.
- **The word itself**: *radiesthésie* — Bouly's coinage — became the standard French name for the discipline, and its association (the AAR) the institutional home of the French school, with the medical section Mermet would later preside.
