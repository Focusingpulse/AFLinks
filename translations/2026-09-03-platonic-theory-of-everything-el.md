               Stereometry, as a branch of Mathematical science, develops the imagination of

               During the golden age, that is, the 5th century BC, Stereometry was a field of
               intensive research by various mathematicians.
               The theory of polyhedra and especially the theory of convex polyhedra, by virtue
               of their high degree of symmetry, constitutes one of the most fascinating chapters
               of Stereometry. It was first studied by the Pythagoreans, by Plato in his Cosmology,
               and during the Renaissance period very meticulously by geometers, astronomers,
               and artists.
               In ancient Greece the idea developed to distinguish polyhedra into groups,
               according to certain special properties they exhibit.
               The group of polyhedra which have the property that all their faces are equal
               regular polygons and their polyhedral angles are equal, are called regular
               polyhedra. From the research of the Pythagoreans it emerged that the only
               regular polyhedra that can exist are five.
               The regular polyhedra are also called Platonic, because the Athenian philosopher
               Plato (4th c. BC) uses them in his dialogue "Timaeus" as models of the structural
               elements for the creation of the world.
               The Platonic solids are:
                   The regular tetrahedron with four (4) equal equilateral triangular faces.
                   The regular hexahedron (the cube) with six (6) equal square faces.
                   The regular octahedron with eight (8) equal equilateral triangular faces.
                   The regular dodecahedron with twelve (12) equal regular pentagonal faces.
                   The regular icosahedron with twenty (20) equal equilateral triangular faces.

The Principle of Indestructibility or Preservation of the Number of Like Elementary Right Triangles

The study of physical reality occupied the ancient Greek philosophers. The passage from myth to logos is accomplished with the pre-Socratic philosophers, who interpret physical phenomena in a rational manner. They attempt to give physical explanations without attributing the evolution of natural processes to supernatural factors and the intervention of the gods. Among the most interesting approaches to this question are those of the atomist philosophers (Leucippus and Democritus), Epicurus, and Plato.

According to Leucippus, all things consist of very small pieces of matter, which are infinite, invisible, complete, indivisible, unchangeable. Through the motion of atoms, everything is created.

Democritus supplemented and completed the atomic theory of Leucippus, saying that atoms are eternal, unchangeable entities and possess no sensible quality. They constitute the ultimate limit of divisibility of material bodies. They are numerically infinite and have material substance. Democritus accepts Leucippus's position that they differ in shape, motion, and the manner in which they touch one another.
