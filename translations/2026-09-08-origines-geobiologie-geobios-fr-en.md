# The Origins of Geobiology

> "Geobiology is a discipline of the future, one that draws its roots from deep in the past."
> — Hans Wilhelmsson, member of the Nobel Prize jury in Physics from 1974 to 1994, physicist, author of numerous reference works

## Geobiology: The Missing Link of Health — The Place

### Geobiology Has Crossed the Centuries

The term "geobiology" covers a reality known under different names ever since humans became sedentary, approximately 5,000 years ago: Feng Shui for the Chinese, Vaastu Shastra for the Indians, geomancy for the Persians. For the Celts, the "Vouivre" or wyvern is a kind of "fire serpent" diving into fountains or wells, carrying an idea of light and heat rising from the bowels of the earth.

All observed that the terrain on which one resides and its orientation influence health.

Throughout history, several writings and facts show us that geobiology has always played a fundamental role:

- **Hippocrates**, renowned as the father of modern medicine, declared in 430 BCE that one must pay as much attention to external causes as to constitutional factors to perfect the art of healing.
- **Claudius Vitruvius**, the famous Roman architect and military engineer, recounted in the 1st century BCE the importance of the subsoil.
- In France, Romanesque churches and, even more so, Templar churches bear witness to remarkable knowledge of geobiology.
- The **Etruscans** grazed their sheep for a year on a site before building, after examining the quality of the livers of some of the animals. The Roman soldiers used the same method to establish their fortified camps so as to be more valiant in combat.
- The builders of **Romanesque churches and Gothic cathedrals**, and the **Templars**, share a common feature: an "orientation" of the choir, an altar placed at the crossing of two underground watercourses. The entire nave-choir ensemble is oriented and positioned on a so-called "sacred" solar network.
- In Europe, it was the **dowsers** (sourciers) and **rhabdomancers** who were charged with finding springs, treasures, or mines. By signaling the presence of underground water, most often using a hazel-wood rod, they enabled inhabitants to avoid placing their beds directly above it.

Since 1937, numerous researchers have invested in understanding the impact of place on health:

- Dr. Peyré, Dr. Curry, Dr. Picard, Professor Yves Rocard in France,
- The researchers Karl-Ernst Lotz and Robert Endrös, Baron von Poll, and above all Dr. Ernst Hartmann, physician at the University of Heidelberg, who truly drove modern geobiology from the 1950s onward.

## Hippocrates, Father of Medicine, Is Also the Founder of Geobiology for the West

### The First Western Writing Concerning Geobiology Comes from Hippocrates, in 450 BCE

Although nomadic, the Native Americans had also understood the importance of place. They observed nervous reactions in their horses at specific locations and moved their tents away from them. They avoided pitching their tents on these spots, establishing camp only where their animals were calm. Through their experience of survival and work in hostile environments, they had arrived at conclusions that were not superstitions but, already, experimental realities.

### The Great Truths of Hippocrates and Our Ancestors

According to the famous Greek physician and philosopher, four factors matter for the health of individuals: their constitution, the air, the water, and the state of the subsoil. He declared in 430 BCE:

> "He who wishes to perfect himself in the art of healing will consider how the place is arranged and will inquire about the subsoil, concurrently with the constitution of the living beings."

— *Treatise on Airs, Waters, and Places*

But what did he mean by "state of the subsoil"? Was he referring to what the Chinese had already been practicing for more than a thousand years on their side with Feng Shui? Since the dawn of time, our ancestors knew that one must avoid sleeping directly above an underground watercourse. The Chinese called these "the veins of the dragon." This designation could also refer to other disturbances of subterranean origin.

Before our era, the Chinese knew nothing of the structure of the globe or the existence of the earth's crust. When a landslide exposed the large bones of a mammoth, they were astonished, having never seen such animals on the surface. From time to time, they observed the snout of a mole emerging from a small mound known in our parts as a "molehill." They understood, by plowing their fields, that these small animals dug galleries.

Consequently, the large remains must have belonged to large animals that also lived underground, whose molehills would be the volcanoes. They tried to represent these invisible animals in the form of a dragon, a mythical creature that breathed fire, which we call a volcanic eruption. And every time these large animals moved through their underground galleries, they created earthquakes.

This approach to reality may make us smile today, and we know that these interpretations are wrong. The causes were misunderstood in those times. However, the same is not true of the remedies. When a remedy is still in use after more than 3,000 years of experimentation, one can reasonably say that it must have some effect. This is the case with Feng Shui, as with acupuncture.

## Geobiology, Descendant of Traditional Feng Shui

The Forbidden City, the imperial palace of Beijing, was built in 1406. In addition to the finest architects of the era, its construction mobilized Feng Shui specialists, with the aim of bringing prosperity, security, and happiness to the emperors and their dynasty. Feng Shui (pronounced "Fong-Choy") means "wind and water." This art, created in China, as we have seen, more than 3,000 years ago, consists of choosing the best site and then finding the most favorable placement for tombs, cities, houses, rooms, and objects within dwellings.

This discipline aims to bring balance, harmony, and happiness to the occupants of a place. It takes into account orientation, forms, the spatial distribution of the terrain, and the horizontal circulation of wind and the energy it carries (the "Chi" or "Tch'i").

Several ancient writings attest to the antiquity of Chinese geomancy: during the Zhou dynasty (1121–221 BCE), a poem is dedicated to the hero Liu, who studied orientations relative to the sun. Prior to 235 BCE, under the Qin dynasty, a Taoist hermit called Chi Zhongzi (the Master of the Red Pine) taught Chinese geomancy.

At this same period (3rd century BCE), healing, longevity practices, and divination took into account the natural energies of the environment through the practice of Kanyu, the ancestor of Feng Shui. Five hundred years later, the *Zhanshu* (The Book of Burials), written during the Jin dynasty (276–420 CE), describes techniques inherited from early Feng Shui: favorable forms for tombs and landscape forms that avoid the stagnation of energies. Yan Junsong is considered one of the most remarkable *Sien-Cheng* (Feng Shui experts) of ancient times.

The Forbidden City in Beijing was elaborated under the supervision of imperial Feng Shui masters. It includes an artificial river that meanders through the outer courtyard to protect the central palaces.

Today, all the Chinatowns scattered across Western capitals make extensive use of Feng Shui. As for the prosperity of Hong Kong and Taiwan, some Chinese masters say it flows from the systematic application of Feng Shui principles, both in spatial arrangement and in the interior design of buildings.

### Vaastu Shastra Is Reputed to Be the Ancestor of Feng Shui

Its trace is found in the Vedas, the sacred books of Hinduism written in Sanskrit from 1800 BCE onward. This science of construction, highly prized by the maharajahs and the Mughal emperors, is the art of maintaining harmony between a building and the five elements: Sky, Earth, Water, Fire, and Wind. It takes into account the site, its orientation, forms, and interior layout. It is from India that this science of construction is said to have spread to Tibet, Southeast Asia, China, and Japan.

Known in the East as Feng Shui (Wind and Water), geobiology earned its letters of nobility from the Zhou dynasty in 1040 BCE onward.

The Chinese resort to it systematically, notably in the construction of Chinatowns around the world and in the siting of commercial and office buildings.

## Geobiology in the Lineage of Dowsers

Before piped water, no one considered digging a well without bringing in a dowser with the famous "dowsing rods," universally known.

In those days, not so long ago, wells were dug with shovels. A well had to be wide enough for a man to dig and climb back up using a ladder. Without motorized drilling equipment, it was preferable not to make a mistake. So a "rhabdomancer" (dowser) was asked to do his work before the well-digger started digging.

In less than half an hour, the local rhabdomancer was able to specify the exact spot to dig and the approximate depth at which water would be found.

The best dowsers were even able to provide an estimate of the flow rate. They worked by radiesthesia, using flexible wooden rods (hazel, witch hazel) or a pendulum.

## Geobiology Today

Geobiology is multidisciplinary. It draws on concepts from geology, physics (electric fields, magnetic fields, ionizing radiation, natural radioactivity), and biology.

The geobiologist sheds light on questions such as:

- Can I buy this plot of land?
- Where should I place my house to live in good health?
- How should I arrange my renovation?
- Where should I position my beds and seating to feel fit?
- Are there too many electromagnetic waves?
