A Universe without dark matter?

04.06.2018, by
Yaroslav Pigenet
Reading time: 11 minutes

Allegorical view of the Universe and dark matter.



Lynette COOK/SCIENCE PHOTO LIBRARY/COSMOS


Share

Share


---


We could solve at a stroke the double enigma of dark matter and dark energy by admitting that antimatter remains in our Universe and that it possesses negative mass. A bold hypothesis, but one that should soon be tested. The explanations in this article, published in issue 4 of the review "Carnets de science".


A little over a century after Einstein rid physics of the hypothesis that an unknown substance, the aether, filled Space, astrophysics and cosmology are once again confronted with a curious enigma: our Universe would be 95% filled with a dark matter and a dark energy whose origin and nature no one has so far been able to explain. Accepting the existence of a dark sector as pervasive as it is elusive remains, for a majority of physicists, the most obvious way to make the whole body of astrophysical data at their disposal compatible with the theory of general relativity. Except that here is the thing: after forty years of intensive hunting, no instrument and no experiment has allowed the observation of the slightest cosmic structure or the slightest particle that could take on the role of this invisible mass.

Without calling it a crisis, this situation is very frustrating for a discipline whose goal is to arrive at a theory exhaustively describing the content of our Universe and its evolution. The physicist Gabriel Chardin, who received the CNRS Silver Medal in 2007 for his work on dark matter, notably within the international Edelweiss 1 collaboration, believes it may be possible to find a way out by envisaging, as Einstein did in his time, a new breach with common sense: accepting that negative masses exist in our Universe, just as positive and negative electric charges exist. From there, he envisions a new cosmological model with a Universe containing as much matter as antimatter, in which there is no longer any need to invoke the concepts of dark matter or dark energy. "This Universe, developed over the past ten years with Aurélien Benoit-Lévy, is still very far from being accepted as an alternative to the standard cosmological model, with dark matter and dark energy, concedes Gabriel Chardin, who develops his ideas in detail in the essay L'Insoutenable gravité de l'Univers (The Unbearable Gravity of the Universe). But it does not rest only on speculation: it reproduces, in a rather astonishing way, a large number of the data we possess, and it makes predictions that will be testable in the coming years, or even the coming months."

Anomalies in the Coma Berenices (Berenice's Hair)
But for what reasons did the scientific community come to consider the existence of a dark sector as necessary? And what are the reasons driving a growing number of researchers today to consider alternative hypotheses? The first to have raised, as early as 1933, the hypothesis of an invisible mass was the Swiss astrophysicist Fritz Zwicky (1898-1974). While observing the Coma Berenices, where he was measuring the velocities of galaxies orbiting in the Coma cluster, he calculated that the gravity exerted by visible matter could not compensate for the centrifugal force acting on these galaxies: they should have been flung out of the cluster as from a merry-go-round spinning too fast. He deduced the presence of an obscure matter whose mass was far greater than that of visible matter. More than thirty years passed before his intuition was confirmed by the study Vera Rubin conducted on rotation profiles inside galaxies. The only way to explain this profile within the framework of the laws of gravity was to admit that these galaxies were immersed in a halo of dark matter six times heavier than their visible content. These results were subsequently confirmed, and many other independent observations accumulated evidence in favor of the existence of dark matter. Though concordant, these various pieces of evidence are nonetheless indirect. All the more so since none of the known forms of dark matter (planets, neutrinos, black holes) seemed able to contribute significantly to this excess of mass, dark matter can only belong to physics still unknown.

Modifying the laws of gravity
This matter, or the mysterious particles that compose it, physicists the world over have therefore been hunting for forty years, whether in Space, in accelerators like the LHC, or with the help of increasingly sensitive detectors. The trouble is that they have so far discovered nothing. This admittedly allows theorists to constrain a little further the possible characteristics of this exotic matter, but it also raises growing doubts as to its existence. As early as 1983, the Israeli physicist Mordehai Milgrom realized that there would be no need at all to invoke the slightest dark matter... on condition that Newton's laws of gravity be modified. Noting that these laws have in fact only been tested and verified in the relatively strong gravitational fields of our Solar System, he proposed to rework them when they apply to bodies moving in very weak fields, those that reign in interstellar space.

"This theory, baptized Mond (Modified Newtonian Dynamics), does predict the velocity profile in galaxies. The problem is that at this stage we have no means of testing it experimentally," notes Gabriel Chardin, who is also president of the CNRS Committee on Very Large Research Infrastructures. "The most distant craft from us, the Voyager probe, is subjected to a gravitational field a hundred million times weaker than the one it was subjected to on the surface of the Earth, but that is still a thousand times stronger than what it would experience in so-called 'weak' fields!"

Lacking this experimental proof, the majority of researchers hesitate to call into question laws which, since Einstein, have never been found wanting. All the more so since even if Mond can rid us of dark matter, this theory is in any case incapable of answering a new enigma posed by gravity: the discovery in 1998 of the acceleration of the expansion of the Universe, interpreted as the manifestation of a kind of repulsive gravity.

Since the nature of this mysterious phenomenon remained unexplained, physicists treated it like dark matter: they added it to the list of their "known unknowns" and measured its effects. They named this new mysterious component dark energy. The measurements of the Planck satellite indicate that this dark energy today represents a little more than two-thirds of the energy contained in the Universe, dark matter about a quarter, and known matter barely a twentieth. By accepting this curious composition, cosmologists were able to elaborate the standard cosmological model. A model which, while remaining compatible with general relativity and observations, strives to describe in a coherent manner the history of the Universe from the Big Bang to the present day.

Antimatter in the intergalactic vacuum?
"If it remains the reference solution for the majority of physicists, in my view as in the minds of a growing number of my colleagues, the standard cosmological model has several very inelegant aspects," nuances Gabriel Chardin. "It works rather well, it's true, but it appears less and less plausible in its composition, and it counts too many free parameters, which must be readjusted with each new observation. In short, something is wrong with the Universe it describes..." And it is for this reason that new models have recently been proposed by theorists such as André Maeder, of the University of Geneva, or Thomas Buchert, of the Lyon Astrophysical Research Center 2: Universes needing neither dark energy nor dark matter to explain current observations.

This is also the case for the Dirac-Milne Universe elaborated by Gabriel Chardin. He bases it on two revolutionary hypotheses. First, he considers that this Universe contains as much matter as antimatter, and that antimatter possesses a negative gravitational mass. "The total, at least apparent, disappearance of antimatter from our Universe remains to this day another of the great mysteries of the standard model: yet it indicates that the Universe contained, during its very first moments after the Big Bang, as much matter as antimatter," he insists. "In the Dirac-Milne Universe, there is no longer any need to explain the disappearance of antimatter: it is still there, hidden in the intergalactic voids." The supposition that antimatter possesses a negative mass is a plausible supposition, because to this day no experiment has been able to measure the mass of antimatter. "At the atomic scale, if we compare it to electromagnetism or to the kinetic energy of particles, the effect of gravity is negligible," explains the physicist. "We have known how to produce antihydrogen atoms for more than twenty years, but we have only just found the means to slow them down sufficiently to be able to weigh them soon." Negative masses in the Universe?
Theorists have in any case demonstrated that the existence of negative masses is perfectly compatible with general relativity, even if the motions these masses would have relative to one another are very strange: while positive masses clump together, negative masses repel each other and spread apart! "In such a Universe, under the effect of gravity, matter — of positive mass — clumps together and forms large structures. By contrast, antimatter particles, of negative mass, spread out into a cold gas in what we take to be great cosmic voids." Voids which, as they spread out and repel the positive-mass structures, would explain, without any need for dark energy, the expansion of the Universe as it is observed.

Moreover, the fact of supposing that both positive- and negative-mass particles exist has another unexpected effect: it creates a polarization of the vacuum which modifies the equations of gravity. These then present a great similarity with those of the Mond theory. One can therefore dispense with the dark matter hypothesis to explain the rotation of galaxies. "As things stand, the Dirac-Milne Universe model remains highly speculative, but the values it arrives at are astonishingly close to those already measured by the great observation programs, and it is going to be directly tested in the coming years, or perhaps even by the end of 2018, thanks to the Gbar, Alpha-g and AEgIS experiments at Cern, which will at last measure the gravitational mass of antimatter," assures Gabriel Chardin. "We will then know whether the proposed explanation of a matter-antimatter Universe, guided by its arguments of mathematical and physical elegance, is indeed confirmed by experiment." ♦

Weighing antimatter: a heavy task!
Antimatter would possess a negative mass and would behave differently from matter when subjected to gravity. This hypothesis, proposed by the physicist Gabriel Chardin, is plausible, but very bold. For while we now master the production of antiprotons in accelerators fairly well, "weighing" these negatively charged antiparticles is almost impossible, so negligible is the effect of gravity compared to that of the electromagnetic interaction. One way of neutralizing this force consists in creating neutral atoms of antihydrogen by combining an antiproton and a positron (anti-electron). The difficulty then lies in managing to confine and slow these anti-atoms before they encounter atoms of matter and annihilate. Using a magnetic trap, the Alpha collaboration at Cern recently succeeded in confining a thousand antihydrogen atoms for several hours. By the end of the year, Alpha will attempt to determine exactly what happens when these anti-atoms are "released" into a gravitational field. To be continued...
