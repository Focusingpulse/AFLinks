# A Scientific Blunder: When a Great Physicist Investigates the "Powers" of Dowsers — Denis Machon on Yves Rocard's Dowser Experiments

*English digest by Forge. Original: "Bavure scientifique : quand un grand physicien se penche sur les « pouvoirs » des sourciers", The Conversation France, 1 August 2026, by Denis Machon (INSA Lyon – Université de Lyon).*

**Machon's thesis:** Some people claim to detect underground water — the *sourciers* (dowsers). Yves Rocard, one of France's great physicists, proposed an experiment to test their supposed "power." When you study his method and his analysis of his own results, serious biases appear — enough to conclude this was a *scientific blunder* (*bavure scientifique*), not a fraud.

## The man

Yves Rocard belongs to the class of France's "great scientists." He helped develop new disciplines — semiconductors, NMR (RMN), radio astronomy — leading major research programs in prestigious institutions: professor at the École Normale Supérieure, director of its physics laboratory, scientific advisor to the CEA. (He is also the physicist the French radiesthesia tradition most often cites as its scientific patron — which is exactly why Machon's critique matters for the corpus.)

## An audacious hypothesis, a seductive experiment

From 1957, Rocard undertook an unusual scientific study: understanding the origin of dowsers' sensitivity — their claimed ability to detect underground water. The initiative may seem surprising, but it showed open-mindedness and attacked a concrete problem that magnetism might explain. His whole approach — experiments, results, interpretations — is detailed in his book *La science et les sourciers* [Science and the Dowsers].

**The hypothesis for the "dowser's signal":** local variations of the Earth's magnetic field are induced by the circulation of underground water, and certain humans are sensitive to them.

**The protocol:** generate a controlled magnetic field with a current-carrying wire. The subject (a person claiming dowsing abilities) stands near the wire holding a pendulum. If the subject is magnetically sensitive, the field should induce muscular movements and thus rotation of the pendulum; reversing the current direction should reverse the rotation. The experiment takes place in the countryside, in an isolated house, to minimize "magnetic" pollution. First a calibration: for each subject, the current direction is announced, the pendulum is allowed to stabilize, and the direction of rotation is observed for each current direction.

Of five subjects initially selected, only three showed a response to magnetic fields — pendulum rotation when current flowed, and reversal when the current reversed. Machon notes the small sample already limits the statistical reach of any conclusion.

## The biases pile up

**1. Blind testing improvised too late.** Rocard's own report admits the blind experiments were not his idea but were demanded by a young assistant, a well-trained physicist — and nothing had been prepared for them: "We were pressed for time, everyone wanting soon to return to Paris." An experiment run in haste has little chance of being significant. Blind procedures have been known to be essential in experimental physics at least since 1904 and the unfortunate case of the N rays. Without them, subjects — even in good faith — can unconsciously make the pendulum's rotation match the calibration direction. Thanks to the assistant's insistence, Rocard did eventually allow the current to be reversed "…without the subject's knowledge."

**2. Human "randomness" instead of objective chance.** The choice of current direction was left to the operator's discretion: "The random choice is at the operator's discretion; he, being very honest, is quite convinced he is playing at random, and we are prepared to admit this point without discussion." Machon: this forgets that humans are very poor random generators and prone to bias. A predictable alternation of current direction contaminates the statistical analysis. The protocol needed objective chance — coin flips.

**3. Small arrangements with the data.** After the trials on the three subjects, the results were **5/10, 4/10, and 4/10** — very close to what pure chance would give (one chance in two of matching the current direction). Rocard himself concluded: "This experiment leads to a quite visible failure." Machon questions even this framing: with no preconceived result expected, it is rather a *success* — the experiment clearly answers the question "Does the magnetic field influence the direction of the pendulum's rotation?"

But then Rocard's analysis drifts onto a path that is no longer scientific:

- *"One observes without difficulty that the results would have been better if the series had been shorter. If we agree, for example, to reduce the series to the first four trials only, the three subjects improve considerably: the answers become correct in the proportion of 3/4, 3/4 and 2/4 — obviously better."* — Reducing the number of trials taken into account reduces statistical significance. Pushed to its paroxysm: consider only the first trial, and subjects 1 and 3 have a 100% success rate. As for subject 2 — perhaps badly disposed that day…

- *"It must also be noted that each series was immediately preceded by two trial shots which also reinforced the magnetic imprinting. For each series of four trials, one can therefore add two which are always a success, giving, for series of 4+2, success proportions of 5/6, 5/6 and 4/6."* — Those two "trial shots" are the calibration measurements, which succeeded by construction — they could not not succeed. Machon's analogy: it is as if, before a football World Cup match, the referee had team B shoot twice at the unguarded opposing goal to verify the video refereeing works, then, after a nil-nil draw, added those two goals to team B's score.

## Bavure, not fraude

In this re-reading of the data we witness a drift intended to validate the scenario decided in advance — an experiment conceived as a demonstration of the announced effect. Nevertheless, unlike fraud, everything is displayed, presented, and not concealed — which is precisely what characterizes a *scientific blunder*. Rocard even closes with: "Why not? If this procedure had been agreed before the experiments, everyone would have accepted it." It was not.

**Machon's conclusion:** Scientific rigor depends not only on researchers' competence but on the methods they apply. Methodology counts more than the author's name. Science is above all a process: a badly designed experiment proves nothing… except that it is badly designed.

## Why this matters for the corpus

Rocard's NMR-era physics credentials made him the single most-cited "hard science" authority in the French radiesthesia literature (the astroya history page, the Vibratis guides, and multiple corpus docs lean on his and Harvalik's geomagnetic-sensitivity work). Machon's critique does not dismiss the geomagnetic hypothesis itself — it shows Rocard's own experimental support for it was unsound: non-blind, non-randomized, small-n, and rescued post-hoc. The corpus should therefore treat "Rocard proved dowsing sensitivity to magnetic fields" as **not established by Rocard's own experiments** — the honest status of the claim rests on later work (biogenic magnetite, Kirschvink-style replication attempts) rather than on La science et les sourciers.
