1 Introduction
1.1 Starting Position
The human mind strives in science and philosophy to recognize a unified law within the diverse. Even in ancient wisdom teachings, reference is made to the vortex or spiral as an archetypal phenomenon and as a bridge between the visible and the invisible. In nature, nothing moves in straight lines — all movement is spiral and can be regarded as a vortex. Vortex flows are thus omnipresent in nature and technology.

In the middle of the last century, the vortex even stood at the forefront of scientific investigation of matter. Like a torch, it was able to show a whole generation of physicists the way toward a unified understanding of light and matter. At the beginning of the 20th century, however, through a series of remarkable events in the world of physics, this illumination was lost — and quantum mechanics and relativity theory became the path and paradigm of official physics. Yet despite the indisputable successes of this path, more and more researchers recognize that fundamental questions remain open and that physics still relates only to the material side of our reality.

In parallel to official science, creative outsiders have always developed alternative ideas and concepts. And so in the field of vortices, many new ideas have emerged over the last 150 years, and some have even been put into practice. Unfortunately, these researchers mostly had to operate outside mainstream science and therefore had difficulty publishing their findings. As a result, their ideas are often inadequately documented, poorly accessible, and scarcely processed.

If we expect specialists of conventional physics to assess these ideas without prejudice for their scientific merit, these outsider ideas must first be brought into a readable, logically consistent, and coherent form.


And this task is now being undertaken with this compendium.

The motivation to actively promote vortex physics is based on the hope that through the reanimation, investigation, and further development of the old vortex and ether concepts, an important contribution can be made to expanding natural science from the purely material into higher planes of existence. In this way, vortex physics could develop into a key for implementing our holistic understanding of Free Energy and gain research-strategic significance.

May this work serve as a starting point for those called to examine and further develop the vortex concept.

1.2 Scope and Objectives
The domain of science is limited by its methodology to a small section of total reality. The purpose of scientific methodology is to continually expand this domain and to wrest ever more territory from the outer region that is not yet scientifically graspable (but in everyday life has long been familiar to humans).

Compendium Goal:

                       with provision of the minimally necessary inner foundations.

1.3 Approach and Focus
The compendium will understandably take on the character of an eclectic summary, i.e., from everywhere the best material on the topic of vortices will be brought into a logical context, and an attempt will be made to work out the fundamental concepts in a didactically understandable way.

In doing so, we pursue a combined approach:
• On the one hand, we collect material per vortex researcher, and on the other hand, we attempt to build a hierarchically ordered topic overview of vortex physics from this material.
• With this simultaneous author-based and topic-based presentation, we strive for a comparative overall view of vortex physics. The redundancies that inevitably arise in the process are not considered disadvantageous but rather didactically meaningful.

For the hierarchical topic overview, the basic hypotheses lead organically to the following chapter structure, oriented toward the utility of the vortex concept:

The motion hypothesis ("we move incorrectly — all motion is spiral") refers to vortices in material media (gases, liquids, solids) and is to be treated in the chapter "Natural Technology" with the aim of improving technical-biological processes.

For the ether vortex hypothesis ("elementary particles are ether vortices"), the chapter "Deepening of Physics" will first examine whether it can provide new or deeper explanations for previously poorly understood relationships. Then, in the chapter "Extension of Physics," the potential of the vortex concept will also be demonstrated for phenomena from the domain of the mental-living and made accessible to physical research.

1.5 The Reader's Fundamental Attitude
Constructive engagement with outsider topics presupposes the moral quality of intellectual honesty:
• We should be willing to revise any of our views,
  i.e., "be open to the (currently still) impossible";
• We should change a view when there is a compelling reason to do so;
• We should not, however, change a view wantonly without good reason.

This inductive attitude aims to adapt our views to reality as appropriately as possible.

Since in the field of Free Energy much is operated with half-knowledge and hope, it is first necessary to cleanly think through, formulate, and understand the theoretical foundations. But the best theory is worth less than the paper it is printed on if it cannot be put into practice. And so summary documents like this can only ever serve as a basis to inspire interested readers to prove the described theories through practical implementation. For only practical confirmation through concrete experiments and demonstrable devices will provide those compelling reasons needed to help the visionary ideas so necessary for our world achieve broad breakthrough.

2.1 Philosophical-Spiritual Thoughts
For a work of the alternative science scene, it is important to mention the necessary underlying and complementary philosophical attitudes.
Physics is still searching for the "Theory of Everything (TOE)," the all-encompassing and all-founding theory. In particular, it concerns the unification of the 4 fundamental forces (electromagnetism, strong and weak interaction, as well as gravitation).
However, this materialistic approach to physics has moved so far from nature that it barely appears in the calculations of theoretical physics anymore (supersymmetries, string theories in 10 dimensions, etc.). Abstract mathematical, yet merely descriptive derivations have replaced intuitive models that can truly explain physical relationships. Thus we feel that modern physics is trapped in a dead end of the material level.
According to Fritjof Capra (The Cosmic Dance, 1977), today's natural scientists know the branches of the tree of knowledge, but not its root. The mystics, on the other hand, know the root of the tree of knowledge, but not its branches. From this we conclude that a future natural science will have to concentrate primarily on the integration of the root.
The terms ether, energy, illusion (Maya), vortex, etc. originate from this root area. We therefore assume that for the deepening and expansion of physics, fertilization from esoteric philosophy is needed in 3 points:
1. The step from material elementary particles to the next higher level of being of immaterial ether substance
2. The step from dead to living matter (hylozoism), by recognizing consciousness in ether substance
3. Development of consciousness in order to recognize that through the ether level everything in the universe is interconnected — or in the words of physicist David Bohm: Everything is One
Thus the merging of natural and spiritual sciences ... back into the lap of religion (re-ligio = reconnection of humans to their origin, unity).

2.3 Mathematical Fundamentals

In place of the ordinary differential equations that govern the mechanics of systems with finitely many degrees of freedom (the rigid body!), partial differential equations appear here, and in place of vector algebra, proper vector and tensor analysis.
In this chapter, the mathematical concepts, definitions, transformation rules, theorems, etc. required for the further exposition are provided as a review.

• Vector field quantities by bold capital letters.
• t always denotes time.
• x, y, z as indices denote the components of a vector in a Cartesian coordinate system

2.3.3 Vector fields

2.3.3.1 The concept of a field
The concept of a field is fundamental to the description of physical phenomena. One speaks of a field when one or more measured quantities are assigned to each point of a spatial region at each instant of time.
A distinction is made by the number of these measured quantities:
1. Scalar field S,
   given by exactly 1 measured quantity (incl. the unit of measurement)
   (e.g. temperature, density, electric potential, etc.)

2.5.2 Kinematics
Kinematics is the study of motion, i.e. it is limited to the purely geometric description of motion conditions, omitting the forces that cause the motions.

2.5.2.1 Fundamental theorem of kinematics
Helmholtz stated at the beginning of his famous work on vortex motion in 1858:
The general displacement of a deformable body can be represented for a sufficiently small volume of it as the sum of
1. a translation
2. a rotation
3. and a deformation (stretching or compression) along three mutually perpendicular directions
In this general sense, the vortex is a fundamental form of motion alongside translational and deformational motion.

The prerequisite for this fundamental theorem is the continuum hypothesis, i.e. the assumption that the space filled with matter can be regarded as a continuum. This means that the state variables of matter such as temperature, density, or pressure, as well as the laws determining these state variables, do not lose their meaning when the volume of the matter particle is arbitrarily reduced.

2.5.2.2 Material and field description
The most obvious way to describe a fluid is to mathematically describe the path of each point or fluid particle. This approach is called the material or Lagrangian description.

2.5.2.3 Pathline, streamline, streakline
The pathline results from following the path of a fluid particle over a certain period of time.

To introduce the streamline, imagine the following experiment:
We suspend particles in a flowing fluid, preferably shiny or colored ones. On an instantaneous photograph (e.g. with 0.1 s exposure time) under side lighting, each particle traces a short line whose length and direction indicate the local flow velocity V (though the direction cannot be easily read from the image). The entire flow is thus described by the set of all these vectors, the vector field V(r). The velocity vectors V, drawn at sufficiently many points, join together to form streamlines, of which they are the tangent vectors.
The significance of pathlines in the material description corresponds to that of streamlines in the field description.

In the simplest case of a vortex, the streamlines are closed.

2.5.3 Statics

2.5.3.1 Classification of deformable media
Deformable media are first divided into fluids and elastic solids.
A fluid differs from a solid mainly in that it cannot sustain a shear or shear stress, not even for the shortest times. If one applies a shear to a fluid, it moves under its influence. Thicker fluids such as honey move less easily than fluids like water or air. The measure of the ease with which a fluid yields is its viscosity.
When viscosity vanishes, one speaks of a frictionless or ideal fluid. However, for real fluids it is almost never the case that internal friction or viscosity can be neglected, for most of the interesting things that can happen depend in one way or another on viscosity.
Furthermore, fluids can be incompressible (e.g. water) or compressible (e.g. gases).

2.5.3.2 Hydrostatics
Statics is the study of the forces acting on matter. In deformable media, a distinction is made between external and internal forces, the latter defined by the fact that within the system under consideration they satisfy the principle of action and reaction:
• External forces include in particular gravity, centrifugal force (although actually belonging to dynamics as an inertial effect), as well as capillary forces, which are transmitted from internal molecules to the surface molecules.
• Internal forces include in particular the reactions (stresses, pressures) caused by the spatial occupancy of matter, i.e. its macroscopic and microscopic structure.

The summation of internal and external forces to zero is the condition for a fluid to be in hydrostatic equilibrium.

Introduction
Phenomenological thermodynamics dispenses with any deeper assumption about the nature of heat and thus with an atomistic interpretation of the thermal behavior of bodies. As a result, its statements are independent of any change in ideas about the microscopic structure of matter.

2.7 Electrodynamics

2.7.1 Basic concepts
Magnetism
   The first description of stones from the region of Magnesia with mysterious powers comes from the Greek Magnus (ca. 900 BC). Every material body is fundamentally magnetic. The measurable magnetic force effect between two bodies is today divided into four groups:
   Diamagnetism: Weak force effect; the bodies repel each other; the cause is the magnetic moment of the electrons in the body (electrons in orbit). All substances are diamagnetic in all states of aggregation. However, diamagnetism only appears when it is not masked by the other, stronger magnetic properties of paramagnetism and ferromagnetism. Example: bismuth.
   Paramagnetism: Weak force effect; the bodies attract each other; the cause is the spin moment of the electrons (intrinsic rotation of the electrons). Paramagnetic substances have a magnetic moment aligned with the external field. Example: platinum.
   Ferromagnetism: Strong force effect; the bodies form magnetic dipoles; the cause is aligned spin moments of electrons. Ferromagnetic substances have a high degree of geometric order (crystal lattice). Ferromagnetism is only possible in solids. Examples: iron, nickel, cobalt.
   Antiferromagnetism: Strong force effect; the bodies repel each other; the cause is pairwise antiparallel aligned spin moments of electrons. This type of magnetism is also only possible in solids. Example: manganese ions.

   Thales of Miletus rubs an amber rod (amber in Greek: elektron) on cat fur around 600 BC and then picks up chicken feathers from the ground with the rod. 2.7.2 Fluid-dynamic analogy to the electric field
In 1856, Maxwell compared the electrostatic field lines with the streamlines in the flow field of an irrotational (rot v = 0), frictionless fluid (potential flow). An electric charge q can be illustrated by a spatial source or sink flow. For a source E at the coordinate origin, in spherical coordinates (r0: radius measured from the origin):

   Potential field:                                  Φ(r0) = −    E / (4π r0)

   Velocity field:                                   v(r0) = ∇Φ =  E / (4π r0²)

The productivity E denotes the source strength, which is independent of the radius of the sphere. Analogously, for a positive or negative electric charge:

   Potential field:                                  ϕ(r0) = −    Q / (4π r0)

   Electric field:                                   E(r0) = ∇ϕ =  Q / (4π r0²)

The comparison shows that the electric charge can be compared with the source strength and the electric field with the velocity field of a stationary source or sink flow. The electric field lines correspond in this (stationary) analogy to the streamlines. Because rot v = rot (grad Φ) ≡ 0 or analogously rot E = rot (grad ϕ) ≡ 0, the electric field is also described as irrotational or curl-free.

2.7.3 The magnetic effect of current
In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted discovered that compass needles align perpendicular to current-carrying conductors. Only a week after Oersted, Ampère published his discovery that current-carrying conductors exert forces on each other; parallel conductor currents attract, antiparallel conductor currents repel.
In the same year, Jean-Baptiste Biot and Felix Savart from Paris showed that the intensity of the newly discovered magnetic field around a current-carrying conductor decreases with the proportionality 1/r and that the field vectors are oriented perpendicular to the conductor. The relationship between current I and magnetic field H at distance r is:
   H = (I / 2πr) × r̂
The Biot-Savart law, valid for arbitrarily shaped conductors, has the general form for a connection vector r-ri between the current element I⋅ds and an observer:
   dH_i = (I ds_i / 4π|r-ri|²) × (r-ri)/|r-ri|

If one winds a conductor loop into a closed circuit, one obtains the same field as with a magnet. This corresponds to the equivalence between a circular current and a magnet, which was used, for example, in section 2.7.1 Basic concepts to explain the different types of magnetism.
If a current-carrying conductor is exposed to a magnetic field, a force acts on this conductor. The force exerted by a magnetic field on a current element is perpendicular to both the current element and the magnetic field.
   dF_i = µ0 I ds_i × H
If the current I is considered as the movement of charge carriers q, all moving with drift velocity v, then the sum of all forces on these charge carriers reveals the relationship between the so-called Lorentz force and the velocity v of a charge carrier:
   F = µ0 q v × H = q v × B

2.7.4 The fluid-dynamic analogy to the magnetic field — The vector potential
The second fluid-dynamic analogy is also given by Maxwell. Not coincidentally, in the same period when Maxwell derived his theories based on the assumption of an ether, Helmholtz also developed his theories of vortex flow (1858). The description of a magnetic field outside a straight, current-carrying wire is analogous to a planar potential vortex. The vortex strength Γ equals the current I in the wire. The wire itself corresponds to the vortex filament.

   Velocity field of a planar potential vortex:                  v(r0) =  Γ / (2π r0)

   Magnetic field around a straight, current-carrying conductor:  H(r0) =  I / (2π r0)

These equations apply to the magnitudes of the vortex fields. The cause of the vortex (rotation vector of a rigid body or electric current direction) is perpendicular to the vortex motion. The direction of the cause determines the direction of rotation of the vortex. To find a direction-dependent fluid-dynamic description of the magnetic field, the direction of the cause is included differentially:

   Differential of the vector potential:                          dA(r0) =  µ0 I ds / (4π r0)

The vector potential points in the same direction as the cause (electric current). Its magnitude is proportional to the magnetic field (potential vortex). Note that this definition was made by Maxwell out of pure expediency, so that the direction of action can be correctly accounted for as a function of the direction of the cause. That this definition of the vector potential A can also be interpreted as analogous to a real flow has been mentioned many times since. The relationship between magnetic field and vector potential is defined by the better-known expression
   B = µ0 H = ∇ × A.

2.7.5 Electromagnetic induction
Michael Faraday showed in 1831 that alternating currents in one electric circuit induce a current in a neighboring circuit. If the second circuit is open, a voltage is induced at the wire ends. Faraday built the first induction motor. When a conductor moves through a magnetic field, a voltage is induced in it according to
   E = v × B
If a magnetic field H changes, its induction B and magnetic flux Φ (flux = induction / area) also change. This also leads to the induction of a voltage or electric field:
                         dΦ
   ∮ E_ind ⋅ ds = −    dt

If one considers two superimposed current windings, one has the simplest coupling circuit. A current pulse in the lower winding (let us call this the primary winding) generates an alternating magnetic field, which leads to an induction in the upper winding (let us call this the secondary winding). If this secondary winding is short-circuited so that a current can flow, it also builds up a magnetic field, which acts back on the primary winding. This is the mutual induction observed by Faraday. According to a rule by Lenz, it always opposes the cause. The mutual induction is thereby reduced. All electrical machines, from the transformer to the synchronous motor, are based on this principle of induction and mutual induction.
In 1834, Faraday also discovered the self-induction of a conductor onto itself. The time-varying magnetic field of a likewise time-varying current thus also acts back on the current that generates the magnetic field. Every electrical conductor is therefore also an inductance, i.e. it is capable of storing energy in its surrounding magnetic field.

3.2 What Is a Vortex?

The answer to this is neither simple nor unambiguous. Without doubt, vortex motion has to do with rotating
matter, and one must distinguish here whether an individual particle of matter rotates about itself
or whether it rotates together with many others about a common center. First, the self-rotation
of a particle is disregarded and its trajectory is compared with that of neighboring particles.

Then the following definition 1 applies:
      A vortex is understood as the circular motion of a multitude of
      matter particles about a common center.

Since these matter particles occupy a certain space, the vortex forms a macroscopic flow
pattern. The center need not be only a point or a line, but can be a finite space.

In contrast to vortex definition 1, which is based on the trajectories of the matter particles, the
flow behavior at a point in space can also be used for a vortex definition. In this case, one assigns
an angular velocity to each point in space.

This leads to the following definition 2:
      The double angular velocity 2ω of matter at a point in space within
      a continuum is called vorticity (vortex strength) Ω and is expressed
      by the curl of the velocity vector: Ω = ∇ × v.

There is no vortex without vorticity, while a vorticity field need not be a vortex (e.g., parallel
shear flow). Since almost every flow has vorticity, this concept is therefore too general for the
treatment of vortices in the sense of definition 1. Nevertheless, the concept of vorticity cannot be
dispensed with, because it is necessary for understanding the formation and propagation of vortices.

The seemingly simple question "What is a vortex?" could only be answered scientifically correctly
on a topological basis at a late date (1979 for ideal and 1995 for viscous fluids).

3.4 Vortex Laws

3.4.1 Vorticity / Vortex Strength

For review:
The vorticity or vorticity vector is defined as the vector field Ω = ∇ × V derived by taking the curl
of the velocity field V of a fluid.

If the vorticity is zero everywhere, the flow is called irrotational.

In the special case of a rotating rigid body, the vorticity amounts to twice the
angular velocity.

As the simplest vortex structure, the vortex tube is of particular interest; it consists of several
side-by-side vortex lines, while outside the vortex tube the vorticity vanishes everywhere.

3.4.2 Circulation

The concept of circulation was introduced by Lord Kelvin in 1869.
It is defined as the line integral of the velocity field along a closed curve.
Transforming this line integral using Stokes' integral theorem leads to the linkage of circulation
with vorticity.

(Note: Bauer uses the terms circulation and vortex strength synonymously [Bauer, K10/p.2])

3.4.3 The Vorticity Induction Equation

If the velocity field V is given, the vorticity field can be derived from its definition by a
differentiation process: Ω = ∇ × V.
This leads to the question of the inverse process:
Given a vorticity field, find the velocity field.

As a result, one obtains the vorticity induction equation, which is well known in electrodynamics as
the Biot-Savart law. There is thus a complete analogy between a vortex filament in an incompressible
fluid and a current-carrying wire: the circulation corresponds to the current intensity, and the
flow velocity corresponds to the magnetic field strength in both direction and magnitude.

3.4.5 Helmholtz's Vortex Theorems

The physical content of the vorticity transport equation was expressed by Helmholtz in his 1858
work on vortex motions with the help of 3 theorems in words [Spurk]:

1.    The circulation of a vortex tube is constant along this tube — i.e., spatially constant —
      meaning vortex lines are either self-closed or they begin and end at
      boundary surfaces.

2.    A vortex tube always consists of the same fluid particles
      i.e., a vortex tube is a material tube.
      (this is why smoke remains in the smoke ring during smoke rings and is transported with it)

3.    The circulation of a vortex tube remains constant in time
      i.e., vortices can neither be created nor destroyed.

(Scope: Incompressible and inviscid fluids!)

3.4.6 Kelvin's Circulation Theorem

Lord Kelvin was able to generalize the 2nd and 3rd Helmholtz theorems in 1869 to the class of
inviscid barotropic fluids. A barotropic fluid is restrictedly compressible in the sense that its
density is a unique function of pressure.

The fact that circulation is thus often a conserved quantity, i.e., its time derivative is zero,
provides in many cases an explanation for the peculiar and unexpected behavior of vortices
and vortex motions.

or circulation adheres to the material particles and drifts with them in unchanged strength.

4.1 Wilhelm Bauer

4.1.1 The Man and His World

Wilhelm Moritz Bauer was born in 1911 in Vienna, studied after his school-leaving examination at the then
Technical College for Mechanical Engineering and Electrical Engineering in Vienna, and went after the 1st
state examination to Moscow to help build the metro. On the side, he filed patents, e.g., on the
protective circuit for power lines and for a vacuum pump. After the 2nd World War, after various
professional activities, he took up the study of physics, which took him via Zurich and Munich
to Berlin. At the age of 59, in 1970, he submitted his self-chosen thesis "Integration of the Euler
Equation for Stationary Flows" at the Free University of Berlin and became a diploma physicist.

Unfortunately, his vortex physics research and insights soon led him into the scientific margins.
Neither state nor private sources provided funding for Bauer's work. Nevertheless, he published his
research results from 1970 until his death in 1986 in periodically appearing green booklets through
his own publishing house. In the 1970s, Bauer lived for some time in the house of Bernhard Schaeffer,
the co-founder of the Workshop for Decentralized Energy Research in Berlin-Zehlendorf. An extensive
and mutually fruitful exchange of ideas took place, and both — the (more) science practitioner
Schaeffer and the (pure) theorist Bauer — benefited greatly from each other.

Bauer starts from a concept he derived from thermodynamics: In an energy-occupied medium (e.g., an
ideal gas), vortex rings can be formed that spontaneously stabilize themselves. The energy required
for this is drawn by the vortex rings from the gas of which they consist. This process can be
demonstrated experimentally. However, it would hardly be acknowledged, let alone discussed, by the
"professional community." Bauer applies his insight to the concept of the aether. All elementary
matter particles are, for him (as already postulated by Lord Kelvin), vortex rings. He imagines atoms
and molecules as stabilized systems of vortex rings. Electric and magnetic fields are, for him, flow
fields, for which he derives an exact correspondence to Maxwell's equations. Electrodynamics therefore
denotes, for him, the totality of phenomena triggered by flow processes in the aether; the electric
field characterizes the irrotational, the magnetic field the source-free portion of the aether flow
field. Moreover — and with this he goes far beyond current physics — he finds conceptual access to

6.1 The spiral of knowledge
The scientific path of knowledge through the mechanism of paradigm shift [Kuhn] proceeds in steps or spirals:
1. The current state of a discipline, previously experienced as satisfactory, increasingly reveals limits in the form of unsolvable questions, contradictions, divergence between theoretical predictions and experimental findings, etc.
2. Gradually, approaches to overcoming the recognized limits are proposed. The most successful overcoming approach begins to prevail successively — despite emerging tendencies to maintain the status quo.
3. After a breakthrough has been achieved, the discipline establishes itself at a new, higher, and provisionally again satisfactory level of knowledge — and the next spiral turn begins (→ 1.).
In the following we attempt to trace this meta-process in physics.

6.2 State and limits of classical physics
Toward the end of the 19th century, physics in its great theories — mechanics, thermodynamics, optics, and electrodynamics — reached limits through expansion into the large (speed of light) as well as the small (research of the atom), such as the following:
1. What is the carrier of electromagnetic waves and of light in a vacuum?
2. How are the building blocks of matter, the atoms, truly elementary, or how are they structured (electron and nucleon)?
3. What is the nature of the so-called action-at-a-distance forces (gravitation)?
4. How does radioactive decay work?
5. etc.


Since the time of the Greek Democritus (ca. 440 BC), who founded the concept of the smallest building blocks (atoms) to explain space and matter, people worked with the idea of the ether: a fine, all-pervading and connecting primordial substance. The ether is therefore inseparable from the history of natural science. A fascinating overview of the evolution of various ether theories is given by [Waser, 1993].
Until the beginning of the twentieth century, the ether was regarded as a real component of the world.
The ether served as an illustrative model for the interactions in electricity, magnetism, gravitation, temperature, etc., and held a firm place in the worldview of the sciences for a long time.
By the middle of the 19th century, the speed of light was known to be 300,000 km/sec. The discussion then arose as to which medium moves the light and who is responsible for the speed of light.
Maxwell proposed the term ether (Greek: sky wind) for the carrier medium of light postulated on logical grounds. The search for this luminiferous ether occupied the entire physical world at that time.
Of all experiments in the field of light research, the experiment conducted in 1887 by Michelson and Morley has had a lasting impact on our physical worldview:
It was to be determined whether the speed of light changes with the Earths rotation and the Earths orbital speed around the Sun. From such a result, conclusions were to be drawn about the ether wind.
The experiments were repeated in various months and years, as the Earth continuously assumes different positions in its orbit around the Sun.

The result from all these experiments was always the same:
The speed of light always remained constant.
Technically, the experiment proceeds as follows: one splits a light beam by means of a semi-transparent mirror into two coherent beams perpendicular to each other, reflects them back onto themselves, and reunites them at a point on an interference screen. Different velocities would reveal themselves as interference patterns.
But because the ether had not been found in this way, the experiments were judged as failures. One wanted to find the ether and instead found a constant speed of light.
Einstein took this constancy of the speed of light in 1905 as the starting point for his Special Theory of Relativity and discarded the hypothesis of an ether, since it had not been found through the Michelson/Morley experiment.


6.3 The Transition to Modern Physics

The transition to modern physics thus occurred at the beginning of the 20th century through the first realization that the intuitive but contradictory model of an ether could be dispensed with, by introducing less intuitive mathematical models.

Einstein cut this Gordian ether-knot by formulating two postulates — not logically compelling, but adopted:
1. The principle of relativity: There is no means to measure absolute velocities;
2. The speed of light is independent of the motion of the light source;

From these postulates, the constancy of the speed of light is derived, and the relativity principle forbids the existence of an absolute reference frame — which was the last role assigned to the ether.

The generalization of the Special Theory of Relativity to accelerated systems — through formulation of the equivalence principle (identity or indistinguishability of inertial and gravitational mass) — need not be discussed further here.

Incidentally, the elimination of the ether concept was apparently predicted by the author of "The Secret Doctrine," H.P. Blavatsky, in 1888...

The name of the unconventional thinker Einstein is also connected with the other pillar of modern natural science: quantum physics. For a long time, the observation of the photoelectric effect (conversion of light into electricity) had remained a puzzle, according to which the energy of the released electrons is determined only by the frequency of the triggering light, while their number is determined only by the intensity of the light. Classical electromagnetic light theory would have expected the decisive influence on electron energy to come from light intensity.

Einstein resolved this puzzle in a very simple way by postulating, also in 1905, that only whole units of light energy are available for electron emission, concentrated quasi in light corpuscles, the photons. This led to the second realization that light — and as later became apparent, also "true corpuscles" such as electrons and protons — had to be attributed both wave and quantum nature (wave/particle dualism).

And thus, for a second time, classical intuitiveness was replaced by new mathematical-abstract formalism.


6.4 Status and Limits of Modern Physics

As fundamental limits of a physical state of knowledge, we consider all assumptions in the form of fundamental forces, fundamental laws, postulates, and natural constants. These assumptions are, on the one hand, experimentally established facts and, on the other, conceptual models that allow parts of reality to be abstracted.

Unlike formal mathematics, where the individual components merge into a compact whole through the power of logic, the real science of physics therefore does not form a stable but a relatively fragile structure. In particular, in physics, hypotheses according to Popper can never be proven, only refuted.

The touchstone of new physical theories will therefore always be the reduction of current limits to even more elementary limits.

6.5.1 The Advantages of a Medium

The ether-free formulation of physics is therefore not even a hundred years old.

Although the theory of relativity seems to have proven itself to this day and the statements and consequences of quantum theory are truly astonishing and have led in many cases to novel technologies, particularly in electronics, critical voices regarding these theories are multiplying. Even Einstein himself often mentioned that the Michelson-Morley experiment cannot prove that no ether exists (there are at least five different explanations for the outcome of that experiment). Carnot cycle
    Experience has shown that work can only be obtained from heat when part of the heat passes from a warmer (T1) to a colder (T2) body. The processes in a thermal energy converter can be represented as a cycle in work diagrams. The classical cycle, studied by Sadi Carnot in 1824 on the steam engine, consists of 2 adiabatic and 2 isothermal work strokes. 10.1 AGWI01: The scientific worldview and its limits


10.1.1 Scientific understanding through abstraction

No human being can grasp the world of nature in all its magnitude, refinement, and diversity. Our intellect, however, can abstract rules and regularities from the diversity and in this way understand arbitrarily many concrete individual cases. This understanding is based on an adaptation of our thinking to the world in which we live. For the laws of nature, only a fraction of the storage capacity of the human brain is required; the same brain, however, would not be able to store even a small part of the processes in our environment in the form of concrete individual cases. For the intellectual mastery of the real world, we have only the path of abstraction and synthesis in thought. We must image the natural connections in our imagination. The goal of the natural sciences is therefore an accurate, yet also comprehensible conception of the real world.

10.1.2 What is science?

Science — in the sense of the Anglo-American concept of "Science" — is a very late result of cultural evolution; as a creation of the Renaissance, it is only half a millennium old. Operationally defined, scientific research is the conscious, logic-following effort of human beings to gain knowledge about the real world, including human beings themselves. Its results are initially singular insights (facts, data), from which a hypothesis is formed inductively, which is refuted or confirmed by further deductively derived insights or observations. In the latter case, a hypothesis can condense into a theory. Whether a scientific statement is true, i.e., proves itself, depends only on the fulfillment of two principles:
1. the principle of objectivity, i.e., the statement must be intersubjectively comprehensible, reproducible, and independent of the method applied;
2. the principle of positive prediction, i.e., the conclusions drawn from a scientific insight according to the principles of logic must yield a contradiction-free system, and derived predictions must come true.
If one of these principles is violated, the statement is either scientifically untrue, or the statement cannot be assigned to the domain of science.


10.1.3 Science and induction

Scientific conceptions (theories) can never be definitively proven, because there can be no complete induction (conclusion from the specific to the general). But conceptions can be recognized as false when one of their essential statements proves incorrect. There is no strict verification, but there is falsification (==> Popper). Through the latter, the remaining possibilities of thought are narrowed. This selection process must ultimately leave the single correct conception. However, this makes the first limit of scientific knowledge clear: we have no way of inferring the extent and nature of the unknown from the extent and nature of the known. The scientific worldview is therefore necessarily fragmentary, never final, and thus always an open worldview.

10.1.4 Science and chance

A second limit of our capacity for knowledge lies in the fact that the worldview we strive for should be surveyable. But one cannot describe the world arbitrarily precisely and still comprehensibly. The worldview must in principle be simpler than the world itself. The worldview refers to regularities; but these can only be recognized in repeatable processes. In the case of a solitary event, however, predictability fails. We can grasp the general law; what remains inscrutable is what we call chance. Herein lies a fundamental one-sidedness of natural science.

10.1.5 Science and morality

The goal of science is knowledge. A third limit of science arises from the observation that knowledge, while a prerequisite, is not a sufficient reason for correct action or for the correct conduct of our lives. Science therefore cannot create values or make statements about the meaning of existence and the goal of society — knowledge is not normative. The examination of the guiding principles of human action leads to the moral problem of philosophy.


10.1.6 Science and philosophy

The limits of scientific knowledge recognized so far raise the question of what science lacks for recognizing the full truth. The answer is: it is not philosophical, i.e., it does not know itself, and does not attempt to know itself.
If one interprets philosophizing as continuing to ask questions, particularly asking back about one's own presuppositions, then the relationship of philosophy to science can be summed up in the formula:
Philosophy poses those questions that not having posed them was the condition for the success of the scientific method.
This thus claims that science owes its success, among other things, to the renunciation of asking certain questions. These are in particular the fundamental questions of the respective discipline. Historically, they are the typical crisis questions of science, questions that arise in the self-reflection of a crisis, until they are forgotten again in the calm of a new plateau of progress (==> paradigm shift). This prohibition on asking certain meaningful questions constitutes the fourth limit of scientific knowledge.
The reference to the role of philosophical questions as crisis questions of science already shows that science and philosophy are not separate domains, but rather different motives and modes of behavior in the search for truth. Philosophy deals predominantly with those problems that science sets aside. All in all, exact science must be supplemented in three respects by philosophy: through the metaphysical, the epistemological, and the moral problem.

10.1.7 Science and causality

A further, fifth limit of natural science arises from its limited view of causality. Causality is not only one-sided (the past acts on the present), but must be understood as two-sided (the future also acts back on the present) and thus bipolar.
According to Aristotle, the efficient cause must be paired with the polar final cause (causa finalis).
Example:
- What is the cause of a ticket machine issuing me a ticket?
- Efficient cause: Because I (in the past) put in money!
- Final cause: Because I (in the future) want to go to Bern!
Natural science therefore always falls short, e.g., in its search for viruses and bacteria as causes of diseases, for which the final cause — leading human beings to higher insight — is far more essential.


10.1.8 The value of science

We live in a world that, in its magnitude and diversity, can only be described through a multitude of images that represent partial worlds. There seems to be no other access to the whole than that of limitation. We must be one-sided in order to rise above the immediate. There are many worldviews, rational and irrational, static and dynamic, none is perfect, and their totality is a sign of the fullness of being human. We seemingly live in many worlds at once, in religious, artistic, scientific. But the world is always the one infinitely rich world that we approach in different ways. The great views of this world are not alternative, but complementary. Precisely those who do not overestimate the natural sciences will also find the unfinished worldview on which they work rich and magnificent.

10.6 SCHA01: The infinite power of water
(Excerpt from ZeitenSchrift No. 14, 1997)

A man showed over 60 years ago how one can naturally purify our water and harness its tremendous powers. If we were to follow the insights of Viktor Schauberger, we would not only have healthy water, but could also draw unlimited, clean energy from water and air that would cost us almost nothing. If we replaced today's death-technology of explosion with the biotechnology of implosion, all the great problems of humanity would be solved — and that is exactly why they are still withheld from all of us today.

The power of cool water

Schauberger soon recognized that water does not like to be exposed to sunlight. For example, there had long been a spring over which a stone hut was built. Later it was demolished and the spring lay open and unprotected to the sunlight. It did not take long before the spring suddenly dried up, and no one knew why. But when the stone hut was rebuilt over it, the water returned. From the ancient Romans we know that they also covered their springs with stone slabs, leaving only a round stone opening free, into which the drainage pipes were inserted so tightly that not even air could enter.

Water evidently loves the shade. That is why all springs lie hidden deep in the forest or in dark rock crevices, and a natural river or stream protects itself with shade-giving trees and bushes on its banks against direct sunlight.

Schauberger also observed that rising floodwaters during thaw (the water warms up) build up gravel banks, which are then often naturally washed away on clear, cool nights (the water gets colder). From this he concluded that the carrying and suction power of water is greatest when its temperature is low and its flow is undisturbed.

Of stones that float high in the water

Another phenomenon that immensely fascinated Viktor Schauberger: the trout and salmon in mountain streams. How did trout manage to stand motionless even in the most turbulent currents? Why did they dart upstream against the current instead of drifting with the water? And then upward to the surface, instead of into the protective depths!

Could this ability of the trout also be connected to water temperature? No sooner thought than done: Schauberger had a hundred liters of water warmed and poured into the stream about a hundred meters above a trout. This small amount of water could not noticeably warm the stream, and yet... A short time later the trout began to become restless and beat its fin violently. It could barely hold its position and was even swept downstream shortly after.

Viktor Schauberger also wondered how trout could overcome cliffs and waterfalls, and why they jumped higher the more furiously the water crashed down. He observed how the trout floated motionlessly upward in the plunging stream and were suddenly hurled into the incoming water above, just like that. The answer would only come to Schauberger after decades of intensive water observation. Today we know that every force — whether material or immaterial — generates an equally strong counter-force. Just as a tornado swirls air masses downward on the outside in order to suck them back up inside, naturally flowing (vortexing) water also generates an energy that flows in the opposite direction of the water — upward instead of downward. This energy flow, which in a waterfall can be seen as a bright channel of light within the water stream, is what the trout utilize, being pulled upward by it as if into the interior of a waterspout.

Schauberger made yet another incredible discovery: On a moonlit, freezing winter night, he saw head-sized stones detach from the bottom of a mountain stream pool and float circling to the water's surface like the trout before their great "leap," hovering there! Heavy stones! Schauberger could not believe his eyes. What force could accomplish such a thing? It was the same force of levitation dormant in the water that also makes the trout "leap."

But not all stones levitated. Only the smooth, egg-shaped stones floated seemingly weightless on the water, dancing; the angular ones remained motionless on the bottom, as one would expect of ordinary stones.

How to clean rivers cheaply and naturally

Throughout his life, Viktor Schauberger had been able to observe the interplay of forest and water, and he knew that without forest, there would soon be no more water. He had seen the untouched mountain streams of his birthplace, whose beds were covered with moss and which never overflowed their banks, even after the heaviest rainfall.

But when a forest was clear-cut, the streams were the first to react: they became wild torrents that washed away the moss on the bottom, could no longer keep their beds clean (because the shade-giving forest was gone and the water temperature rose), and consequently deposited debris and mud. As a result, the stream and river beds were attacked and the banks undermined. When it rained heavily or after snowmelt, floods finally came.

So people developed torrent control structures and forced the waterways into stone and concrete. Because the water cannot flow and vortex freely in these straightenings and corsets, it constantly tries to break out and undermine its artificial prison — which causes enormous costs, because we have to constantly repair the structures.

Already in the late 1920s, Schauberger fought vehemently against clear-cutting and torrent control, preaching that one should only harvest the "interest" of the forest, not cut it down. He, who had earlier built log flumes himself, withdrew from it when he realized that his installations mostly served the unscrupulous clear-cutting of entire forests.

Schauberger knew that it lies in the water's own striving to restore its equilibrium, and that a river can keep its bed in order itself if it is simply allowed to flow naturally. Consequently, Schauberger saw human intervention not in straightening, but in helping the river to vortex naturally again: "One never regulates a watercourse from its banks, but from within, from the flowing medium itself."

In 1929 and 1939, he filed patents for torrent control and river regulation, which consisted of installing braking elements at suitable points to direct the flow axis of the river toward the center (so that it could neither undermine nor deposit). In addition, he developed methods for mixing the warmer surface water with the colder bottom water, so that the water temperature could adjust to the current air temperature, because he knew that water temperature influences flow behavior.

A tragic example of a river dying is the Rhine. Once it was a calm, mighty river with water so crystal clear that one could see to the bottom. At night, the riverbed glowed in a fiery, golden light — discharges created by carried pebbles — from which the legend of the Rhine gold arose, according to which dwarves on the river bottom crafted splendid jewelry in their forges.

When people began to clear the forest at its upper course in the Swiss high mountains, the equilibrium was disturbed and the Rhine began to silt up. To increase the flow velocity (so that it could keep its course clean), people began to straighten the Rhine. Now the silt simply deposited further downstream — and they had to straighten there too. Finally, the entire river was straightened, and it consequently silted up even more. The cause lay solely in the deforestation, which not only disturbed the ecological balance but also eliminated the enormous cooling effect of the forest (through evaporation in the tree canopy, heat is drawn away from the root zone; groundwater and soil are thus cooled by the forest).

Because the forest was missing on the straightened banks, not only did the water temperature rise, but the rainfall could no longer be absorbed by the ground and flowed unhindered into the Rhine, which naturally flooded wide areas. So they had to build even higher walls, dredge even deeper, throw even more money down the drain (the contractors were delighted). For nothing will ever change in this vicious circle.

The authorities ignored Schauberger's proposals

Deposits and siltation are a sign that a flowing waterway is dying. They disappear only when one gives the river new life by imparting ordered impulses to it. The forest — the cradle of water

For Schauberger, not only was the harmonious interplay of laminar and turbulent motion important for the "metabolism" of water, but also the "positive temperature movement." By this he meant the approach of water toward +4 degrees Celsius. During this temperature movement and a simultaneous cycloidal spiral motion (vortex), the energy of the water rises, it becomes fresh and alive. Through "emulsion," new water forms, in which oxygen is separated from hydrogen. During "negative temperature movement," the water is heated above +4 degrees Celsius, leading to a decrease in energy and poorer biological quality. Hydrogen is bound by oxygen and the water slowly decomposes; it loses its carrying power and develops pathogenic germs.

Schauberger also described the cycle of water, how it circulates between sky and deep Earth. An important link in this is the forest: through evaporation via the tree canopy, it draws heat from the soil, and this cooling causes the groundwater to rise (especially during dry periods), because according to Archimedes' principle, warmer water masses cannot lie beneath colder ones.

If, however, the forest is cut, direct solar radiation warms the cleared surfaces, the groundwater — and with it the deposition of nutrient salts — sinks to depths where they become unreachable for plant roots; the springs dry up. As a result, the entire landscape becomes karstified. Thus one can understand why Viktor Schauberger called the forest the "cradle of water."

Good drinking water is vital

Schauberger was also against the now-common practice of pumping up groundwater. In his view, groundwater is not yet "ripe" to be drunk, which is why it still lies deep in the Earth. Only water that reaches the surface by itself, i.e., spring water, is ripe enough, for it has completed the entire developmental cycle.

Therefore, Schauberger recognized early on the need to create devices that could supply people with drinking water of spring quality. Today, various further developments are available on the market.
