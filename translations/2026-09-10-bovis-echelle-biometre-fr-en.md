# André Bovis and the Bovis Scale: Measuring "Vibratory Rates"

*English translation from French · source: https://soins-energetiques-distance.fr/andre-bovis-biometre/ (Antoine Lesaffre, 2025) · translated by Forge (translation-qc), 2026-09-10*

**The essentials:** André Bovis, an inventor from Nice, created in the 1930s a scale for measuring "vibratory rates" with the pendulum, based on a graduation anchored at 6500 Bovis Units (UB) tied to visible light. Despite its status as unvalidated pseudoscience, his scale remains a reference tool in radiesthesia.

Do you confuse Alfred Bovis and "André Bovis"? Discover the improbable path of this Nice coppersmith/hardware merchant, self-taught visionary of radiesthesia, who designed the Bovis scale to measure "vibratory rates" from 0 to 10,000 UB. This enthusiast of experimentation — inventor of an incubator and an ovimeter for testing egg freshness — drew on the theories of Abbé Th. Moreux on "pyramid radiations," without ever leaving France. His scale, first engraved on a 20 cm rule, evaluates the vitality of places and beings by combining a craftsman's approach with energetic beliefs, still triggering scientific controversy over its pseudo-objectivity today.

## Who was André Bovis, the man behind the myth?

### Demystification: the true path of a Nice inventor

André Bovis — often confused with a certain "Alfred" — was a self-taught inventor from Nice (1871–1947). Contrary to persistent myths, he was neither a physician, nor a physicist, nor an Egyptologist. From a family of merchants, he had to abandon his studies at 16 to take over the family business.

This coppersmith, passionate about innovation, left concrete traces in several technical fields, with verifiable creations:

- An incubator for poultry raising
- A hygrometer for measuring humidity
- An ovimeter for testing egg freshness

These achievements testify to an inventive, experimental mind. His collaboration with the engineer André Simoneton on calibrating the biometer shows his will to confront his ideas with technical competence. That collaboration transformed his initial idea (a scale of 0 to 10,000 units) into an operational tool based on the red spectrum of sunlight — demonstrating his concern for practical grounding despite the method's limits.

### From experimentation to radiesthesia: a self-taught passion

André Bovis's move into radiesthesia fits within his experimental approach. His research to determine the sex of eggs using a pendulum sharpened his interest in his tool's unexplained reactions. This curiosity led him to rely on the writings of Abbé Théodore Moreux — without traveling to Egypt, as some accounts suggest.

The scale models of the Great Pyramid of Khufu (Cheops) that he built were not the fruit of a mythical journey, but of experiments inspired by the preservative properties of the pyramids. Observing the preservation of living matter in these structures, he linked geometric orientation to subtle energies, inspiring modern "pyramidology." His approach, while not scientific, reflects a craftsman's logic seeking to reproduce observed effects.

This atypical trajectory — between the verifiable and the speculative — explains both the enthusiasm and the skepticism he still arouses. His legacy lies in his attempt to quantify the impalpable, as his scale of "Bovis Units" shows (1 UB = 1 angström), seeking to give scientific footing to phenomena that cannot be measured. Despite the lack of scientific validation, his tools are still used by some practitioners as symbols of a holistic approach.

By replacing the imaginary Egyptologist with the Nice coppersmith, one grasps how a curious mind was able to create measuring instruments for phenomena science has never validated. This blend of technical ingenuity and esoteric speculation perfectly illustrates the tensions between empiricism and intuition in the history of pseudosciences.

## The birth of the Bovis scale: an attempt to quantify vital energy

### The concept of the "vibratory rate"

André Bovis, a radiesthesist of the early 20th century, sought to transform an esoteric practice into a measurable tool. His central hypothesis? Every living being, every place, every substance would emit a "vibratory rate" revealing its vital energy. This concept — today central to geobiology — aimed to replace the subjective interpretation of pendulum movements with a standardized method.

Drawing on research into waves and the nascent quantum physics, Bovis postulated that precise measurements would distinguish positive energies from harmful ones. Bovis's legacy is found in the work of his grandson Jacques Bovis, a major figure of modern geobiology.

### The first tool: the Bovis rule and its graduation in angströms

In 1930, Bovis developed a simple rule: a 20 cm stick graduated from 0 to 100. The value 100 corresponded to 6500 angströms (Å), a decisive wavelength for Bovis. This self-taught physicist — who had abandoned his studies at 16 to manage the family business — believed 6500 Å represented the threshold of human visibility for "vital" radiations.

His method rested on the idea that an organism's health could be measured by its proximity to this reference. A healthy human being would vibrate around 6500 Å, while a lower rate would signal an energetic imbalance. This innovative approach, though never scientifically validated, marked a rare drive toward objectivity in the field of radiesthesia. Bovis — an enthusiast of aviation and beekeeping — transposed his technical knowledge into this new domain.

### The evolution toward the "Bovis Biometer" and Bovis Units (UB)

Unsatisfied with his first scale, Bovis teamed up with the engineer André Simoneton to create the Bovis Biometer. This new tool — a 30 cm stick graduated up to 10,000 Å — allows more precise measurements. The notion of the Bovis Unit (1 UB = 1 Å) was born then, definitively popularizing his system.

Foods, for example, are categorized by vibratory level: fresh fruits reach 9,000 UB, while processed products fall below 1,000 UB. As one external reference puts it: "André Bovis's ambition was not to prove a mystical truth, but to create a standardized tool to quantify the subtle radiations he perceived — an attempt to give a measure to the invisible."

## How to use and interpret the Bovis scale?

### The measurement method with the pendulum

The radiesthesist uses a pendulum to measure vibratory rates. After preparing in a calm place, the pendulum is held above the neutral point (6,500 UB), the universal reference corresponding to the average of living beings. Concentrating on the element to test (object, place, person), the pendulum reacts with oscillations or rotations, revealing the value on the scale. The orientation of the movements (left/right, clockwise/counterclockwise) can indicate positive or negative energies.

The pendulum's movements are said to be influenced by the ideomotor effect: unconscious micro-movements of the muscles linked to the user's expectations. Technical mastery is essential to reduce subjective bias — hence the importance of regular exercises to refine one's sensitivity. Practitioners recommend training on known objects before moving to complex measurements.

### Rule or dial: which tool for which measurement?

The Bovis rule, linear (0–10,000 UB), serves for everyday measurements: human vitality or food quality. A fresh fruit can reach 9,000 UB, versus 3,000 UB on average for market products. This scale is used by wellness practitioners to advise on diet or diagnose imbalances.

The Bovis dial — the circular Bovis scale — allows extended measurements (up to 60,000 UB). It is suited to sacred places (values exceeding 100,000 UB for sites like Glastonbury) or deep meditative states (40,000–60,000 UB). This format is used in geobiology to diagnose a place's energy, or in energetic therapy for advanced care.

## André Bovis's place in the history of radiesthesia, and the view of science

### A major contribution: standardizing a subjective measurement

André Bovis (1871–1947), radiesthesist of Nice, marked the history of radiesthesia by creating the so-called Bovis scale in the 1930s.

Originally, his tool consisted of a 20 cm stick graduated from 0 to 10,000 Bovis Units (UB), based on the angström (1 UB = 1 angström). This scale aimed to quantify the "vibratory rates" of living beings, objects, or places via the pendulum.

In collaboration with the engineer Simoneton, Bovis perfected his tool to create the "Bovis biometer" — a 30 cm stick up to 10,000 Å. Its use consisted of locating the "neutral point" (6,500 UB), then measuring the pendulum's reactions.

This tool standardized the practice, becoming an unavoidable reference for radiesthesists, despite its lack of scientific validation.

### The scientific debate: between the ideomotor effect and pseudoscience

Radiesthesia is considered a pseudoscience by the scientific community. The pendulum's movements are attributed to the ideomotor effect, a psychological phenomenon documented as early as 1854 by Michel-Eugène Chevreul.

"Despite practitioners' enthusiasm, the scientific community attributes the pendulum's movements not to external radiations but to the ideomotor effect — a phenomenon where the operator's thoughts unconsciously influence their own muscles."

Measurements in Bovis Units rest on an unverifiable theoretical framework. No rigorous study has ever demonstrated the existence of the "vital radiations" being measured. Non-reproducible results are explained by the practitioner's expectations.

As scientific analyses of pseudosciences explain, the criteria of pseudoscience apply here: esoteric jargon, absence of experimental protocols, and inversion of the burden of proof.

The Bovis Unit, though widely used in geobiology, has no physical basis. The fanciful measurements (e.g., cathedrals at 4 million UB, viruses at 170,000 UB) illustrate the absence of an objective method.

André Bovis, the self-taught inventor of Nice, created the scale of vibratory rates, which has remained in the domain of pseudosciences. Though his work structured a language for geobiology and radionics, science attributes the pendulum to the ideomotor effect, without experimental proof. His legacy mingles creativity with the mystery of invisible energies.

## FAQ (selected)

**What is the normal vibratory rate for a human being in Bovis Units?**
According to the Bovis scale, the normal rate sits between 8,000 and 10,000 UB, associated with a state of balanced health. A neutral point at 6,500 UB is considered the critical threshold: below it, values are tied to fatigue or energetic imbalance; above it, vitality increases. These interpretations remain founded on pseudoscientific theories, without recognized empirical validation.

**Does radiesthesia work?**
Radiesthesia is a divinatory practice using tools like the pendulum or rod to detect "radiations" or subtle energies. Though adherents see an intuitive method, the scientific community classes it as pseudoscience. The pendulum's movements are attributed to the ideomotor effect — an unconscious muscular phenomenon linked to the operator's expectations. No rigorous study has validated its efficacy, as the Munich experiment (1986–1988) and the work of Michel-Eugène Chevreul from 1854 onward underline.

**Is 10,000 UB normal for a human being?**
Yes, a rate of 10,000 UB falls within the normal range described by the Bovis scale, associated with optimal vitality — even considered the upper threshold for physical health. These scales remain subjective tools without established scientific basis; their use belongs to a holistic approach, often criticized for its lack of experimental reproducibility.

**Which foods have a high vibratory rate?**
Fresh, organic foods generally reach the best values — often around 9,000 UB for fruits and vegetables picked at ripeness. Natural spring waters can rise to 8,000–18,000 UB, while processed or aged foods see their rate fall below 3,000 UB. Living, chemically unaltered products are systematically better ranked in radiesthesia, though these measurements are descriptive without a demonstrated physico-chemical basis.

**How can I know if my vibratory rate is high?**
To measure one's vibratory rate via the Bovis method, one uses a pendulum above a graduated rule or dial (from 0 to 10,000 or more). The point of oscillation or stop of the pendulum indicates the value. A rate above 8,000 UB is interpreted as high and healthy. This practice, though rooted in radiesthesia theory, rests on a non-standardized method subject to perceptual bias, limiting its reliability by scientific criteria.

**What does a session of radiesthesia cost?**
The cost of a radiesthesia session varies by practitioner, often between 50 and 150 euros in France. Since the activity is not regulated, prices and training are not standardized. Sessions can include energetic diagnoses of places or foods, with advice to improve vibratory rates — though these services remain within a non-medical holistic framework.

**What question should you not ask your pendulum?**
It is advised against asking vague, moral, or opinion-bound questions, such as "Should I quit my job?" or "Does this person love me?" Radiesthesists prefer precise, objective questions ("What is the vibratory rate of this place?") to avoid interpretation bias. The method also demands a protocol of calibration and concentration to limit the user's unconscious influence on the pendulum.

**What do you call a person who uses a pendulum for radiesthesia?**
A practitioner of radiesthesia is designated a "radiesthesist" or "radiesthète." These terms designate individuals using tools like the pendulum, the rod, or the Bovis dials to detect subtle energies. The practice — though it has a rich history (with figures like Alfred Bovis or Abbé Bouly) — is not recognized by the scientific community for medical or technical applications.
