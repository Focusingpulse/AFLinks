# The Government sends the Classified Information Law to Congress

22.7.2025

The Council of Ministers gives the green light to the norm that will replace the Official Secrets Law of 1968. For the first time in Spain's history, automatic declassification of information with the passage of time is established.

Madrid (Spain)
Pool Moncloa/Raúl Salgado

The Council of Ministers on Tuesday definitively approved the Draft Classified Information Law, a norm promoted by the Ministry of the Presidency, Justice and Relations with the Cortes, which replaces the Official Secrets Law of 1968. The draft bill, which is now being sent to the Congress of Deputies for its parliamentary processing, guarantees the State's duty to classify sensitive information that may affect national defence and public safety, but also makes this compatible with the right to information and transparency. In this way, Spain will place itself in line with the most advanced legislations of the EU and NATO on classified information. In fact, France, Denmark and Sweden have declassification timelines that are more extensive than those provided for in this norm. Furthermore, as the Minister of the Presidency, Justice and Relations with the Cortes, Félix Bolaños, has stated, one of the commitments acquired in the Action Plan for Democracy is fulfilled, and one of the recommendations that the European Commission has systematically been making to our country in its Report on the Rule of Law is taken on board, with a view to improving citizens' access to information and, in particular, to official documents.

Automatic declassification

The draft law clearly establishes that the classification of information shall be exceptional in character, must be duly justified, and stipulates that information relating to gross violations of human rights or crimes against humanity may not be classified. As Bolaños explained at the press conference after the Council of Ministers, the law develops the procedures for classifying, reclassifying and declassifying information and endows them with full guarantees. To this end, it provides for, for the first time in Spain's democratic history, automatic declassification. In this regard, four categories of classified information are regulated — "Top Secret", "Secret", "Confidential" and "Restricted" — and each of them is assigned a gradual time limit for classification, taking into account the risk of unauthorised disclosure or misuse. For "Top Secret", automatic declassification is stipulated after forty-five years, which may only be extended once, with justification, for a further fifteen years. Information classified as "Secret" shall be declassified after thirty-five years and may only be extended by ten more years, likewise exceptionally and with justification. In the "Confidential" category, an automatic declassification period of between seven and nine years is established, which cannot be extended. And finally, information classified as "Restricted" shall be declassified within a period of four to five years, which likewise cannot be extended. All of these periods shall conclude automatically on the last day of the year in which the classification period expires. The norm also establishes who shall have the competence to classify and declassify information: it is the exclusive competence of the Council of Ministers — at the proposal of the Prime Minister or the ministers — in the case of "Top Secret" and "Secret", and of a limited and reduced number of authorities in the "Confidential" and "Restricted" categories. As for information classified before this law enters into force, it shall be automatically declassified once forty-five years or more have elapsed since its classification. In addition, persons who can justify a professional interest in this information — such as researchers, journalists or historians — shall be entitled to request its declassification once the classification period has expired. Access by the Congress of Deputies to classified information is also strengthened through the parliamentary committee constituted for this purpose. Likewise, the law stipulates an administrative sanctioning regime with penalties ranging from 30,000 euros in the least serious cases to 2.5 million euros in the most serious ones. Through this route, a more proportionate regime is created than the previous one, which only contemplated the criminal route with high prison sentences. The right to freedom of information is introduced as a criterion for the grading of penalties.

Creation of the National Authority for the Protection of Classified Information

Bolaños also explained that the draft law approved today by the Council of Ministers regulates the bodies with competence in this matter, among which is the National Authority for the Protection of Classified Information, which shall depend on the Ministry of the Presidency, Justice and Relations with the Cortes. This is already the case in a large part of the European and NATO countries, where this function is attributed to the department in charge of interministerial coordination due to the cross-cutting nature of the information that may be classified.
