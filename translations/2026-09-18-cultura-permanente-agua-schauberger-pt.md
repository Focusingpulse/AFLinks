Introduction
The book "The Water Wizard" (Schauberger, 1999) was introduced to me in 2018, when I received
a visit from Tita, founder of the NGO Nascentes, at a rural property where I was developing
an agroforestry and spring-recovery project. The book, at the same time as it
deconstructed my training in Environmental Engineering and also my understanding
of water management and dynamics in nature, formulated an intuitive and
profound desire to discover Viktor Schauberger's vision of the natural intelligence of water in
shaping life.
As happened with many people, the pandemic brought a new insight and joined that desire to
translation, the profession in which I have worked for 10 years. I acquired the
author's rights to the book for Brazilian Portuguese in 2021 and, along this path, I
discovered many people who were also
inspired by Viktor Schauberger. Since then, the book's publication project has
a complete editorial team, in which I am the translator and coordinator, five institutional
supporters from the field of water and ecology, support and a preface from the esteemed Professor Dr.
Demétrios Christofidis, a master sponsorship and a large network of support and new friends
who wish to change the current conception of water management applied by public and
private powers in Brazil and the world. Soon, the launch and publication of the book in Portuguese,
scheduled for March 2026, will be announced in the media with great joy and a beautiful
path of union traveled.
Amid our growing contact with the consequences of water crises and the collapse
of ecological systems, together with the growing loss of ethical reference in the interaction
with nature, figures emerge such as the Austrian Viktor Schauberger (1885–1958). His work
proposes, beyond practical solutions, a profound reconnection between science, nature and
spirituality. In his book The Water Wizard, his observations about water, considered
a living organism in constant transformation, shed light on regenerative practices
aligned with what we today know as permaculture.
Permaculture is a systemic science of socio-environmental character that brings together
scientific knowledge with traditional popular knowledge in the planning of permanent human
environments in dynamic equilibrium with nature, within an ethics that, nowadays,
must seek new creations for the principles of care for the earth, people, and the
limits of consumption. Such principles must accept the intensity and scale at which
current energy conditions manifest themselves in ecological, social, economic and
cultural contexts. The conditions for the connection between dynamic, holistic and
systemic equilibrium to occur are precisely the meeting point between the paths of Schauberger
and of permaculture.

Water, according to Viktor Schauberger
Viktor Schauberger maintained that water, in its natural state — that is, born in fresh
springs, generally close to 4 °C, in organic movement and in equilibrium
with the environment — is more than a liquid: it is a living substance, endowed with soul,
character and a vital function.

Romantic as they are, the words "soul" and "character" have a scientific and systemic basis. In
Schauberger's time, in the middle of the Second World War, when mechanistic
science was the guiding voice of technology, there was much resistance to his ideas and theories.
The "character of water" cited by Schauberger is nothing more than its process of maturation and
transformation, which varies depending on the environment it permeates. Yes, water, like
any living being, emerges in a "spring" and, like us, has its character influenced by the
environment in which it is raised during the primary part of life. Depending on its cycle of
development — that is, its interaction with the deepest layers of the earth, in a
slow process of geothermal, energetic and structural transformation — in its reaction with
pH, pressure, temperature, contour and interaction with minerals and trace elements — water
is born with certain living characteristics. If captured underground prematurely, before
its total maturation process, the "juvenile water," as the author describes it, presents itself
sterile — that is, poor in salts and microorganisms, biologically immature — since it does not yet
carry the microbiota and minerals that make it nutritious, and it is endowed with a high capacity to
absorb elements from the environment, in the same way as a baby in the womb. According to the author,
this characteristic can be extremely harmful to those who consume it, because it tends to
withdraw minerals from the organism instead of replenishing them.
When, in his research, Viktor Schauberger cites the "soul of water," he is referring to the
capacity to sustain life through its functional organization in its natural regime.
Today, science could also attribute this capacity and organization to molecular
structure, conductivity, and resonance. In other words, the author classified the
functional organization of water as the dynamic state in which it conserves and renews its
vital processes, related to its temperature gradients and its self-cooling,
connected to its movement in vortices in its natural flow. Also part of its
functional organization are the water's contact with the minerals, roots and micro-life of the river's
living bed, exposure to radiation along its course, and the condensation of energy in its movement —
that is, its capacity for systemic organization and equilibrium through its natural
movement.
Authors such as (Sokhan et al., 2015), (Brini et al., 2017) and (Sieroka et al., 2024) bring in their
studies concepts about the molecular structure of water, thermal capacity, anomalous
density and other factors that converge with Viktor Schauberger's theories. We know
that the molecular structure, sustained by the dynamics of hydrogen bonds, regulates the
water's thermal gradients and stability. Conductivity is related to the ionic exchanges
provided by contact with salts, gases and organisms dissolved in the water. Finally,
resonance, expressed in helical and hydrodynamic movements, organizes energy
into vibratory patterns. These dimensions reinforce the view that water is a living system,
capable of conserving and renewing its vitality in interaction with the environment it travels through.
We can say that, according to Viktor Schauberger, water is far from being a "neutral"
compound — on the contrary: it changes according to the environment and its history.
Now, using the current findings of science, let us understand another fundamental point of
the author's theories about water's systemic relationship with the world. Water is an anomalous
substance par excellence and, unlike other liquids, its greatest density occurs at
+4 °C, not at its freezing point. This singular behavior is crucial for the

thermal equilibrium of nature and of life. Viktor Schauberger emphasized that the specific heat
of water — its capacity to resist rapid changes in temperature — is extremely
high, and its lowest point lies only 0.5 °C above the normal temperature of human
blood, +37 °C; that is, at +36.5 °C, water shows its greatest resistance
to temperature change! This reveals to us that the human body itself, composed of
around 60–62% water in an adult individual (Lu et al., 2023), is anchored in this
anomalous behavior to keep life stable even amid intense climatic variations. Water,
therefore, is not merely a solvent; it is the thermal and vital mediator of the
organism.
No less important, the author defended as a priority the natural movement of water as
something intrinsic to its quality and vitality. Schauberger observed that nature operates
with centripetal, spiral, vortex and rhythmic movements — movements opposite to the
straight-line and angular forms proposed by mechanistic engineering. His studies
show the flow of water in rivers in spiral concentric layers, with the center of the
flow being colder, faster and denser. This vortex flow creates a self-organization that
regulates temperature, oxygenates the system, transports nutrients and prevents pathogenic
buildup.
When the river flows in a spiral fashion, or when the helical flow of its central core occurs,
it shapes its banks with intelligence: the outer curve is deepened, the inner one
smoothed, and sediments are distributed according to their specific weight. This
dynamic equilibrium not only preserves the structure of the bed but also fertilizes the
banks, allowing riparian vegetation to grow vigorously, creating a cycle in which the
river feeds the land, and the land, in turn, protects the river with its vegetation.
In this sense, Schauberger asserted that, in a river, the helical movement that occurs on
the inner part of each meander — the so-called "curve within the curve" — constitutes the
sexual organ of water, since at this inflection point the current regenerates itself, fertilizes and multiplies its
vitality. The author explains that the longitudinal spiral movement of water inside the
river's meander, a kind of internal vortex that makes the current ascend along the concave
bank and return along the bottom, creates zones of suction and compression that allow it to
absorb oxygen and nutrients from the subsoil, mixing them into the river's living flow. This
conception was later deepened by John Wilkes, creator of the Flow Forms, who
demonstrated how the spiral movement and pulsing rhythm of water promote processes
of self-purification and revitalization of the flow. Thus, in both Schauberger and Wilkes, the
form and movement of water reveal a morphogenetic principle essential to the regeneration
of aquatic ecosystems (Wilkes, 2003).
Therefore, according to Schauberger, each curve of the river is not merely a change of direction,
but an act of continuous reproduction of the water's energy and fertility. Such a conception
translates the idea that the river, by shaping itself in successive curves, perpetuates its
formative and maternal character, sustaining both itself and the life on its banks. In this
dynamic, the oxygen absorbed in the curves acts as a fertilizing substance, reinvigorating the
current with vital energy and enriching the flow with elements capable of sustaining the
fertility of the soil and the health of the organisms that depend on it, as illustrated in
Figure 1.

According to Schauberger, direct radiation from the sun on bare water causes its
degeneration. Although water in movement and with diluted organic load can better
resist solar impact, (Häder et al., 2011) state that solar radiation causes the formation
of reactive oxygen species, the breakdown of dissolved organic matter and alteration of the
trophic web. Viktor Schauberger maintained that heated water loses its internal structure,
oxygen and vital capacity, becoming stagnant and susceptible to the proliferation of
pathogens.
Deforestation breaks this natural protection and aggravates the problem: without shade, rivers
warm, their vortices rupture, sediments accumulate in disorder and the
bed rises. This reduces infiltration and causes the imbalance between surface and aquifers,
generating the so-called "chained floods," a common phenomenon in Brazil and the world,
when one flood leads to the next, in rapid succession, due to the absence of underground
recharge and the loss of the landscape's retention capacity.

Parallels with permaculture
Now that we have seen some important concepts about the author's theories regarding the natural
flow of water, we find direct parallels in permaculture's water management
techniques, such as swales2, curved infiltration channels and rainwater
harvesting systems that respect the contours of the land and water storage in soils.
In permaculture, designing with water means understanding that it seeks harmonious
paths, never straight ones, and that its healthy flow regenerates the life around it.
Still on Schauberger's relationship with permaculture, among so many virtuosities as a
visionary scientist, physicist, naturalist and mathematician, he was always remembered mainly
for his gift as an observer. It is very common to see the planning principle
"observe and interact" in permaculture circles. Schauberger, in contact with the untouched
forest, had his consciousness touched by the vitality of water's natural movements in
nature.
In a romantic and, at times, indignant way, the author wrote his theories emphasizing how
society was moving in directions totally opposed to the dynamics of nature's vital movements
and cycles, citing as an example the channeling of rivers in straight lines, the conveyance of
water through linear pipes, the use of centrifugal turbines to pump water — whereas in
nature, movement and energy are generated by much smaller temperature gradients. The
author cites and criticizes such practices of society in order to compare the
behavior of water in nature with that of society, which inhabits the same environment.
Just like water, which upon losing its vital bed becomes turbulent and destructive, so too
society, if poorly guided, degenerates into aggression and disorder.

important characteristics of vital water. As an example, Viktor asserted that the composition and

the "behavior" of oxygen in water change, making it "aggressive" — a theme worth
covering briefly. Simply put, we can say that the concept of
"aggressive oxygen" proposed by Schauberger dialogues with the biogeochemical cycles in
regenerative agriculture. Excess oxidation, whether in the human body or in the soil, degenerates
life. The equilibrium between oxygen, carbon and moisture, on the other hand, promotes
health. The living water Schauberger proposes is, in fact, the conducting link of the
respiration processes of soil and plants.
"Aggressive oxygen," then, is a state in which this compound, intensely activated
by heat or mechanical agitation, stops building and starts destroying. In this context,
oxygen undergoes an increase in its redox potential, becoming highly reactive and
manifesting itself biochemically in the form of uncontrolled oxidation.
This process breaks molecular chains, accelerates the decomposition of organic matter and
breaks the subtle bonds that maintain the living structure of water. In the soil, this translates into
the loss of humus, the death of beneficial microorganisms and the proliferation of
pathogens. In the bodies of animals and humans, excess oxidation generates metabolic
stress, inflammation and systemic weakening. More profoundly, Viktor Schauberger asserted that this
imbalance also reaches people's character and soul, for it destructures the internal natural
rhythms, preventing the being from living in harmony with the cycles of life.
In his work, among so many questions like this last one, Viktor Schauberger also
questioned the use of iron pipes — the material used at the time — for conveying
water, even suggesting the use of wood for systemic flow. He strongly criticized
water disinfection processes using chlorine, associating such practice with the increased
incidence of cancer in society. He even mentions the use of iron tools in agriculture, giving
preference to copper on account of soil oxidation.

This shows us how a fantastic, visionary and romantic scientist acted amid the
troubled scenario of Central Europe between the 1930s and 1950s, the period in which
Austria was annexed by Nazi Germany and subjugated by military and mechanistic
interests. Viktor Schauberger, with his uncommon sensitivity and profound observations
of natural rhythms, came into direct confrontation with the dominant scientific
view. While orthodox science anchored itself in the Second Law of Thermodynamics and
in explosion technologies, Viktor Schauberger proposed implosion, regeneration and living
energy — concepts that seemed too mystical for his time. Frustrated at not seeing his ideas
understood or applied in an ethical way and, after being forced to collaborate with military
projects during the Second World War, the author died disillusioned, in the face of a
humanity that insisted on walking against the living synchrony of nature. I leave here
Viktor Schauberger's maxim: "Comprehend and copy nature!" — just as permaculture
proposes.

Final considerations
Considering the information cited so far in this article, I bring some important reflections
on Schauberger's ideas, published nearly a hundred years ago, in light of
the present day. The world population grew 112% between 1950 and 1990 and 51% between
1990 and 2022 (World Population Prospects, 2022). The peak of population growth is intimately
linked to the systematization of agriculture (Evenson & Gollin, 2003) — the so-called "green
revolution" with synthetic fertilizers, agricultural mechanization with large machines and
high-yield seeds. (Ritchie & Roser, 2018) connects the expansion of the systematization of irrigation
and of the treatment, channeling and distribution of "potable" water to population growth. As
said, Viktor Schauberger associated this "development" with the increased incidence of
cancer. A study published in the journal BMJ Oncology in 2023 revealed a 79% increase
in the disease since the 1990s — precisely the 40 years of age of the generation of the
1950s.
Thus, we have a complex question: at the same time that we criticize the common practices of
agriculture and the management of waters and soils, the exploitation of raw materials,
mining, oil extraction and the food industry, we must remember that we and
our ancestors are here, the fruit of an immense world population, shaped and
inserted, from a certain point on, into this very system. No less important, we use at
least some of its resources every day and also feel on our own skin the consequences
of our habits.
It is very complex both to criticize and to provide solutions on a large scale and in the short term to
real problems in a worldwide context where people are working, raising their
children and seeking a better life. Like Schauberger, the book "The Hidden Life of
Trees" (Wohlleben, 2017) also cites how nature's rhythm is slow in its creation,
defense and regeneration. Schauberger, in his predictions regarding inadequate management of and interaction
with nature, as well as his proposal to copy the natural dynamics of water,
sought solutions such as the regulation of watercourses, storage and management — and, in
the same way, permaculture and its niches, such as agroforestry, seek to anticipate these
natural processes of regeneration.
We know that permaculture, before any grafting or bioconstruction technique, appeals
to solutions from the micro to the macro, local scale, change in the environment where one
lives and, from there, it makes sense to expand to the world. While public policies permeate the
cabinets of Congress and ministries for years and public incentive for such policies is slow,
while enforcement of environmental laws is nearly nonexistent and has opponents in Congress
itself, we must think about how to reduce our lives to this natural flow with a
certain urgency.
Reducing is not just consuming less; it is aligning our habits with the metabolism of the Earth
itself. As (Capra & Luisi, 2014) suggest in their book "The Systems View of Life," living
systems operate through closed cycles, cooperation, energy efficiency and equilibrium. When
we reduce waste, speed, noise and even excesses in relationships,
