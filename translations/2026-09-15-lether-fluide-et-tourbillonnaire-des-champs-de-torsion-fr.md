## Bruce DePalma's Balls and Gyroscopes

### Variable Weight and Invariable Mass in Classical Physics

To understand why mass is considered invariable in the physics accepted until today, it is useful to clarify what the mass and weight of an object are and to distinguish between them.

When I lift a stone, I must exert a force to counteract its weight. I realize that its weight p is the force that pulls it vertically downward onto the ground. The larger the stone, the more matter it contains and the heavier it is. The quantity of matter is called mass m. Weight p is proportional to mass.

**p = m × g**

According to classical physics, mass m (or m₀ in quantum physics) is a characteristic of the object. It is the same regardless of where the object is located. However, the gravitational force varies according to location.

Indeed, the weight of an object—the gravitational force—is due to the influence exerted by the Earth's mass on this object. This influence is called the force of gravity (denoted g). It is proportional to the planet's mass. This is why the weight of an object differs depending on which planet it is placed on. Compared to its weight on Earth, an object is 6 times lighter on the Moon, but 2.64 times heavier on Jupiter.

On Earth, gravity decreases when one moves away from the Earth's center. It therefore decreases with altitude (9.81 N/kg in Paris; 9.78 N/kg at 9 km altitude). At 6400 km high, its value is 4 times weaker than at the surface. Since the Earth is flattened at the poles, gravity is greater at the poles than at the equator (9.83 N/kg at the poles, 9.81 in Paris, and 9.78 at the equator).

Entropy and the death of the universe
Thermodynamic science was built starting from the study of the variations of
temperature of bodies when they are placed in various environmental conditions. It
rests on two principles, posed as postulates, suggested by experimental facts.

First principle of thermodynamics
Quantitative equivalence of energies: Mechanical energy can transform itself
into heat and inversely, and the energies are equal. For example in friction,
mechanical energy is transformed into heat.

Second principle of thermodynamics
In fact, heat energy has an inferior quality to mechanical energy. Indeed, if
mechanical energy can easily dissipate into heat, it is much more difficult to
transform heat into mechanical energy. It is however possible, for example in steam
engines. However there exists a fundamental limitation, namely that heat can
naturally pass only from a hot body to a cold body and not the inverse. The
evolution continues until an equilibrium is reached between the two bodies.
                                        Entropy

concept invented by Rudolf Clausius in 1865. When the body loses its organization,
its entropy increases. The entropy of an isolated body, that is to say fed by no
energy, can only increase.
