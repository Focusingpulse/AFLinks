# Dynamic Hydroelectric Energy

## The "Suction Turbine" or "Jet Turbine" of Viktor Schauberger

Hydroelectric engineering, to this day, deals almost exclusively with two variables: one is the height difference between the headwater and the turbine, and the other is the quantity of water that can flow through the turbines.

A third important variable, the velocity of water flow, is generally not considered important. It is taken into account only as the velocity resulting from the release of water pressure related to the height difference and dependent upon it, but not as an important factor in itself. In fact, current hydroelectric facility designs normally exclude the utilization of the dynamic energy potential inherent in the free flow of water. A dam destroys this natural energy potential by bringing water from its dynamic flowing state to a static state, a total absence of movement.

If we study the writings of Viktor Schauberger and Ludwig Herbrand, we discover that the energy inherent in free and unobstructed water flow can potentially be much greater than what can be obtained through the exclusive use of pressure resulting from altitude difference.

In the watermills and old ironworks of the pre-industrial era, normal water flow was used instead of pressure induced by altitude.

### Schauberger

In recent times, it was Viktor Schauberger, the Austrian inventor and brilliant observer of nature's mechanisms, who first advocated for the use of greater water velocity rather than water pressure for hydroelectric power generation. In 1930, he obtained a patent for what he called the Jet Turbine (Strahlturbine). (1)

The principles used by Schauberger to increase water velocity were the jet configuration of the water inlet pipe and the promotion, through spiral ribs inside the jet, of a vortex movement of the water.

Schauberger's patent gives us two very important clues about innovative changes in hydroelectric technology.

The first is that a pipe configured as a funnel or jet will increase the velocity of water flow by restricting the space available for water to flow. This velocity increase is especially large if the funnel or jet allows or even encourages the water to form a characteristic flow pattern known as a vortex. This vortex pattern itself has a tendency, quite independent of the jet effect, to increase water velocity, decrease its temperature, and increase water density.

The second innovation proposed by Schauberger is a revolutionary turbine design, which achieves rotation at very high speeds while avoiding the usual cavitation difficulties found in normal high-speed turbine designs. In fact, Schauberger's turbine wheel is conical in shape, with "ribs" that descend in a spiral down the cone's surface in a corkscrew shape, and is situated in the center of the water jet. The corkscrew-shaped turbine wheel divides the water flow, absorbs the dynamic energy of the water, and allows the flow to continue without major interruptions. Turbines of current design "cut" the water into thousands of destructive counterflows and crossed vortices, thus wasting much of the available energy and causing the common problem of cavitation—a super-rapid corrosion and destruction of turbine blade material. Below is the description of this new type of turbine according to Schauberger's patent number 117,749:

> "The object of the invention is a hydroelectric machine that utilizes the living energy of a water jet for the purpose of generating power.
>
> According to the invention, the turbine wheel is a cone with corkscrew-shaped blades.
>
> The cone is aligned with its axis in the direction of the jet's axis. In this manner, the water jet is divided and deflected from its course and thus delivers all its living energy to the rotating cone in such a way that, provided the length of the cone and the width of its base are in a correct relationship with each other and provided the blades are placed at the correct angle—these parameters depending on the velocity of the water jet—the water will exit the machine without agitation.
>
> The illustration is an approximate schematic representation of the invention.
>
> The rotating cone, which is aligned with its axis (1) in the direction of the water jet exiting the jet tube (2), is formed by corkscrew-shaped blades (3). The ends (4) of these blades (3) are bent slightly upward against the direction of the incoming water jet to cause a deflection of the jet and transfer as much as possible of the living energy of the jet to the rotating cone.
>
> Inside the jet tube (2) there are screw-shaped ribs (5) that promote a rotation, which according to actual observations increases the velocity of the water jet and the efficiency of the machine.
>
> **PATENT CLAIMS:**
>
> - A jet turbine, distinguished by the fact that in the path of the water jet and aligned with its axis so as to divide the jet, there is a turbine in the shape of a cone, whose surface is formed by corkscrew-like blades.
> - Jet turbine according to claim 1, distinguished by a jet tube (2) with ribs (5) inclined in the direction of rotation of the turbine wheel."

This patent was applied for in 1926 and granted in 1930. It appears that Schauberger actually used a small turbine of this design in a water stream near the forester's building during those years to generate electricity, but no reliable records are available. (2)

### Herbrand

Another example of using the dynamic powers of flowing water was documented by Ludwig Herbrand, a German engineer who, as a student in the mid-1930s, was called to evaluate and calculate parameters for some generators and exciter units that had recently been installed at the Rheinfelden power plant, as well as to design overload protection and relevant switching mechanisms for these generators.

He was also asked to compare the generators with those of another power plant that had been described in an article in a specialized journal.

To the great consternation of the then young and inquisitive engineering student, it seemed that the generators under examination were supplying more electrical power than they should, according to accepted theory. One of the generators at the Rheinfelden power plant, with 50 cubic meters of water per second and an altitude difference of only one meter, supplied as much power as a generator at Ryburg-Schwörstadt, which had a capacity of 250 cubic meters of water per second and an altitude difference from headwater to turbine of 12 meters. (3)

This fact was confirmed by Professor Finzi, the designer of the turbines and generators, who told the young Herbrand:

> "Don't worry about this. It's correct. The generator has been running smoothly for some time. Do the calculations backwards and you will see for yourself. We are electrical engineers. Those other problems are not ours to solve—we leave them to the water experts. We have repeated our measurements and the generator's power is exactly as specified. The only thing is that no one knows about it." (4)

Herbrand was soon drafted into the army, and World War II prevented him from investigating the subject further. Only much later, in the 1970s and 1980s, did Herbrand resume the calculations from his engineering exams and attempt—so far without success—to interest industry and government in this different and more efficient use of hydroelectric energy.

## Technical Data

I will attempt to outline the technical data here, using calculations based on accepted formulas and physical considerations confirmed by actual experiments, to demonstrate that with a different approach to hydroelectric engineering, we could obtain significantly more electrical energy than is currently extracted from water resources, with simpler machinery and less expense, as well as fewer disturbances to the environment.

As mentioned earlier, current hydroelectric engineering works with water pressure, obtained as a result of the altitude difference between headwaters and the turbine location. This pressure, when released through the turbine, results in momentary acceleration of the water and thus a certain water jet velocity.

This velocity is calculated with the formula:

```
v = √(2 · g · h)
```

where v is velocity, g is Earth's gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/sec²), and h is the altitude difference measured in meters.

**Example:** An altitude of 12 m results in a velocity of √(2 · 9.81 · 12) = 15.3 m/sec.

The progression of velocity in relation to altitude difference is shown in the following table:

### Calculation

The electrical energy that can be obtained from water is calculated based on flow velocity and water mass—that is, the magnitude of flow measured in cubic meters per second—according to the formula:

```
E_kin = (m/2) · v² (kw)
```

**Example:** Assuming a velocity of 25 m/sec and a mass of 5 cubic meters per second:

```
5 / 2 = 2.5 · 25 · 25 = 1,562.5 kw
```

For comparison, some additional examples are offered (assuming a constant small water flow of only 2 cubic meters per second):

The graphical representation makes clear that a velocity increase brings progressively greater energy increases. Therefore, the greater the water velocity, the higher the overall efficiency of the power plant.

However, for the purpose of using hydroelectric energy to generate electrical power, it is quite irrelevant whether water velocity results from pressure obtained through altitude difference or is obtained in some other way, such as by encouraging the natural tendency of water to flow. And it seems we can increase water flow velocity almost at will.

## How to Increase Electrical Output

There are two basic variables in hydroelectric engineering that determine electrical output. They are the quantity of water available and the flow velocity. The first variable, water quantity, depends largely on location and generally cannot be increased through human intervention.

It is the second variable—water flow velocity—that can be manipulated in many ways. Apart from increasing water pressure, which is a comparatively inefficient way to increase flow velocity, this parameter can be influenced by other, simpler and more cost-effective engineering solutions.

It is a common principle in rocketry to increase the flow velocity of hot exhaust gases through a restriction in the path of these gases. This is called the jet principle and has been successfully used for decades.

The same principle can be used to increase the velocity of a water flow, such as a river. In fact, when a river is forced, by the natural configuration of the terrain, to flow through a narrow gorge, the velocity at the narrowest point is much greater than before and after the river passes through the gorge. This effect can be harnessed by seeking a natural gorge or by artificially narrowing a riverbed to induce an increase in water velocity.

Another way to increase water flow velocity is to promote the formation of a longitudinal vortex. This is a rotating or rolling movement whose axis coincides with the direction of water flow. These vortices have the property of causing an increase in flow velocity and a contraction of the diameter of the space needed by the water mass. They also cause a decrease in water temperature and thus an increase in its density. (The highest specific density of water is reached at a temperature of +4°C.)

Water has a natural tendency to form vortices, especially if its flow is accelerated by some external influence such as gravity. We can observe this by noting the whirlpool with which a full bathtub or sink empties, or any other container full of water, if the water is forced to flow through a tube connected to a hole in the bottom of the container. But even a simple water tap, releasing a flow of water, will show this same phenomenon if the water flows relatively calmly, without bubbles or agitation. As water gains velocity, it forms a clearly funnel-shaped vortex right before our eyes.

A confirmation of this tendency of vortices to increase water velocity (or in other words, to decrease resistance to water flow) comes from experiments conducted in 1952 at the Stuttgart Technical University by Prof. Franz Pöpel and Viktor Schauberger.

The experiments were conducted with pipes of different materials and different shapes, to determine whether materials or shapes had an influence on resistance to water flow in pipes.

It appears that the best results were achieved with copper pipes, and that this material caused less resistance to water flow than even the smooth glass pipes used for comparison. But the most important fact emerging from these experiments is that, when using a certain pipe configured in a spiral, based on the shape of the kudu antelope's horn, friction in this pipe decreased with an increase in velocity and, at a certain point, water flowed with negative resistance. (5)

## Theory and Practice

The best theory is not worth the paper it is written on if it cannot be put into practice. Therefore, we will examine the practical utilization of these principles in hydroelectric engineering.

The goal is to increase water flow velocity to such a degree that the resulting jet releases more kinetic energy than the conventional use of water pressure achieved with comparable means.

**Step 1:**
As a first step, the normal flow of a river is brought to a higher velocity through the resource of a wall that gradually restricts the riverbed. This will increase the normal flow velocity of 2–5 m/sec to a considerable 10–15 m/sec.

**Step 2:**
At this point, to further increase velocity, we must provide a flow channel that more closely resembles the shape of a natural vortex. We do this by channeling the already rapidly flowing water at the narrowest point of the riverbed into an approximately round "funnel" or "jet tube" that gradually further restricts the diameter of the water's flow channel and thus causes a further velocity increase.

To assist this process, we can promote the formation of a vortex in the funnel or jet tube that will ensure the water exits the jet at considerable velocity. This is done either through spiral ribs inside the jet tube as proposed by Schauberger, or by forming the entire tube into a slightly "corkscrew" configuration.

Installing a turbine and generator at the point of water jet release, preferably of the design proposed by Schauberger, will now provide a much greater electrical power output than achieved by comparable means in the conventional manner.

When Step 1 is not possible because the river is too small, or when we simply want to adapt existing power plants to utilize the dynamic energy of water flow, Step 2 can still be cost-effectively combined with current small hydroelectric plant design, modifying the shape of the supply pipe to a funnel or jet tube configuration, thus obtaining part of the velocity increase from the normal use of gravity and part through the specific action of the jet effect and vortex flow.

## No Theoretical Limit

Are there limits to the velocity at which a water jet can be made to flow? This is a question we obviously must ask ourselves before embarking on this type of project.

It appears that theoretically there are no limitations, provided the vortex flow mode is used.

If water is forced to flow in straight pipes, resistance increases with increasing velocity. This is not the case when we allow water to flow in its natural mode, accommodating the resulting vortex in our pipe design.

In this case, resistance can be very low and even negative, as demonstrated by the experiments conducted in Stuttgart.

To estimate the potential benefits of using the dynamic powers inherent in water flow, we can conservatively assume that we should be able to obtain, without particular difficulties, velocities between 40 and 50 m/sec. This is an estimate based on Herbrand's observation that a velocity of 35 m/sec was achieved at the Rheinfelden power plant.

We can see from the previous statistical tables that 45 m/sec of velocity is equivalent to an altitude differential of more than 100 meters.

Assuming a water flow of 10 m³/sec, we can predict (at v = 45 m/sec) a power output of 10 megawatts. This is a considerable amount of energy and can be obtained practically anywhere along the normal course of a river, without the costly and environmentally questionable practice of building a dam and artificial lake to obtain 100 meters of altitude difference.

If it is true that water flow velocity can be increased almost at will and with comparatively simple means at a fraction of the cost of current hydroelectric designs, someone might ask: **Why aren't we using this obviously superior method?**

## Fixed Ideas and the "Law of Conservation of Energy"

It is very difficult to unlearn something one studied, especially if what was learned was needed later to pass an exam. The weight of so-called "natural laws" used to support these doctrines makes it even more difficult for anyone to stand up and say "Hey, we've overlooked something here!"

Of course, "everyone knows" that water has to be pressurized if we're going to use it for hydroelectric power generation. And everyone also knows that hydroelectric engineering technology has been on the right track since the beginning of the century. Why bother looking further?

Ludwig Herbrand disagrees. He has waged an incessant battle for more than 20 years to gain recognition for this new technology. He has literally sent hundreds of letters to government and industry, as well as international institutions, with very many negative responses, telling him more or less politely that his proposals are not welcome.

It is difficult to break through this "knowledge" barrier, especially when experts believe they see a violation of the law of conservation of energy.

Conservation of energy is invoked when calculations do not seem to allow for greater energy production. But in this case we have a factor that has been neglected in our calculations, not a violation of conservation laws.

## Water is an Energy Accumulator

There is some evidence that the decrease in water temperature, which is a consequence of vortex movement, provides the energy to water that we then see as kinetic energy in the form of increased water velocity. In this way, a vortex would transform heat (which is random molecular motion) into dynamic energy (which is motion in a determined direction).

Schauberger emphasized that water could store enormous amounts of energy when heated. In an article about the Danube River, he states that to heat one cubic meter of water by just 0.1°C requires about 42,700 kgm of energy, and says this demonstrates the enormous energies that are bound when water is heated and released when water cools. (6)

Thermodynamics, as taught in our schools and universities, does not allow for a bidirectional transformation of heat with low temperature differences. Thermodynamics is based on the observation of steam engines and has little to do with nature, although some insist that the so-called laws of thermodynamics are "natural laws."

However, thermodynamics cannot explain certain natural phenomena. (7)

In calculations of electrical energy production, velocity is not considered separately, but as a result exclusively and solely of altitude difference. That is like saying there is no other way to achieve water velocity than through pressure. This may be the experts' way of calculating, but physical reality is different. Water velocity, as we have seen, is not exclusively linked to pressure but can be achieved by different means.

Therefore, the correct way to calculate is to start from velocity and arrive at power output. Altitude difference and the equivalent velocity calculated in the formula given above are a special case, not the general rule.

We must distinguish between pressure-induced equivalent velocity and the natural velocity of flowing water. That is, we must distinguish between gravity and inertia. These two forces are similar in their effects, but are nonetheless two clearly different forces (*). This article does not allow for a detailed examination of the physical forces involved. For those interested in this subject, I would like to refer to an article I wrote on the foundations of physics in EXPLORE! in 1992. (8)

To anyone wishing to utilize the dynamic powers of water, I recommend studying the writings of Viktor Schauberger, the great master of hydraulic engineering who remained on the margins of official science all his life because his views were radically different from those of the professors of his time.
