# Morphic Resonance – Rupert Sheldrake

*By Claudia Vece, Quantasia Blog, 8 January 2020. Translated from Italian by Forge.*

Rupert Sheldrake, British biologist and essayist, in his works *A New Science of Life* (1981) and *The Presence of the Past* (1988), develops the theory of morphogenetic fields: invisible forces present in a system (a complex organism composed of many parts) that do not identify it with any one of its components, but with the system itself. The "morphic field," responsible for the organization, structure, and form of the system, would have its own memory, determined by the contribution of each member.

The book explains: — how the past forms and behaviors of organisms determine those of similar organisms in the present through morphic resonance; — the immaterial connections that allow direct communication across time and space.

Sheldrake proposes that morphogenetic fields are responsible for the characteristic form and organization of systems in biology, chemistry, and physics — and that they have measurable physical effects. Using his theory of morphic resonance, Sheldrake has been able to reinterpret the regularities of nature as more similar to habits than to immutable laws, offering a new understanding of life and consciousness.

Sheldrake demonstrates that the past forms and behavior of organisms influence present organisms through direct immaterial connections across time and space. This can explain why new chemicals become easier to crystallize all over the world the more often their crystals have already formed, and why when laboratory rats have learned to navigate a maze in one place, rats elsewhere seem to learn it more easily. With over two decades of new research and data, Rupert Sheldrake provides an even stronger case for the validity of the theory of formative causation, which can radically transform the way we see our world and our future.

## The three basic principles of the Formative Causation hypothesis:

1. Morphogenetic fields are a field that until now has not been recognized or taken into consideration by physics, and cannot be understood through classical mechanistic conceptions — they require entirely new concepts.

2. Organisms evolve. They have a history and, thanks to a process called morphic resonance, they contain a memory within themselves. Every individual belonging to a species draws on the collective memory of the species and tunes in to its past members, in turn contributing to the further development of the species itself.

3. Morphic fields are memories of influence within space-time, localized in and around the systems that they organize and structure with their information.

The basic function of morphic fields is to impose order on the indeterminism of the systems they preside over: they work at the subatomic level, acting as programmed and schematized restrictions on the multitude of probable events. Morphic fields guide systems toward specific goals, which represent the limits toward which a dynamic system is attracted (what René Thom, in his chaos and catastrophe theory, calls "attractors").

Rupert Sheldrake (Newark-on-Trent, 28 June 1942), PhD, is a biologist and author known for his theory of morphic resonance. At the University of Cambridge he worked in developmental biology as a fellow of Clare College. He was chief plant physiologist at the International Crops Research Institute for the Semi-Arid Tropics in Hyderabad, India. From 2005 to 2010 he was director of the Perrott-Warrick project for research into unexplained human and animal abilities, funded by Trinity College, Cambridge.

Sheldrake is the author of over 90 scientific articles and 9 books, and co-author of 6 books. He was among the top 100 global thought leaders for 2013, ranked by the Duttweiler Institute of Zurich, Switzerland's leading think tank. On ResearchGate, the largest online scientific and academic network, his RG score of 33.5 places him among the top 7.5% of researchers, based on citations of his peer-reviewed publications.
