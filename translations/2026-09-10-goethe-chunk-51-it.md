It is worth noting that this judgment by Steiner on the "objectivity" of Goethe's thought coincides with that expressed (in 1822) by Johann Christian August Heinroth (1773-1843), professor of psychiatry at Leipzig, and thus commented by Goethe himself: "In his Anthropology, a work to which we will have occasion to return, Dr. Heinroth speaks benevolently of me and my works, and defines my way of proceeding as genial, consisting – he writes – in the fact that my thought works objectively or, in other terms, does not separate itself from objects, but the elements of these, their sensible images, are absorbed and intimately penetrated by it; that my seeing is already a thinking, my thinking a seeing – a procedure to which he cannot deny his applause (Decisive impulse of a single acute judgment in Works, vol. V, p. 57).

I think you know, in this regard, the definition of truth given by Thomas Aquinas: "Veritas est adaequatio rei et intellectus."

This definition can however mean – as Heidegger rightly notes (On the Essence of Truth – Armando, Rome 1999, p. 37) – both that truth is adaequatio intellectus ad rem (adequation of thinking or knowing to the thing) and that it is adaequatio rei ad intellectum (adequation of the thing to thinking or knowing).

Well, there is no doubt that the first of these two formulas fits perfectly with Goethe's way of proceeding (and with the science of the spirit), while the second fits no less perfectly with the way of proceeding of that science which "adapts" precisely every thing to the thinking or knowing (quantitative) of the intellect or, as one hears said more and more often today, of the "computational mind."

To understand the significance of this difference, try to imagine a person who, in the hope of being able to overcome their anxiety, anguish or despair, turns to a psychometrist: that is, to a psychologist expert in the measurement of psychic phenomena, obtained through the administration of "mental tests."

Well, what is likely to happen? That the first tries to speak and open their soul to the second, but the latter pays little or no attention, entirely taken by the (protocol) requirement to administer tests.

Certainly, such a situation is perhaps paradoxical; nevertheless it exemplifies quite faithfully the way science behaves toward nature.

Even the "naturometrists" (to call them thus) do not in fact listen to that soul which reveals itself through natural beings and phenomena, entirely taken by the (protocol) requirement to weigh and measure them, thus transforming them into numbers.

The fact is that only one who takes seriously, and therefore listens to, their own soul can take seriously, and therefore listen to, the soul of the world (Anima mundi) (let us not forget, in this regard, that Lucifer is a soul without spirit, while Ahriman is precisely a spirit without soul).

You see, almost everywhere today it is taught (once again following Kant) that science has nothing to do with morality, because science is one thing and morality another. But this is not true.
