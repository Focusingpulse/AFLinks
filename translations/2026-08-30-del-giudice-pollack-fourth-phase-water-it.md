# Emilio Del Giudice Explains Gerald Pollack's Discovery of the Fourth Phase of Water

*Originally published in Italian on A.CS.I. (Associazione CranioSacrale Italia). The Italian physicist Emilio Del Giudice discusses a property of water discovered by Gerald Pollack — a discovery that clarifies one of the greatest mysteries of biology, first highlighted in the 1950s by Nobel laureate in medicine Albert Szent-Györgyi.*

---

Let us briefly summarize. In living matter, an enormous quantity of chemical reactions take place. These are possible only if some substance is capable of releasing electrons. But which of the substances present in living matter is able to release them? That is the mystery. Szent-Györgyi thought it was water. Yet it takes 12.6 electronvolts to free an electron from a water molecule — an energy level (nuclear or thermal) incompatible with life. Where, then, do the electrons come from that are needed for the chemical reactions constantly occurring in the body?

As Albert Szent-Györgyi had intuited, they come precisely from water. Here is Emilio Del Giudice's account.

In the early 2000s, the American physicist Gerald Pollack of the University of Seattle experimentally observed that water near surfaces, to a depth of several microns, has properties different from bulk water and is capable of readily donating electrons.

The water molecule, when isolated, requires 12.6 electronvolts to liberate an electron, but the same is not true when that same molecule is together with other water molecules and when it is near a surface.

We must remember that in living matter everything is organized through membranes, and there is no point in our organism that is more than one millionth of a centimeter from some membrane. This means that there is no water molecule (99% of all the molecules present in the body are water) that is more than one millionth of a centimeter from a membrane.

Pollack therefore demonstrates that water near a membrane has a different nature, and that it is sufficient to place the end of a wire into this water to obtain a current. This current, according to Pollack, is the expression of what was once called the vital force — a specific form of energy at work in biological organisms.

Del Giudice describes one of Pollack's experiments: "He fills a container with water whose walls are made of hydrophilic protein substances, then adds traces of dye, observes this colored liquid with a powerful microscope, and what does he discover? That the dye does not dissolve uniformly but leaves dark zones near the walls. He calls this layer the **exclusion zone**. In other words, he demonstrates that solutes cannot penetrate this zone. Not only that — Pollack discovers that in this zone, water readily donates electrons. In water with these particular characteristics, nothing dissolves, and solutes remain on the surfaces and organize themselves on the surfaces. This is the mechanism that gives rise to biological morphologies. If we have these regions of water into which nothing penetrates, the solutes concentrate in the spaces between them. And so architectures form — structures with their own morphology that are the radiograph of the coherence domains of water. In other words, these structures trace the structure of the organized regions of water — what Pollack calls exclusion zones."

*— Rosella Denicolò*
