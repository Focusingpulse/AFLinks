Viktor Schauberger's "suction turbine" or "jet turbine"

To this day, hydroelectric engineering deals almost exclusively with two variables: one is the difference in height between the head water and the turbine, and the other is the quantity of water that can flow through the turbines.

A third important variable, the velocity of the water flow, is generally not considered important. It is taken into consideration only as the velocity resulting from the release of water pressure related to and dependent on the difference in height, but not as an important factor in its own right. In fact, the current design of hydroelectric installations normally excludes the utilization of the dynamic energy potential inherent in the free flow of water. A dam destroys this natural energy potential by taking the water from its dynamic state of flow to a static state, a total absence of movement.

If we study the writings of Viktor Schauberger and Ludwig Herbrand, we discover that the energy inherent in the free and unobstructed flow of water can potentially be much greater than what can be obtained through the exclusive use of the pressure resulting from the difference in altitude. In the mills and old smithies of the pre-industrial era, a normal flow of water was used instead of pressure induced by altitude. Schauberger

In recent times, it was Viktor Schauberger, the Austrian inventor and brilliant observer of the mechanisms of nature, who first advocated the use of increased water velocity instead of water pressure for the production of hydroelectric energy. In 1930 he obtained a patent for what he called the Jet Turbine (Strahlturbine). (1)

The principles used by Schauberger to increase the velocity of the water were the jet configuration of the water intake pipe and the promotion, by means of spiral ribs inside the jet, of a vortex movement of the water.

Schauberger's patent gives us two very important clues about the innovative changes in hydroelectric technology.

The first is that a pipe configured as a funnel or jet will increase the velocity of the water flow by restricting the space available in which the water can flow. This increase in velocity is especially great if the funnel or jet allows or even fosters the water forming a characteristic flow pattern known as a vortex. This vortex pattern itself has a tendency, quite independent of the jet effect, to increase the velocity of the water, to decrease its temperature, and to increase the density of the water.

The second innovation proposed by Schauberger is a revolutionary turbine design, which achieves rotation at very high speeds and at the same time avoids the usual cavitation difficulties encountered in normal high-speed turbine designs. In fact, Schauberger's turbine wheel is cone-shaped, with "ribs" descending in a spiral down the surface of the cone in corkscrew fashion, and it is situated in the center of the jet of water. The corkscrew-shaped turbine wheel divides the water flow, absorbs the dynamic energy of the water, and allows the flow to continue without major interruptions. Today's turbines "cut" the water into thousands of destructive counter-currents and crossed vortices, thus wasting much of the available energy and causing the common problem of cavitation, a super-fast corrosion and destruction of the turbine blade material. Below is the description of this new type of turbine according to Schauberger's patent number 117,749:


---


"The object of the invention is a hydroelectric machine, which uses the living energy of a jet of water for the purpose of generating energy.

According to the invention, the turbine wheel is a cone with corkscrew-shaped blades.

The cone is aligned with its axis in the direction of the axis of the jet. In this way, the jet of water is divided and deflected from its course and, therefore, delivers all its living energy to the rotating cone in such a way that, provided the length of the cone and the width of its base are in a correct relationship to each other and provided the blades are placed at the correct angle, these parameters depending on the velocity of the water jet, the water will exit the machine without agitation.

The illustration is an approximate schematic representation of the invention.

The rotating cone, which is aligned with its axis (1) in the direction of the water jet exiting the jet tube (2), is formed by corkscrew-shaped blades (3).

The ends (4) of these blades (3) are bent slightly upward against the direction of the arriving water jet to cause a deflection of the jet and to transfer the greatest possible amount of the living energy of the jet to the rotating cone.

Inside the jet tube (2) there are screw-shaped ribs (5) that promote a rotation which, according to actual observations, increases the velocity of the water jet and the efficiency of the machine.

PATENT CLAIMS:


---


• A jet turbine, distinguished by the fact that in the path of the water jet and aligned with its axis in such a way that the jet is divided, there is a cone-shaped [eel-like] turbine whose surface is formed by corkscrew-like blades. • Jet turbine according to claim 1, distinguished by a jet tube (2) with ribs (5) inclined in the direction of rotation of the turbine wheel."

This patent was applied for in 1926 and granted in 1930. It appears that Schauberger actually used a small turbine of this design in a stream of water near the foresters' lodge building during those years, to generate electricity, but no reliable records are available. (2) Herbrand

Another example of the use of the dynamic powers of flowing water has been documented by Ludwig Herbrand, a German engineer who, as a student in the mid-1930s, was called in to evaluate and calculate the parameters of some generators and exciter units that had recently been installed at the Rheinfelden power plant, as well as to design the electrical overload protection and the relevant switching mechanisms for these generators.

He was also asked to compare the generators with those of another power plant that had been described in an article in a specialized journal.

To the great consternation of the then young and inquisitive engineering student, it appeared that the generators under examination were supplying more electric energy than they should have, according to accepted theory. One of the generators at the Rheinfelden power plant, with 50 cubic meters of water per second and a difference in altitude of only one meter, was supplying as much energy as a generator at a plant near Ryburg-Schwörstadt, which had a capacity of 250 cubic meters of water per second and a difference in altitude from the head waters to the turbine of 12 meters. (3)

This fact was confirmed by Professor Finzi, the designer of the turbines and generators, who tells the young Herbrand:

"Don't worry about this. It is correct. The generator has been running without problems for some time. Do the calculations backwards and you will verify it for yourself. We are electrical engineers. Those other problems are not ours to solve; we leave them to the water experts. We have repeated our measurements and the power of the generator is exactly as specified. It's just that nobody knows about it." (4)

Herbrand was soon drafted into the army and the Second World War did not allow him to research the subject further. Only much later, in the 1970s and 1980s, did Herbrand take up again the calculations he had made for his engineering examinations and tried — so far without success — to interest industry and government in this different and more efficient use of hydroelectric energy.

Technical data


---


I will try to outline here the technical data, using calculations based on accepted formulas and physical considerations confirmed by real experiments, to demonstrate that with an approach different from current hydroelectric engineering, we could obtain significantly more electrical energy than is presently being extracted from water resources, with simpler machinery and less expense, as well as fewer disturbances to the environment.

As mentioned before, current hydroelectric engineering works with water pressure, obtained as a result of the difference in altitude between the headwaters and the location of the turbine. This pressure, when released through the turbine, results in a momentary acceleration of the water and, consequently, a certain velocity of the jet of water.

This velocity is calculated with the formula.

v = Sqrt 2 . g . h

v is the velocity, g the gravitational acceleration of the earth (9.81 m/sec2) and h the difference in altitude measured in meters.

Example: An altitude difference of 12 m results in a velocity of Sqrt 2 . 9.81 . 12 = 15.3 m/sec.

However, for the purpose of using hydroelectric energy to generate electrical energy, it is quite irrelevant whether the velocity of the water is the result of pressure obtained through a difference in altitude, or whether it is obtained in some other manner, such as by encouraging the natural tendency of water to flow. And it seems we can increase the flow velocity of water almost at will.

How to increase electrical output

There are two basic variables in hydroelectric engineering that determine electrical output. They are the quantity of water available and the velocity of the flow. The first variable, the quantity of water available, depends largely on location and is generally not subject to increase by human intervention.

It is the second variable, the velocity of the water flow, that can be manipulated in many ways. Apart from increasing water pressure, which is a comparatively inefficient way of increasing flow velocity, this parameter can be influenced by other, simpler and more cost-effective engineering solutions.

It is a common principle in rocketry to increase the flow velocity of hot exhaust gases by means of a restriction in the path of flow of these gases. This is called the jet principle and has been used successfully for decades.

The same principle can be used to increase the velocity of a flow of water, such as a river. In fact, when a river is forced, by the natural configuration of the terrain, to flow through a narrow gorge, the velocity at the narrowest point is much higher than before and after the river's passage through the gorge. This effect can be exploited by seeking out a natural gorge or by artificially narrowing the bed of a river to bring about an increase in the velocity of the water.

Another way to increase the flow velocity of water is to promote the formation of a longitudinal vortex. This is a rolling or turning movement whose axis coincides with the direction of the water flow. These vortices have the property of causing an increase in flow velocity and a contraction of the diameter of the space needed by the mass of water. They also cause a decrease in the temperature of the water and, consequently, an increase in its density. (The highest specific density of water is reached at a temperature of + 4° C.)

Water has a natural tendency to form vortices, especially if its flow is accelerated by some external influence such as gravity. We can observe this by noting the whirlpool with which a bathtub or sink full of water, or any other container full of water, empties if the water is forced to flow through a pipe connected to a hole in the bottom of the container. But even a simple water tap, releasing a flow of water, will show this same phenomenon if the water flows relatively calmly, without bubbles or agitation. As the water gains velocity, it forms a clearly funnel-shaped vortex right before our eyes.

A confirmation of this tendency of vortices to increase the velocity of water (or in other words to decrease the resistance to the flow of water) comes from experiments carried out in 1952 at the Technical University of Stuttgart by Prof. Franz Pöpel and Viktor Schauberger.

The experiments were conducted with pipes of different materials and different shapes, to determine whether the materials or the shapes had an influence on the resistance to water flow in the pipes.

It appears that the best results were achieved with copper pipes, and that this material caused less resistance to water flow than even the smooth glass pipes used as a comparison. But the most important fact to emerge from these experiments is that, when using a certain pipe configured in a spiral, based on the shape of the kudu antelope's horn, the friction in this pipe decreased with an increase in velocity and, at a certain point, the water flowed with negative resistance. (5)

Theory and practice


---


The best theory is not worth the paper it is written on, if it cannot be put into practice. Therefore, we shall examine the practical utilization of these principles in hydroelectric engineering.

The goal is to increase the flow velocity of water to such a degree that the resulting jet releases more kinetic energy than the conventional use of water pressure achieved with comparable means.

Step 1:

As a first step, the normal flow of a river is brought to a higher velocity by the expedient of a wall that gradually restricts the river bed. This will increase the normal flow velocity of 2 to 5 m/sec to a considerable 10 to 15 m/sec.

Step 2:

At this point, to further increase the velocity, we must provide a flow channel that more closely resembles the shape of a natural vortex. We do this by channeling the already fast-flowing water at the narrowest point of the river bed into an approximately round "funnel" or "jet tube" that gradually restricts the diameter of the water's flow channel further and, consequently, brings about an additional increase in velocity.

To assist this process, we can promote the formation of a vortex in the funnel or jet tube, which will ensure that the water exits the jet at a considerable velocity. This is done either by means of spiral ribs on the inside of the jet tube as Schauberger proposes, or by forming the whole tube into a slightly "corkscrew" configuration.

By installing a turbine and generator at the release point of the jet of water, preferably of the design proposed by Schauberger, we will now obtain a much higher electrical energy output than that achieved by comparable means in the conventional manner. Where step 1 is not possible because the river is too small, or where we simply wish to adapt existing power plants to use the dynamic energy of water flow, step 2 can still be combined cost-effectively with the current design of the small hydroelectric plant, by modifying the shape of the penstock to a funnel or jet tube configuration, thus obtaining part of the velocity increase from the normal use of gravity and another part through the specific action of the jet effect and the vortex flow.

No theoretical limit

Are there limits to the velocity at which a jet of water can be made to flow? This is a question we obviously must ask ourselves before embarking on this kind of project.

It appears that there are theoretically no limitations, as long as the vortex mode of flow is used.

If water is forced to flow in straight pipes, resistance increases with increasing velocity. Not so when we allow the water to flow in its natural mode, accommodating the resulting vortex in our pipe design. In this case, resistance can be very low and even negative, as demonstrated by the experiments carried out in Stuttgart.

To estimate the possible benefits of using the dynamic powers inherent in water flow, we can conservatively assume that we should be able to obtain, without particular difficulties, velocities between 40 and 50 m/sec. This is an estimate based on Herbrand's observation that a velocity of 35 m/sec was achieved at the Rheinfelden power plant.

We can see in the previous statistical tables that a velocity of 45 m/sec is equivalent to a difference in altitude of more than 100 meters.

And assuming we have a water flow of 10 m3/sec, we can predict (at v = 45 m/sec) an energy output of 10 megawatts. This is a considerable amount of energy and can be obtained practically anywhere along the normal course of a river, without the costly and environmentally questionable practice of building a dam and an artificial lake to obtain 100 meters of difference in altitude. If it is true that the flow velocity of water can be increased almost at will, and with comparatively simple means at a fraction of the cost of current hydroelectric designs, someone might ask: Why are we not using this obviously superior method?

Fixed ideas and the "law of conservation of energy"

It is very difficult to unlearn something one has studied, especially if what was learned was later needed to pass an exam. The weight of the so-called "natural laws" that are used to support these doctrines makes it even harder for anyone to stand up and say "hey, we have missed something here!"

Of course, "everyone knows" that water has to be pressurized if we are to use it for hydroelectric power generation. And everyone also knows that hydroelectric engineering technology has been well established since the beginning of the century. Why bother to search further?

Not so Ludwig Herbrand. He has waged an unceasing battle for more than 20 years to obtain recognition for this new technology. He has literally sent hundreds of letters to government and to industry, as well as to international institutions, with a great many negative replies, telling him more or less politely that his proposals are not welcome.

It is hard to break through this barrier of "knowledge," especially when the experts believe they see a violation of the law of conservation of energy.

Conservation of energy is invoked when the calculations do not seem to permit a higher energy output. But in this case we have a factor that has been neglected in our calculations, not a violation of the laws of conservation.

Water is an accumulator of energy


---


There is some evidence that the decrease in water temperature, which is a consequence of vortex movement, provides the energy to the water that we then see as kinetic energy in the form of increased water velocity. In this way, a vortex would transform heat (which is random molecular motion) into dynamic energy (which is motion in a determined direction). Schauberger stressed the fact that water can store enormous amounts of energy when heated. In an article about the Danube river he states that to heat one cubic meter of water by just 0.1 degrees C, approximately 42,700 kgm of energy are needed, and he says this demonstrates the enormous energies that are bound when water is heated and released when water cools. (6)

Thermodynamics, as taught in our schools and universities, does not permit a bidirectional transformation of heat at low temperature differences. Thermodynamics is based on the observation of steam engines and has little to do with nature, although some insist that the so-called laws of thermodynamics are "natural laws."

Thermodynamics, however, is not able to explain certain natural phenomena. (7)

In the calculations of electrical energy production, velocity is not considered separately, but as a result solely and exclusively of the difference in altitude. That is like saying there is no other way to achieve water velocity than pressure. That may be the experts' way of calculating, but physical reality is different. Water velocity, as we have seen, is not exclusively tied to pressure, but can be achieved by different means.

The correct way to calculate, therefore, is to start from the velocity and arrive at the power output. The difference in altitude and the equivalent velocity calculated in the formula given above are a special case, not the general rule.
