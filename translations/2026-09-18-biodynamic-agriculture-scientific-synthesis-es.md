1. Basis and systemic approach
Among the methods of organic agriculture, the biodynamic method, also known as
biodynamics, is the one that takes a holistic approach furthest, both in its basic
ideas and in practice. Historically, it is the origin of the development of organic
agriculture 1. The starting point of
biodynamics was a series of lectures given by Rudolf Steiner 2 in 1924, which were then
published under the title Geisteswissenschaftliche Grundlagen zum Gedeihen der Landwirtschaft –
(Spiritual foundations for the prosperity of agriculture) (Steiner, 1925), known as The
Agricultural Course 3. During this agricultural course an "Agricultural Experimental Circle of

biodynamics were developed, based on the understanding of the farm as an organism and agricultural individuality. The human

as a more or less self-contained living entity with the corresponding organs. An

Consequently, the interrelations between organs such as crops, orchards, wild nature,

through biodynamic practices. For example, through the continuous cycle between the soil, the
meadows, and the animal manure. This strengthening of organs should stimulate the self-regulating forces of
agriculture. The goal of this farming system is to guarantee sustainable fertility, good
production capacity, healthy agriculture, and high nutritional quality of the products. Today
these considerations correspond to terms such as resilience 4, which play an
important role in the current debate on sustainability (Darnhofer & Milestad, 2003, Hubenthal, 2012). In
its understanding of nature, biodynamics is based on a hierarchy of kingdoms (levels of action)
(Steiner, 1925; Wegmann, 1925): matter is shaped by a higher living element. Life is
ordered by the soul in animals and human beings. In the human being, the soul is structured
by the I or spiritual identity. In plants, for example, this specific identity is
represented by the species. The natural sciences, which today have an almost exclusively
reductionist view, are thus expanded with new dimensions, which is particularly important for
biology and agriculture. These dimensions can be attributed to the natural qualities in the
following way, in additive order: the mineral is above all physical-chemical, the plant
incorporates life, the animal the soul, and the human being the I.
In biodynamics an intense interpenetration of these levels is sought. These levels also
represent the kingdoms of nature. The higher levels participate in structuring the
lower ones: the soil (initially only mineral) is enlivened by the plants (living), which in turn
are reinforced with appropriate livestock keeping (element of the soul), which characterizes the
place, in correlation with the animal feed and the corresponding manure. The application of biodynamic
preparations, a cultural act of the human being, should promote both the self-regulation of a farm's organs and the
interaction of the processes within the soil and between it, the plants, and the livestock.
Referring to the concept of resilience, organic agriculture follows three successive stages of
self-regulation. Taking the example of the regulation of a pest such as the aphid in apple
orchards, they can be described as follows:
         1. Natural sprays (without synthetic chemical pesticides)
         2. Natural environment, for example favoring beneficial organisms through the use of hedges
         (agro-ecological)
         3. Understanding the natural development of the apple trees and supporting it by pruning and fertilizing the
         trees in order to support the integrity of the tree (Bloksma, 2002, Verhoog et al., 2003).
Verhoog (2009) concludes that biodynamic agriculture is the one that comes closest to the understanding of
integrity in the treatment of plants and animals.
Another important theme in agriculture is efficiency. It turns out that the biodynamic concept of a
mixed agricultural organism of farming and livestock has obvious advantages in terms of nitrogen
efficiency, humus balance, energy consumption, and climate protection (see, for example, Hülsbergen
& Rahmann, 2013).
Biodynamic agriculture is practiced today on all continents and in many different
cultures, as well as in the Arab world, in India, in China, etc. There are more than 5,000 Demeter-certified
farms worldwide, representing more than 160,000 ha of agricultural area, and individual
projects in 43 countries with a predominance in Europe. The biodynamic and Demeter
associations (grouped together in the Biodynamic Demeter International Federation) are present in more than 20 different
countries.

2. Foundations of biodynamic agriculture

The farm, an individualized organism in a living natural space
The goal of biodynamic agriculture is the individual configuration of each farm, taking into
account the qualities of the local landscape, the animals, and the people who live on the farm, in order
to constitute an organic whole: an agricultural organism or individuality (see Sattler and
Wistinghausen, 1989). A farm is considered "healthy" (Steiner, 1925) when it approaches autonomy,
with the smallest possible input of substances from outside (circular economy).
In terms of substances, this translates ideally into the need for a conversion of the whole
farm and a high proportion of feed produced on the farm (see Demeter, 2014). The individuality of the
farm is considered as forming part of the organism of the landscape. The elements of the landscape and
the various animal populations such as birds, earthworms, insects, or livestock are considered of
great importance for the balance between natural forces, closely linked to agricultural
production (Steiner 1925).

Enlivening the soil
The soil and the plant are considered as a unity. An excessive density of pests, fungi, or
weeds is the result of imbalances. The goal is therefore to "enliven" the soil as the key mediator
of substances in agriculture through diversified crop rotations and the application of
compost enlivened by specific preparations (Steiner, 1925), instead of simply fertilizing the
plants according to their needs.
All measures aimed at increasing soil fertility are therefore of great importance on
biodynamic farms. This also includes the method of raising and feeding the animals, the
treatment of the manure (including composting to obtain more stable forms of humus), and
diversified crop rotations.

The biodynamic preparations
An organism remains healthy if its own forces and the functional interactions between its organs
are intact. However, according to Steiner (1925), agriculture tends to abuse the vital forces
that organize it. To compensate for this over-exploitation, the biodynamic preparations are used
as a complement to the feed-manure cycle, whose use is established in the Demeter Standards
Notebook (Demeter International, 2014). These preparations are made from minerals, plants, and
animal components by the farmers themselves and are used in small quantities comparable to
homeopathic compositions. They have a balancing effect under one-sided growing conditions
and in this way promote the "natural development and integrity of the plant" (Lammerts
van Bueren & Struik 2005). Six plant preparations are used to improve the compost and the
decomposition of the manure (compost preparations), one sprayed preparation promotes
soil fertility ("horn manure" preparation) 5, and another sprayed preparation is used to
improve the ripening of the cultivated plants ("horn silica" preparation) 6. Horsetail
is also used as a prophylaxis against fungal attacks. It was above all the early
research on biodynamic agriculture that focused on testing the effect of the preparations
(see Spieß, 1978; Elsaidi, 1982).

Plant breeding
Biodynamic agriculture has developed its own methodology for plant breeding, both for
cereals and for vegetables. The central aspects of biodynamic plant breeding are, among
others, the preservation of the integrity of the plant, the transparency of the breeding work, and the nutritional
quality of the food (Demeter, 2014) (see chapter Plant Breeding).

Development of adapted methods
Biodynamics wants to describe the soil, plants, animals, or food in a more
holistic way than analytical methods based on isolated parameters allow. This
expanded understanding of nature has led to the development of new methods, for example, the
study of leaf metamorphosis, which represents the temporal evolution of plants (Bockemühl,
2003), or morphogenetic methods such as the sensitive crystallization of copper
chloride (Kahl, 2006).


---


Ruminant husbandry
An important characteristic of biodynamic agriculture is the keeping of ruminants. Through the
feed-manure cycle, the domestic animals with their soul quality act as a connecting
element. The Standards Notebooks include the obligation for Demeter farms to keep
a certain number of Livestock Units (LU) for the consumption of roughage (minimum 0.2
LU/ha) (Demeter, 2014). Among the breeding animals, ruminants, and in particular cows, occupy
a special place in biodynamic agriculture. Cow manure is considered of great value for
increasing soil fertility, and several organs of the cow are used as sheaths for the
elaboration of the biodynamic preparations (cf. Steiner, 1925). This is why cows are
found on most biodynamic farms.

High demands in matters of animal welfare
The high Demeter animal welfare standards can be considered the expression of the high
esteem with which domestic animals are raised. Let us cite, for example, the prohibition of
dehorning cattle, the limitation of the size of chicken flocks, or the possibility of
swarming in beekeeping. In addition, the goal is to promote the integrity and natural development of
plants and animals (Demeter, 2014).

Food for the body, the soul, and the spirit
According to Demeter principles, food should not only nourish, but also promote the
mental and spiritual development of the human being (Demeter, 2015b). This idea goes beyond the idea of
healthy eating. The quality of food products is very important for biodynamic
production (see the chapter Food Quality). The Demeter Standards Notebooks show
this importance through strict criteria for processors, for example, the prohibition of
homogenizing drinking milk (Demeter, 2014).

The soil
Several publications deal with the impact of biodynamic agriculture on the soil (cf. Turinek et al.
2009). The investigations generally focus on the humus content and on several parameters
related to the state and activity of soil microorganisms. The attention focuses on
two long-term trials: the FiBL DOC trial (Mäder et al., 2002) and the long-term fertilization
trial (LZDV) of the Forschungsring in Darmstadt (Raupp & Oltmanns, 2006). The DOC trial
conducted by FiBL in Therwil, Switzerland, compares biodynamic, organic, and conventional
fertilization since 1978, and the LZDV (long-term fertilization trial 1989-2009) studies the effect of the
biodynamic preparations on variants of composted manure. The two long-term trials
show a clearly positive influence of the biodynamic agriculture method on organic
carbon (Corg) in relation to the organic variant (Mäder et al., 2002; Raupp & Oltmanns, 2006).


---


Other subsequent investigations tend to focus on specific aspects of soil development:
Burkitt et al. (2007), except for a lower phosphorus content, found no differences in an
Australian study of soil properties and phosphorus contents in ten comparable biodynamic
and conventional pairs.
Likewise, Carpenter-Boggs (2000) could not reproduce in a significant way the differences in
a two-year comparison between biodynamic and organic fertilization. However, the
study shows that after the application of all the biodynamic measures, including the
spray preparations, soil respiration and the carbon mineralization measured after
10 days increased, and the fatty acid profile in the soil had partially changed (ibid.).
Zaller & Köpke (2004) found in a nine-year trial comparing compost with and without
biodynamic preparations or only with the yarrow preparation that just 100 days after
fertilization, an accelerated rate of decomposition of the cotton strips had taken place in
the variants with preparations.
They also showed a lower basal respiration in the topsoil layer and higher in the
subsoil, as well as a greater earthworm biomass. Likewise, a trend toward greater humus
stability in biodynamic cultivation has been observed, which is indicated by
a lower basal respiration and, respectively, a lower metabolic quotient (ibid., or Birkhofer et
al., 2008).
The lower transformation of fungal carbon into bacterial carbon (Joergensen et al., 2010 but
also Sradnick et al., 2013) and the higher proportions of C in the intermediate or passive
carbon depot in the biodynamic variants point in the same direction (Heitkamp et al., 2011).
This finding of stability coincides with the results of Berner et al. (2012), who
found in some years an increase in the microbial C/N ratio in the variant with preparations
of a long-term reduced soil tillage trial, which suggests a higher proportion of
fungi or of older cells in the soil biomass.
This can be explained by the effects of the biodynamic method on the edaphic environment: in the DOC trial,
the highest pH values of all the variants and the highest proportions of microbiologically bound C and N
were found in the biodynamic variant (Fließbach et al., 2007). The
soil amoebas allow a clear differentiation of the three cultivation methods in the DOC
experiment, in which they have been shown to be five times more abundant in the biodynamic variant
(Heger et al., 2012). Likewise, the variants fertilized with manure with biodynamic preparations
always show higher levels of hydrolyzable proteins or amino acids in the soil than those
with manure without preparations (Scheller & Raupp, 2005).
This indicates a modification of the manure decomposition processes, and therefore of the soil, in
biodynamic agriculture. Ultimately, research to understand soil processes as a whole has
barely begun, which also affects the understanding and choice of
parameters to measure the effects of biodynamics.
For example, the reference to the regulation processes of soil life by auxins, which
could have a link with the material and microbial composition of the biodynamic horn
manure preparation (Giannatasio et al., 2013), or even, the description of bacterial species and

Plant production and plant nutrition
According to Steiner (1925), fertilization is more than a simple restitution of nutrients; it serves to activate
the interaction between the soil and the plant, which he considers as a whole. Scheller (2013) describes and
justifies in a synthesis work the possibilities plants have to actively mobilize soil
nutrients based on his own investigations.
Because of the widespread skepticism among biodynamic experts about the use of biogas
fermentation substrates in fertilization, an experiment was carried out over four years on a
farm with liquid manure. In addition to the soil parameters, food quality was also
studied. No significant differences were found. The authors saw a possible cause of these
small differences in the relatively short duration of the experiment (Elers & Schmidt, 2011).
The germination capacity of broad-leaved dock (Rumex obtusifolius) was significantly lower after
one month in manure treated with the biodynamic compost preparations. Its germination rate
was 18% compared to the control variant without preparations, which had a germination rate
of 28% (Zaller, 2007).
Following Steiner's suggestion (1925) that the Moon influences plant growth,
cultivation experiments were carried out with radishes (Raphanus sativus var. sativus) at full moon and new
moon (Fritz, 2013). During sowing at full moon, night illumination provided significantly
higher yields than the plants that had no night illumination. At the new moon, there
was no difference in yield between the plants that had been covered with transparent or
opaque glass during the night. Leafing studies in Araliaceae of the species Fatshedera lizei have
shown partially a significantly greater leaf formation in correlation with the full
moon than with the new moon (Fritz, 2013; Fritz and Sikora, 2007). The experiments on plant cultivation
taking lunar rhythms into account are described in detail in Spieß's doctoral thesis (1994).
Among other things, a significant increase in carrot production was observed over three
years when sowing two days before the full moon. Zürcher et al. (2014, 1998) observed that a
weak daily pulse occurs in small tree trunks, correlating with the synodic rhythm of the
Moon and gravimetric tides. When felling timber, systematic changes were observed in water
loss, contraction, and relative density before and immediately after the full moon (Zürcher
et al., 2010). These first results from trials with precise measurements show that the moon can
influence the properties of wood. The question of whether this is of practical importance will have
to be the subject of broader investigations.

The biodynamic preparations
The application of horn manure and horn silica increased the yield of cumin
seeds (Cuminum cyminum L.) by more than 12% in two fertilization variants (Sharma et al. 2012). In the case
of Bacchus et al. (2010), the yield differences in three fertilization variants were not
significant when applying the compost preparations and the sprayed preparations to lettuce (Lactuca
sativa L.). The yield of two soybean varieties in Vietnam increased significantly by more than
30% compared to the control variant without preparation treatment (Tung and Fernandes
2007). The two variants were not fertilized. As with the unfertilized varieties, the use of the
biodynamic preparations on two rice varieties led to a significant increase in
yields of 10% and 15% respectively (Valez and Fernandes 2008). Also the root
length, root weight, shoot weight, and available phosphorus after the harvest
(+20%) increased significantly with the use of the preparations compared to the untreated
control (ibid.). In the case of hot pepper, the application of the biodynamic preparations had no
significant influence on plant development (Jayasree & George, 2006).
Compared to purely mineral fertilization, the ascorbic acid content of arugula
(Eruca sativa) increased significantly with the applications of horn silica, a strong

effect of the biodynamic preparations has been determined (the significant effects that did occur were
interpreted as random), no demonstrable effect of the biodynamic
preparations is available. It is incomprehensible that Chalker-Scott (2013) compared the variant "with
organic fertilization" with the variant "without organic fertilization and with biodynamic preparations"
resulting from the works of Tung & Fernandes (2007) and Valez & Fernandes (2008) to test the
effect of the biodynamic preparations. In the comparison, Chalker-Scott (2013) concludes that the
biodynamic preparations have a negative effect on the yield of soybeans and rice. In the
works of Tung & Fernandes (2007) and Valez & Fernandes (2008) there are also two variants in
which only the application of biodynamic preparations varied. When comparing the two variants without
organic fertilization, the application of biodynamic preparations led to a significant increase in the
yield of more than 30% for two soybean varieties (Tung & Fernandes 2007) and a significant
increase in yield of 10 and 15% for two rice varieties (Valez & Fernandes 2008)
(see above). In the present literature search on biodynamic preparations during the period
2006-2015, all the articles found have been presented. Not all the studies have shown
significant reactions of plants and soil after the application of the preparations. Why
only a part of the studies has shown significant plant reactions after the
application of biodynamic preparations must be investigated in subsequent studies. The development of
simple tests with relatively rapid plant reactions to the application of biodynamic
preparations can provide important information in this regard. In addition, a
meta-study on the experiences with biodynamic preparations will have to be carried out. Account will have to be taken of the fact
that the biodynamic preparations have shown a balancing effect on one-sided growing
conditions in numerous experiences (see below and Fritz 2009).

Enology and viticulture
The field of research in biodynamic enology and viticulture has gained importance in recent
years. A long-term fertilization experiment at the University of Geisenheim compared integrated,
organic, and biodynamic cultivation. In biodynamic cultivation, the length of the shoots, the weight of the
pruning wood, the inner leaves, the compactness of the grape cluster, the weight of a single grape, the attacks
of botrytis and acetic acid were significantly lower than in conventional cultivation. The
number of earthworms and the proportion of grapes exposed to the sun were significantly
higher in biodynamic cultivation than in conventional cultivation. Organic cultivation lies in all
parameters between the two variants. The yield was 21% lower in biodynamic agriculture

Tassoni et al. (2013) and Plahuta & Raspor (2007) did not manage to differentiate the wines of the

et al. (2015) as well as Bigler et al. (2009) were not successful. In an ecological balance study in
Spain, biodynamic viticulture showed a lower environmental impact than conventional viticulture
(Villanueva-Rey et al., 2014). In the United States, research has been carried out on what motivates winegrowers to
switch to biodynamic agriculture and on how consumers perceive organic seals
(McCullough et al., 2012). Another case study shows how a Swiss biodynamic vineyard contributes
to climate protection thanks to grape varieties resistant to fungi (Strasser & Coray,
2009).

Animal husbandry
On a Demeter dairy farm, a study was carried out on the effect of a combination of
homeopathic and antibiotic treatments on mastitis. This treatment concept made it possible
to reduce antibiotic use by an average of 75% (Fidelak et al., 2007). Biodynamic dairy farms
in Germany and Switzerland were asked about their strategies regarding mineral supplements. Most
said it did not correspond to the idea of biodynamics. The result also shows that
one third of the respondents do without mineral supplements, while on the other
farms studied, mineral supplements are often used to compensate for deficiencies
(Ivemeyer & Walkenhorst, 2011). Pötzsch et al. (2015) provide an overview of biodynamic pig
husbandry in Germany with the question of species-appropriate breeding. Pigs are raised
in relatively small numbers on a relatively small number of Demeter farms. The
species-appropriate husbandry concept was not described uniformly by the interviewees.

Food quality
Achieving high food quality is one of the main objectives of biodynamic agriculture. A
look at the number of scientific publications since 2008 shows that interest in research
on this topic is high, compared to other research topics.

Geier & Meischner (2011) summarized the state of research on the quality of biodynamic
foods. The authors consider more than 20 studies over a period of 33 years, 18 of
which date from before 2008.
For fruits, wine, vegetables, and milk, several studies have demonstrated the advantages of
biodynamics over organic production. The differences relate to certain components, as well
as to morphogenetic tests.
There are publications on wheat, chicory, lettuce, beetroot, cabbage, lemon, mango, strawberry, milk, and wine
from 2008 to 2015: the parameters studied range from the nitrate content enrichment capacity
to a wide variety of analyses of composition and antioxidant potential. Three
studies are dedicated to health effects, namely D'Evoli et al. (2010) on the effects of
strawberry extracts on human cells, Simões-Wüst et al. (2011) on the fat quality of breast
milk, and Kusche (2015) on the tolerance of raw biodynamic milk in children
suffering from food allergies.
In three cases, biodynamic production was compared with conventional production (Rangel et al.,
2011; D'Evoli et al., 2010; Bavec et al., 2012). In the other studies, comparisons were made with
organic agriculture or other variants.
Rangel et al. (2011) found no quality differences in lime, and Langenkämper et al. (2006) in
wheat. In all the other studies (Bavec, 2010; Maciel et al., 2012; Heimler, 2011; Lucarini et al.,
2012; D'Evoli et al., 2010; Simões-Wüst et al., 2011), differences were found with biodynamic
cultivation for one or more parameters.
Heimler et al. (2009) studied the influence of conventional and biodynamic cultivation on wild
chicory (Cichorium intybus) by measuring the polyphenol content and antioxidant potential. A few years
later, the same methods were used to compare conventional, organic, and
biodynamic cultivation of red batavia lettuce (Heimler, 2011). Both studies demonstrated that the highest
antioxidant potential is found in biodynamic cultivation. On the contrary, the polyphenol
contents were not different for chicory. In the case of lettuce, on the other hand, organic
and biodynamic agriculture showed the highest levels.
Lucarini et al. (2012) studied the nitrate enrichment capacity of various lettuce
varieties cultivated organically and biodynamically. The biodynamically cultivated plants
accumulated 1.3 to 2 times less nitrate than the plants cultivated in organic agriculture.
Bavec et al. (2010, 2012) examined white cabbages and red beetroots from conventional,
integrated, organic, and biodynamic agriculture. Biodynamic agriculture achieved the highest
carbon content in the white cabbage. The biodynamic cultivation of beetroot turned out to have the highest
phenol content, the highest antioxidant potential, and the highest sugar content.
The studies by Kusche et al. (2015, 2011a, 2011b, 2010) showed the highest proportions of
fatty acids of high nutritional value in the milk of biodynamic farms with low input
levels, and a consistent differentiation of milk from biodynamic production with low and high
input levels compared to conventionally produced milk from modern
high-input dairy farms. In addition, a coded series of provocation tests shows
a better tolerance of raw biodynamic milk compared to pasteurized and
homogenized milk from conventional production in children with food intolerances (Kusche 2015).
Simões-Wüst et al. (2011) found that a high consumption of dairy products from biodynamic
agriculture in pregnant women and nursing mothers results in a better fat quality in
breast milk compared to the consumption of dairy products from organic
and conventional agriculture. Newborns of mothers who consumed mainly dairy
products produced biodynamically also had a lower risk of developing eczema (Thijs et
al. 2011).

Development of new methods
The development of a series of research methods is the result of an expanded knowledge of
nature that is the basis of biodynamics. Some methods have been the subject of intensive
scientific research; others have not yet been validated.
The best-known image-formation process is copper chloride crystallization or "sensitive
crystallization". The method is used to characterize foods. In recent years, sensitive
crystallization has been established and validated as a scientific method (Doesburg et al., 2015; Kahl et al., 2015;
Fritz et al., 2011; Huber et al., 2010). The ongoing work is related, among other things, to the
influence of the experimenters' training on the visual evaluation of the results (Fritz,
2016).
The capillary dynamolysis method, similar to chromatography, has not yet been treated scientifically with
the same intensity as sensitive crystallization. Capillary dynamolysis was developed by Lili Kolisko
in the 1920s and then perfected by others (see Engquist 1977). A doctoral thesis was
carried out on the validation of image creation (Zalecka et al., 2010). Several researchers apply
capillary dynamolysis and sensitive crystallization at the same time during food analysis (Fritz et
al., 2011).
The circular filter paper morphochromatography method was developed by Pfeiffer (1950) as early as the
1950s. It is mainly used to evaluate soils and composts (Voitl & Guggenberger, 1986).
In her doctoral thesis, Haßold-Piezunka (2003) was able to compare the analytical parameters of compost
analysis with the characteristics of the morphochromatographic images. She concludes that the
morphochromatographic "Chroma-Boden-Test" analysis can provide a rapid and complete assessment of the
composting process if it is carried out with care.
Likewise, the origin of the psychological test of food effects developed by Geier et
al. (2012, 2016) comes from the expanded understanding of quality in biodynamics. The application of the
psychological test examines the effects of nutrition on the mental and physical state of the people
evaluated. In this way, factors linked to the method of cultivation and
processing of foods can be verified.

Sustainability
While the impact of biodynamic agriculture on the soil has been the subject of scientific research
for many years, there are few studies that take sustainability into account.
Turinek et al. (2010) and Bavec et al. (2012) investigated the ecological footprint of biodynamic,
organic, integrated, and conventional cereal crops using data from a field trial in
Slovenia. In a three-year test, the biodynamic and organic variants showed advantages in
terms of ecological behavior and ecological efficiency with respect to energy use and
climate relevance.
Pechrova et al. (2013) analyze the efficiency of resource use on organic and biodynamic farms
in the Czech Republic. Higher efficiency was found on organic farms.
Garcia-Yzaguirre et al. (2011) carried out an analysis of organic and biodynamic rice cultivation in
Spain. The cultivation of two varieties under the two cultivation conditions showed no significant
yield differences.
In her final thesis, Steiner (2009) analyzed the connection models between agricultural areas on
integrated, organic, and biodynamic farms in Switzerland, all referring to biodiversity. She found the
following differentiation: segregation on the integrated farms, networks on the organic farms, and
integration on the Demeter farms.

Social Sciences
In recent years, the social conditions and implications of the practice of
biodynamics have also been studied. These include both the motivations of producers in conversion in the Czech

Republic (Pechrovà, 2014) as well as historical studies on the expansion of biodynamics in
English-speaking countries (Paull, 2001 ac, Paull, 2014) or representations of the relations between C.G.
Jung's archetypes and biodynamics, according to the example of the walled garden (Damery, 2011). In
complementary works, the theme was the frequent link between biodynamic agriculture and
social responsibilities (for example, Kalisch & van Elsen, 2011), a link that is not limited to this
method of agriculture.

The innovative character of biodynamic agriculture
We can therefore say that biodynamic agriculture is always in development. The
research attitude of the practitioners is part of this and actively fosters innovations, in
cooperation with researchers. Sometimes this research is organized individually
(farmer research). Applied to the optimization of the whole system and its processes as much as
to new approaches. This disposition toward research and development is also expressed in
biodynamic consulting, which differs from other consulting services by its proximity to
research and experimentation (Luley et al., 2015). The following are examples of
innovations resulting solely from practical initiatives.


---


Plant breeding
Barely 20 years ago, the agricultural authorities still held the opinion that conventional varieties
adapted sufficiently well to the needs of organic agriculture; there was no need to
breed organic cereals (according to Jantsch & Schüler, 1995).
This opinion is now considered obsolete, mainly due to the breeding activities carried
out by biodynamic vegetable producers and a handful of biodynamic cereal
producers. At present, some twenty cereal varieties have been
biodynamically bred and registered, specially selected for organic agriculture. The twenty or so biodynamic vegetable
breeders have registered more than 70 vegetable varieties with the Bundessortenamt
(Federal Office for the Registration of Plant Species) through the association Verein Kultursaat eV
(Fleck, 2015). Beckmann (2013) offers an overview of the history of the Demeter movement and
the aspects of biodynamic plant breeding.
There are also breeding initiatives in livestock husbandry, inspired by biodynamics, described in
relation to the concept of family breeding of cows, with their own bulls wherever possible
(Metz et al., 2012). Another practical innovation is the rearing of calves with the mother or a nurse cow, which
dispenses with the immediate separation of cow and calf after birth. Ehrlich (2003)
compiled data on the method of rearing calves with the mother. Four of the eight farms that
developed this system were Demeter farms. Currently, more than 30 Demeter farms and some
farms from other associations allow this intensified contact between cow and calf (Demeter,
2015a). However, the framework conditions still need to be optimized, since the increase in animal
welfare is to the detriment of yield (Barth et al., 2009, Spengler Neff & Ivemeyer, 2012).

Bee-respectful beekeeping
An example of biodynamic standards in the field of livestock husbandry is the effort of beekeepers
to practice beekeeping with respect for the being and welfare of the animals. Demeter certifies
beekeeping with respect for the being and welfare of the animals.
Although this form developed by beekeepers has been included in the Demeter standards notebook
for 20 years, there is little research on it, apart from a comparative work with respect to
colony formation in conventional beekeeping (Friedmann, 2002).

Biodynamic viticulture/silviculture
Other areas in which biodynamic farms or biodynamic actors play an innovative
role in the practice of organic agriculture are, for example, biodynamic silviculture, where, in addition to

11
the sustainable conversion of forests, there is debate about the timing of tree felling (Zürcher,
2003).

Rethinking the economy and social agriculture
Socioeconomic themes have long been a concern of biodynamic practitioners,
both in terms of business management and in the political-economic aspect (Bartsch, 1934). In
fact, the foundational phase of biodynamic agriculture coincided with a period with two extreme
economic crises, the hyperinflation in Germany in 1923/24 and the world economic crisis in 1929
(Koepf & von Plato 2001). In particular, two directions should be mentioned: the development of legal,
economic, and property forms appropriate for a holistic and multifunctional agriculture that
partially integrates social services; to this end, agriculture is often managed
collectively and organized in agricultural communities, which guides the pioneers of today's so-called
solidarity agriculture (CSA and AMAP) (Kraiß & van Elsen, 2008). As for operational
management, we refer here to Hiß (2015), who proposes adding ecological and social elements to
the accounting plans and balance sheets of companies. In addition, the frequent combination of
(curative) pedagogy and biodynamic agriculture as a concept can be found in the Camphill movement, but
also beyond it (Kalisch & van Elsen, 2011; Jaenichen & van Elsen 2011; Hartkemeyer et al. 2014).

3. Challenges and perspectives
The development of biodynamic agriculture and organic agriculture in general is due not only to
traditional research, but above all to the innovative spirit of a large number of practitioners.
Biodynamic research has had, and still has, on the one hand, the function of supporting the development
of the agricultural enterprise that manages to optimize the quality of the products. In addition, there is fundamental
research on agricultural aspects and on the aspects of the processing of the productions.
Since research is only one of the many aspects to be taken into account in the
development of biodynamic agriculture, the perspectives of research cannot be outlined here,
with the exception of the work objectives of the few existing active institutions close to the
Demeter Association. Biodynamic research is carried out worldwide within a
wide variety of contexts, including non-academic contexts, led by very different actors,
with few networks and without a common work plan. Rather, research on biodynamic
questions is guided by the individual interest of the scientist, which is confirmed by the widely
diversified sources of the present work.
The main areas of research in the biodynamic organizations in Germany are the soil and
fertilization, as well as livestock husbandry, always in relation to food quality. On the one
hand, science poses questions deepening original biodynamic themes such as the
quality of the biodynamic preparations, rapid tests for the preparations, and on the other hand, questions
about the quality of living humus, the effect of biodynamic practices on food
quality, and appropriate composting, which can be summarized with the keyword: optimization.
For a decade or two now, the difference between biodynamic and "organic" has been becoming relevant
in research and practice. For example, the studies carried out in this context show
differences in the composition of biodynamic foods, for example, milk, and their effects on
human nutrition (see above, Simões-Wüst et al. 2011 and Kusche 2015). This shows the potential of
biodynamic agriculture for a diet that favors human health.
Fuchs (2010) refers to the peculiarities, limitations, and possibilities of focusing on the tasks and
methods of biodynamic research. Instead of obliging itself to always test the method, Fuchs
suggests focusing more on best practices. Methodologically, he proposes focusing on both
empirical sciences and farmer research, that is, the working methods of
qualitative social research, in order to further develop this type of agriculture close to practice.
To date, no theory of biodynamics is available, which is also linked to the fact that
such a theory should be associated with scientific disciplines beyond agricultural science, since its
origins go beyond a pure and methodologically limited agricultural science. However, it is

tempting to consider explanatory theoretical approaches. If one wants to explore the contribution of
biodynamics in the thematic fields of organic agriculture, the human being, nature, and its
perspectives, it is interesting to know what models and systems of thought are at the origin of
biodynamic practice and research. In summary, the goal is to think about agriculture in a
holistic way, both on an individual farm and in a social context. The biodynamic movement has
always been based on agricultural culture, that is, beyond agricultural production, the social and
economic context is an integral part of its achievements. Cultivating biodynamically is understood in the best
sense as a multifunctional practice.
The background of this broader vision of the foundations of agriculture is what Rudolf Steiner
calls the approach of the "science of the spirit". Unlike Kant, Steiner is based on the non-separation
of the knowing subject and the world in epistemological terms (Grauer 2007), which is then reflected in
the availability of concepts that describe the world. According to Grauer, epistemologically it is a
constructivist-monist interpretation 7 rather than a dualist interpretation of the relation between the human
being and the world. For Grauer (2007), Steiner proceeds from the principle that "knowledge through
experience and the distinctions or observations that constitute the subject [...] can become
directly accessible as experience."
Today we can, based on the recent scientific theory of the philosopher Poser (2012), classify
agricultural sciences in a third category of sciences between the human sciences and the natural
sciences: the technical sciences as applied natural sciences, which derive their results not so much
from the theoretical superstructure but from the question: does this work?
Contrary to the mainstream of agricultural sciences, biodynamic research does not only
want to develop this knowledge of disposition, but also an approach of knowledge of
orientation (both terms according to Mittelstrass, 1989). Knowledge of dispositions, which
studies how or whether something works, has as its consequence the control of nature; knowledge
of orientation leads to knowing nature. Knowledge of orientation can include
knowledge of disposition, but not the other way around. It complements the technical point of view with a
social and philosophical point of view and thus develops regulatory insights that help to identify
purposes and objectives. The concrete observation of the philosopher Böhme (2014): "Biodynamic
agriculture is a practice, not a theory," links the legitimacy and necessity of alternative forms of
natural sciences, beyond the natural science dominated by experimentation that is only valid
under certain conditions. Goethe's phenomenology 8 of nature, bodily phenomenology 9
as an inner experience of nature, as well as the theory of environments 10 as a framework that
shapes nature, are cited here as examples that can be a starting point for broadening the
bases of knowledge.
Leiber (2009) describes the biodynamic approach as characterized by four paradigms:
ontologically, it assumes that, according to Goethe, living ideas give shape to the world;
epistemologically, the human being can recognize this "spiritual" background, and methodologically
anthroposophy describes a corresponding path of knowledge. Added to this is the premise of the
co-evolution of the human being and nature. In 2014, Fuchs tried to make this concrete with respect to
agricultural measures and to link it in advance with new approaches of natural philosophy: keywords
anthropocene and responsibility. Poser (2012), who also takes evolution as a paradigmatic and
superordinate interpretive scheme for scientific work, sees the human being at the same time as a
member and regulator. For him, this leads to a complex theoretical approach that goes beyond
pure (recognized) causality and takes the unforeseeable into account. In relation to life on earth, this

Bechmann (2009) advocates the development of a post-materialist natural science and at the same time

paradigms, which are generally based solely on methodology, to be adequate — among

fruitful research, he proposes an approach to complexity theory that presupposes the
following principle: "Nature as a whole is creative on the path of emergence toward

agriculture and food, are part of biodynamic research. To achieve forms of
recognition relevant to practice, methodological work must first be carried out.
Based on an examination of appropriate research methods, Baars (2002) has developed
a concept of empirical science that differs from the usual scientific mode. This is linked in practice
to the moment of decision-making in a field of activity, for example agricultural management: the
core is the practiced holistic recognition that manifests itself in the identification of patterns (and
of discrepancies with respect to them) in a living context, for example that of a field. As in
a master class for musicians, Baars sees similar skills in the farmer: he can become
an expert practitioner who can successfully combine his knowledge and his intuition. Therefore,
from a methodological point of view, approaches aimed at further developing agricultural
practices are more likely to be the result of examining the top quarter of the most efficient farms in
a survey, and not the average. This faculty of awareness and intuition can be exercised
consciously — there are approaches in biodynamic training — and it is also used in qualitative
research (see, for example, the section on enology and viticulture). Schieren (1998) finds in
Goethe the source of this self-education of the researcher: "The idea Goethe has of knowledge
demands a new concept of science that understands it not as a knowledge accessible and available
in a library, but as a process of development of the scientific individual himself." Once
the exhaustive process of self-education has been carried out, the human being can thus understand and exercise his
role in the scientific process, seeing himself as a kind of instrument.
The science that methodologically rests on this basis, that is oriented toward phenomenology and
integrates the influence of the researcher, has consolidated in a particular form called
"goetheanism." The latter, in turn, originates as a method from Rudolf Steiner's
reflections on the natural sciences (Steiner, 1886). Consequently, Steiner (1920) proposes a path of
formation of the human being. This shows the possibility of the human being being the instrument of
scientific knowledge, a concept for which Böhme (2003) recently created a starting point
with his definition: "The body is the nature that we ourselves are." As for plant
breeding, Timmermann (2007) describes the method of "phenomenology of nature" as
appropriate (complementary) when it comes to qualitative aspects.
In summary, biodynamic research is structured in a very heterogeneous way. It cooperates in a
participatory way with practice, and works methodically on the theme of the qualitative optimization of
the methods of organic agriculture. It is therefore important for an organic agriculture that is
oriented both toward values and toward sustainable and qualitative production.
