PREFACE
Viktor Schauberger's initial assessment of the intimate
relationship between water and forest, and its dual influence on
water resource management, the health of the landscape in
given rainy areas, is undoubtedly correct. His
understanding of these complex and important questions
of human existence was rooted in his experiences
in the natural environment of temperate Austria. Here, the natural
processes of destruction are slow in reaction to human environmental
errors. In the tropics, where the landscape is more vulnerable, the rapid
consequences of human deforestation are more evident and
far-reaching. This often leads to the collapse of the drainage
system, severe erosion, the destruction of productive
soils, the disappearance of vegetation and fauna,
aridity and even negative repercussions at sea.
   Schauberger's theories about Europe are, in the tropics, verified
in a convincing and shocking way. In high-temperature
regions, in the long term, we will suffer similar damage to our
renewable natural resources if harmful ecological
measures are permitted in the productive environment.
   Man has a propensity to plan and execute
the exploitation of natural resources with a view to immediate returns or
in the shortest possible time, without taking account of long-term
prospects or ecological relationships. Modern forestry
illustrates this. To survive, we must think and plan
within an ecosystem dimension, which requires us to

INTRODUCTION
Many people have suggested I write a book about Viktor
Schauberger, the extraordinary natural scientist, inventor and
philosopher. As early as the 1920s he warned us, in speeches and
articles, about the environmental crisis in which we are now trapped and from
which we seem to have little hope of escaping. In his lifetime
he met mainly resistance and contempt, but now
interest in his life and work is growing in many
parts of the world. People are struck by this powerful figure
who had such a tragic fate and by the bold theories
with which he sought to transform the world.
    Viktor Schauberger was not an educated man in the
conventional scientific sense. However, he had seen the depths of
the workings of Nature, and his theories are based on his
personal understanding of life and the functions of Nature.
He was, of course, an outsider, an individualist; but history
teaches us that, even within the natural sciences, such people
have often produced epoch-making discoveries, while in their lifetime
they were considered ignorant laymen by the learned world. Rarely
obtaining recognition, later generations often had
reason to bless their work. It is possible that one
day Viktor Schauberger will be included in this category of
scientists.
    So far there has been no literature in English on Viktor
Schauberger, except for a few articles in the important but
little-known journal The Men of the Trees. This book is a
modest attempt to present material on his life and
work that I have collected since 1956, when I first
learned about it. I did not know him personally,
but a long friendship with his son, Walter Schauberger, and with
many of Viktor Schauberger's old friends and colleagues, yes.

importance. Viktor Schauberger's central theme was: 'Prevailing

gases into the type of movement that nature uses only to break
down and dissolve matter. Nature uses another form of movement
to rebuild. When our technology uses only

A son of water and forests
Viktor Schauberger came from the former Bavarian aristocracy that
had lost its privileges and the family seat, Schauburg,
around 1230 after a feud with the powerful prelate, the Bishop
of Passau. Around 1650 a certain Stephan Schauberger moved
to Austria and settled near Lake Plockenstein at the foot
of the Dreissesselberg. He started a branch of the family whose
members were interested almost exclusively in the creation of
forests and their wildlife. Over time, their motto
became 'Fidus in silvis silentibus' (Faithful to the silent forests),
and the family coat of arms showed a tree trunk garlanded
with wild roses.
  One of Stephan's descendants became the last head of the hunt
at Bad Ischl in the time of Franz Joseph. At the end of the 19th
century, one of his brothers was a master woodsman at
Holzschlag, near Lake Plockenstein. He had nine children, the fifth
of whom was Viktor, born on 30 June 1885.
   Viktor was a true "son of the forest," both by heritage
and by environment. There was never any doubt that he would
follow in his father's footsteps. He once wrote: "From my
earliest childhood, my greatest ambition was to become a
ranger like my father, my grandfather, my great-grandfather and your father
before him."
   From the beginning, the boy showed a great interest in
everything related to nature. He could wander all day
alone in the nearly virgin forest (compared to today) around Lake
Plockenstein, studying animal and plant life,
or following the countless wild streams in the mountains.
He soon learned much not found in books about forest life
and about the water of his father and elder relatives. Of
them he says: 'They trusted what they saw with their own eyes and
what they felt intuitively. Above all, they recognized
the internal healing power of water and understood that water,
directed through irrigation channels at night, can produce a significantly
larger crop than that of the nearby meadows and
fields.34 His main interest, however, was the care of
the forest and wildlife.
   His mother was also close to nature, and he recounted
how she would always tell him: 'If sometimes life is really hard and
you don't know who to turn to, go to a stream and listen to its music.
Then everything will be fine again.'
   Viktor's father wanted his son to have an academic
training as an arborist, but that path interested him little.
He soon interrupted his studies and began practical forestry
school, from which he duly graduated with the state
ranger examination.

The first discoveries
His apprenticeship began with an older ranger and he
described lyrically how happy he was when his dream
was beginning to become reality. After the end of the First World
War he was given his own district and, although remote,
it had many advantages. He was employed by Prince Adolf Schaumburg-
Lippe, who gave him responsibility for more than
21,000 hectares of nearly pristine forest at Bernerau at
Steyerling.
   And so began Schauberger's true period of apprenticeship.
In this vast wilderness, almost untouched by human hand because
of its remoteness, he had the opportunity to study how
nature works when left alone. There were many beautiful
species of trees in this area that have since disappeared, a
wealth of wildlife and in the many streams an
abundance of salmon, trout and other fish.
   What Schauberger was able to study in this wilderness was
often in stark contrast to what was taught in academic
forestry studies and complemented the more traditional
knowledge he had absorbed at home.
   Water was his consuming interest. He began to discover
its laws and characteristics and the connection between its
temperature and its movement. He noticed how water that

He was also very interested in the behavior of trout and
salmon in mountain streams. The large mountain trout
could remain motionless for a certain period of time in
the strongest current. They made a strange movement with fin
and tail, but otherwise seemed anchored in the rushing flow.
If alarmed, however, they shot at the speed of light upstream,
rather than drifting downstream, which would have
seemed more natural.
   Schauberger found no explanation for the
behavior of trout in the existing literature. However,
he knew that a mountain stream was coldest near its
source and became warmer farther from the source.
Could there be some connection with the trout's struggle to
escape the current? He performed several experiments to
investigate this. As an observation point he chose a stretch
of strong rapids along a stream where he liked to watch a
large trout resting. He had his lumbermen heat
a hundred litres of water and discharge it 500 metres
upstream at a given signal. The stream was large, with a flow
of several cubic metres of water per second. The mere 100 litres of
heated water did not visibly warm the stream. Nevertheless,
immediately after adding the hot water, the trout — which
until that moment had remained motionless — became greatly agitated.
It bent its tail and only with considerable effort managed to maintain
its position with vigorous fin movements. Soon
its efforts were in vain, and it was pushed downstream,
out of sight, only much later to return to its previous position. This
convinced Schauberger that his theory was correct and that there
was indeed a connection between
water temperature and trout behavior.

Schauberger, in another connection, suggests that a natural
watercourse that allows natural movement accumulates
energy that flows in the opposite direction of the water. It is this
energy that is used by the trout. In a properly
formed waterfall, this flow of energy can be
distinguished as a channel of light within the running water. The

    It was not only the trout, however, that he saw moving in

formed within a rushing stream. The water of the pool
was several metres deep, but so clear that he could
easily see the bottom. Here lay stones, some the
size of a man's head. As he studied them, he was surprised
to see that some of the stones moved back and forth,
colliding with each other as if drawn together, only

I no longer trusted my usually vigilant eyes, when

Logs that were technological mysteries
Prince Adolf von Schaumburg-Lippe had problems.
War and inflation, postwar crises and, not least, a
young wife who was financially demanding, forced
the elderly prince to investigate every possibility of sustaining
his dwindling treasury. He had already fully exploited his other
forest estates, and now only the Schauberger district remained,
where the prince was wont to wander, complaining
of his misfortune. For here there were vast stretches of mature timber,
so poorly positioned for transport that the costs of moving it
would have consumed all the profits.
   In the end, Prince Adolf announced a competition for the
best solution to this problem, which would unlock his
frozen assets in the Bernerau region.
   Suggestions came from forestry engineers, hydrologists, and
other experts, but none of them caught the prince's attention.
There was one suggestion he never saw: the competition committee
examined it in the preliminary round. A young ranger had the
courage to compete with the experts and, worse still, had
an idea that was pure fantasy: a bad joke. They
returned it with a harsh reprimand for not taking the
competition seriously — and this presumably put an end to the
matter.
   But fate decided otherwise. After an unsuccessful competition,
Prince Adolf was still chasing substantial
sums of money, especially with the approaching annual visit
of the young princess to Monte Carlo. The princess herself
went to the Schauberger district to hunt deer,
accompanied by a young forest warden. During the
hunt, she confided to Schauberger that the prince would soon have to
leave his estate, as he was bankrupt. The conversation
revolved around Schauberger's entry in the competition,
which had so irritated the committee. He presented the plan to the
princess, who asked him how much could be saved on

Deforestation destroys watercourses
It was not only the log flumes that captured Schauberger's
imagination. His area of interest was broad, but water remained
his primary cause. It was behind everything concerning forestry,
agriculture, or energy management. He was increasingly
convinced that economic, social and political stability in Europe
— and in the world — would depend on a new attitude toward
water, forests, and soil. Scientists had to be made to understand
that water was not something to be handled carelessly, as an
inanimate object. Water was not just H2O, but a living organism
with its own laws that demanded humanity's respect, if the
consequences were not to be fatal. Because of his many
opportunities to study Nature's relationships in a relatively
pristine environment, he was able — perhaps better than many
others — to observe the dangerous changes that occur when man
disturbs natural harmony. It frightened him to see what happened
to springs and watercourses, to animal and plant life, when areas
were deforested.
   This clearing of forest areas spread like wildfire in Austria
after the First World War. The country's economy was suffering
and forests were the easiest source of income. No one thought
about deforestation or otherwise limiting the damage that
occurred once the trees were felled.
   Precipitation.
   The changes after deforestation were initially evident along
the watercourses. Schauberger had already studied springs and
streams in detail and had seen how they never ran dry,
how the stream channels were covered with moss that was not
pulled up even when the stream was full. He also noticed how
water weeds could point upstream, a phenomenon he understood
to be associated with the energy of the water. The more strongly
these 'tails' of the water weeds point upstream, the better the
temperature and flow characteristics of the watercourse.
These watercourses never destroyed their beds or overflowed,
even during heavy rains.
   Everything changed when the forest was cut down. The
streams reacted first. They became "wild." The weeds and riverbed
vegetation were uprooted and carried away. The water could no
longer keep its channel "clean" but deposited gravel and mud that
filled the watercourse and caused it to overflow. The water then
attacked its own channels, eroding its banks and endangering
surrounding areas, especially after heavy rains or thaws. Then the
springs began to dry up. The water level dropped over large areas
around a deforested region. Finally the
stream disappeared completely38, except after a sudden torrential
downpour, when it could turn into a violent torrent, threatening
buildings and their inhabitants. Little by little, the entire
interior around this bare area dried up.
   There was great concern about the destruction of
watercourses. This gave rise to water management technology
that was applied to streams and lakes in Austria and parts of
southern Europe. Watercourses were surrounded by stone and
concrete embankments to prevent water from flowing out of its
channels, with the resulting catastrophic effects. This is an
endless task; the walls and embankments must be constantly
maintained, as the water continually seeks to erode and undermine
them. The maintenance costs are enormous, bringing huge profits
to the stone and cement industries.
   In the late 1920s, Schauberger had already begun, in speeches
and articles, to attack the decimation of forests and existing water
control techniques. It is fair, perhaps, to point out that he
contributed to this deforestation through his log flumes. He had,
even if perhaps naively, tried to limit logging by reducing
transport costs and thereby increasing net income. During the
construction of the flumes, he constantly emphasized the need to
harvest only "the interest" on the forest capital,
and not clear it completely. But these warnings, of course, went
unheeded. The large timber companies that sprang up everywhere,
with the encouragement of the state, had only one goal: to turn
trees into money as quickly as possible. This also happened in
Sweden during the period of deforestation, when many unexplored
forests were targeted. The plowing techniques still used in
Swedish forestry are reportedly as negative in their results as the
original clearings.

never be regulated from its banks, but from within, from the
inside of the flowing content itself."

Iron and Copper Equipment in Agriculture
In the 1930s, Schauberger was invited by King Boris of Bulgaria to
examine the reasons for the great decline in agricultural production
in that country. During his trip through the countryside, he noticed
that in the areas populated by the Turks, the harvests were more abundant
than elsewhere. Here the old wooden plow was still in use. The
rest of the country had replaced them with modern iron plows imported
from Germany as part of a general modernization
of Bulgarian agriculture. The first steam plows were also introduced.
Schauberger arrived at the logical conclusion that the
reduction of the harvest was a consequence of the introduction
of iron plows, but only later did he develop his theory
of the harmful effect of iron machinery on agriculture. His
work with water jets gave him a new perspective on the
problem (see page 89). It was demonstrated that if a small amount of rust
was added to the water in these experiments, no charge developed; the water was 'empty.' He
extrapolated this finding to the use of iron plows and thought that
its effect on crops must be related to this. When
the iron plow moves through the soil, it heats up and the
disturbed soil is covered with a fine dust of iron
particles that rust rapidly. He had already noticed that
iron-rich soil was dry and that the turbines of the power plant
"discharged" water.
   The conclusion of all these observations was that iron
had a harmful effect on the water characteristics of the
soil; it expelled water and 'drained' its power. With
the introduction of the steam plow and, subsequently, the tractor
plow, the situation worsened with the increase in speed
at which the blades moved through the soil. Walter Schauberger
said that water disappears from fields plowed in this way for
simple physical reasons; the rapid passage of the iron plow
through the soil cuts the magnetic energy lines of the
field, causing an electric current in the same way that
a coil in an electric generator rotates in a magnetic
field. This, in turn, leads to electrolysis in the soil
which separates water into oxygen and hydrogen. Electrolysis
also compromises microscopic life in the soil and this causes
an even higher temperature to occur, in addition to the
friction of the iron plates with the soil. It is especially with iron that these
phenomena occur. With wooden, copper, and other so-called "biologically magnetic" plows, the magnetic
field of the soil is not disturbed.

The concept of the vortex was fundamental to Schauberger's work. Its application to fundamental physics is also emerging. The vortex is a fundamental principle that sheds new light on discoveries in physics. The vortex points to a completely new understanding of the physical world. At the same time, it opens the doors to the superphysical.

   For most of us, the physical world seems solid and substantial. However, modern physics has shown very clearly that this solidity is an illusion. Matter is composed of atoms and these atoms themselves are mostly empty spaces, containing subatomic particles such as protons and electrons in constant motion.

   These same subatomic particles are far from substantial. From Einstein we know that matter is equivalent to energy. Particles are somehow bundles of pure energy.

   But this equivalence of matter and energy is a mystery. No one understands how particles of matter, apparently so stable, can be a form of dynamic and ephemeral energy. Modern physics knows this and exploits this fact without understanding why.

   It is this central mystery of modern physics that the vortex can explain. The new idea is that a subatomic particle is a vortex of energy. This is a simple principle, but it has immense power. The vortex has the potential to provide a completely new basis for physics.

   The vortex begins to explain for the first time the properties of particles. Particles are a paradox in physics. Sometimes they behave like tiny point-like things; sometimes they behave like waves. If particles are indeed energy vortices, many of these paradoxes can be resolved. The complexity of physics disappears.

   Einstein described matter as "frozen energy." The vortex shows that the energy of matter, far from being frozen, is in constant motion. From this starting point, it is easy to explain the dynamic properties of particles.

   Matter acts dynamically on other parts of matter. It can even act "at a distance" — through apparently empty space. We all know the way two magnets repel — or attract — each other without touching. If particles are represented as inert "bubbles" of material, these effects are very difficult to explain. But if particles of matter are indeed extended vortices, it becomes very easy to see how they can interact with each other to create effects like electric charge and magnetism.

   The vortex, as it develops, shows that even apparently empty space is full of energy. It makes clear how this energy relates to matter and how "subtle" energies interact with the physical world.

   Some of the extraordinary effects produced by Schauberger can be explained in terms of a resonance effect between these subtle energies and the energy of matter. Vortices in air or water, moving in the same way as the underlying energy in matter, can exchange energy with them. This principle can be seen in the tuning fork. Strike a tuning fork in a room with a piano and all the piano strings tuned to that note will begin to vibrate in sympathy. The enormous energies released by the Schauberger vortex could be the result of a similar resonance effect. If so, he may have discovered the possibility of harnessing not only the energy contained in matter, but also the "cosmic" energies of space.

   These ideas are certainly speculative. But they indicate a possible explanation of mysterious and inexplicable phenomena. After Schauberger, others have built flying saucers and "energy machines" that work without fuel. But invariably they have little or no idea why they work and, lacking that insight, make progress only if supported by adequate theoretical foundations. It may be that the new physics of the vortex can one day provide the theoretical framework that allows Schauberger's vortex to be thoroughly explored.
