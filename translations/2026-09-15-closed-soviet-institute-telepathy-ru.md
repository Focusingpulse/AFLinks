# A Closed Soviet Institute Studied Telepathy for 20 Years: What They Found and Why the Documents Are Still Classified

Nikita Shevtsev — May 19, 2026, 18:30

In the Cold War era, a typical secret laboratory in the USSR looked like an ordinary research institute. It was hidden behind a neutral codename — a "post office box" [pochtovy yashchik, the Soviet term for a classified institution]. Inside unremarkable gray buildings, Soviet physicists and physiologists tried to measure and direct the power of human thought using precision instruments. We will tell you why the pragmatic Soviet system spent colossal budgets for two decades researching "mind reading." And most importantly — what real scientific results came out of these deeply classified investigations.

## How Materialists Came to Believe in Telepathy

The study of mental suggestion in the USSR began long before the Cold War started. As early as the 1920s–1930s, the scientists Vladimir Bekhterev and Alexander Chizhevsky laid the foundations for research into the influence of radiation on the human psyche. During the same period, Professor Leonid Vasilyev began his first experiments in mental suggestion at a distance.

The Stalin era was politically harsh, but there were almost no direct repressions against researchers of anomalous phenomena. Scientists continued their work, and the results of their labors saw the light of day at the turn of the 1950s and 1960s.

The catalyst for state funding was intelligence reports. Data reached Moscow indicating that the United States was actively researching the use of psychics for espionage — this is how the prototypes of the future Stargate project were taking shape. The USSR feared technological lag, and a kind of "psi arms race" began. The KGB and the Ministry of Defense began sponsoring closed laboratories to protect state secrets from remote intrusion. In private conversations, Leonid Vasilyev even compared the discovery of extrasensory perception to the mastery of atomic energy.

By the end of the 1960s, more than twenty special laboratories were operating in the country. Leningrad University studied the electromagnetic theory of suggestion. The A. S. Popov Institute in Moscow investigated the transfer of mental images. In Alma-Ata, researchers recorded the energetic glow of objects and developed the theory of "bioplasma." And at secret bases in Novosibirsk, academicians seriously attempted to receive mental signals over ultra-long distances.

## The Most Famous and Strangest Experiments

One of the largest-scale projects was the attempt to transmit thoughts over thousands of kilometers. The best-known of these was the telepathic bridge "Moscow — Novosibirsk." Similar experiments were conducted between Leningrad and Sevastopol. Scientists even tried to establish mental contact with submarines, where ordinary radio waves cannot penetrate. Researchers attempted to encode Morse code into emotional bursts.

In the "Moscow — Novosibirsk" experiment, biophysic Yuri Kamensky sat in an isolated chamber in the capital. At the appointed time, he would take a metal object in his hands — for example, a stiff spring — and mentally transmit the tactile sensations and the image to Karl Nikolaev, who was in Novosibirsk.

Alongside Karl Nikolaev, Soviet science also studied other people with unusual abilities in detail. Ninel Kulagina demonstrated telekinesis — moving small objects without touching them, right in front of academicians. Rosa Kuleshova possessed "dermo-optical perception" ("skin vision") and read printed text with her fingertips. These phenomena were captured on film and carefully analyzed by interdisciplinary teams.

To rule out trickery and external interference, the most modern equipment of the day was used. Experiments were conducted in Faraday cages — special chambers that block any electromagnetic fields. The Kirlian couple developed the method of high-frequency photography to record the characteristic glow around living objects. Professor Viktor Inyushin developed these observations into a theory of invisible "bioplasma," which could serve as the carrier of mental energy.

## What Did They Find?

Despite budgets in the millions, the official verdicts of Soviet scientists were restrained. Staff at Leonid Vasilyev's laboratory ultimately admitted they had never been able to conclusively prove the existence of telepathy as an objective physical reality. Western experts also noted serious methodological flaws in the Soviet experiments, pointing to weak protection against error.

Careful verification destroyed the magic. Ultra-long-distance telepathy turned out to be the result of subliminal sensory sensitivity and lucky coincidences. Ninel Kulagina's telekinesis was explained by subtle hypnosis of the observers, concealed devices, and electrostatic effects. "Skin vision" turned out to be tiny gaps under the blindfold plus sensitivity to temperature. The high-frequency aura was merely a gas discharge dependent on skin humidity.

But twenty years of work were not in vain. Scientists made important applied discoveries. Advanced systems for contactless health monitoring were created, and methods were developed for assessing the condition of pilots, cosmonauts, and submariners. Researchers deeply studied the brain's hidden reserves under stress, which substantially advanced neurophysiology and helped create early prototypes of modern polygraphs.

To shield themselves from the anger of party censors, who associated telepathy with mysticism, the researchers came up with the term "psychotronics." In official paperwork, the phenomena were called the "transfer of bioinformation." This made it possible to legally receive money from the Ministry of Defense, disguising magic as physicochemical processes.

## Why Are the Archives Still Closed?

The main reason for the secrecy lies in defense potential. KGB and Defense Ministry documents describe attempts to create methods of psychological manipulation and remote information extraction. During the Cold War, American intelligence services seriously feared that Soviet military personnel would be able to read secret Pentagon documents at a distance. Crowd-influence technologies, suggestive interrogation scenarios, and the protection of state leaders from mental interference are classic dual-use technologies. Declassifying these archives could reveal the methods of modern operational units. No intelligence service will publish such data, even if the effectiveness of the described techniques is questionable.

The second reason is bureaucratic inertia. In the late 1960s, psychotronics was declared a pseudoscience. The Soviet leadership became frightened of the development of uncontrolled intellectual groups operating outside official ideology. The open laboratories were shut down, and the accumulated work was transferred to special projects. The documents fell under departmental restrictions, which are still being extended simply because the system runs on inertia.

## Ghosts of Soviet Psychotronics

Two decades of research in closed institutes showed the limits of scientific methods in studying the human psyche. Soviet physicists failed to create psychotronic weapons or an army of mutants, but they seriously expanded our understanding of the physiological reserves of the human body.

The main secret of the closed laboratories is not that they found a way to move objects without contact. It lies in how boundless and all-encompassing the superpowers' belief was in the possibility of technologically subjugating the human mind. It was this belief that drove them to spend millions studying murky phenomena, leaving behind archives that are still reliably hidden from prying eyes.
