While century-old olive trees are uprooted, the water keeps waiting: the forgotten alternative

March 26, 2026 — By Digital Menorca


---


WHILE CENTURY-OLD OLIVE TREES ARE UPROOTED, THE WATER KEEPS WAITING

The energy transition cannot serve as an alibi for razing landscape, fertile soil, and rural memory, while the megaprojects of multinational corporations that transform the territory multiply. Against that model, water keeps offering constant, discreet, and technically viable solutions.

1. THE GREEN CONTRADICTION

We are asked to applaud whatever is green, even when it arrives with an excavator. What is presented to us as progress is, too often, nothing more than a new form of occupation of the territory: hectares of altered soil, industrialized horizons, and uprooted roots in the name of a climate salvation that does not always distinguish between decarbonizing and devastating.

This is not about denying the need for renewable energies. It is about saying something much simpler: not everything renewable is the same when it touches the ground. One thing is producing clean energy; quite another is turning the landscape into a sacrifice surface for large interests that then wrap themselves in an impeccable moral discourse.

2. THE FORGOTTEN WATER

While the public debate almost always revolves around panels and wind turbines, water remains there — silent, constant, and often ignored. It needs no daily propaganda. It does not depend on whether there are clouds today or the wind stops blowing tomorrow. Where sufficient flow and a small useful head exist, water works day and night.

There lies the virtue of microhydro: it does not promise miracles, it promises continuity. And that, in an energy world full of intermittency, is no small thing.

3. WHAT THE VORTEX IS

A Vortex system is not magic, nor "free energy." It is a low-head microhydro technology that uses the passage of water to generate a controlled whirlpool capable of driving a turbine and producing electricity. Current commercial models speak of units between 15 and 70 kW, requiring around 1.5 meters of head and a minimum flow also in the region of 1.5 m³/s.

That means not just any postcard stream will do. Real water is needed, a real useful drop, and a place where the works can make sense. Where it fits best is in canals, weirs, small existing drops, or reasonable diversions. That is: not just anywhere, but where the terrain already offers an opportunity without needing to turn the watercourse into an atrocity.

The key is not to colonize the river, but to make intelligent use of where the water is already working.

4. HOW TO IMPROVE IT

Here comes the interesting part. A Vortex can not only work: it can work better. The water intake can be refined to reduce losses, more effective screens installed, fish bypasses provided, sediments managed better, the vortex chamber optimized, and sensors and automation introduced so the turbine works near its optimum point.

Predictive maintenance, remote control, and finer regulation systems can also be incorporated. Artificial intelligence, here, does not replace engineering: it sharpens it. It does not invent energy; it helps lose less, fail less, and decide better.

And that is the difference between a passing fancy and a serious technology: the former gets sold; the latter gets perfected.

5. OUTPUT, COST, AND RETURN

The honest comparison is not made between one turbine and a single solar panel. That would be a fairground trick. It is made by equivalent annual output.

A 15 kW Vortex unit running continuously can produce on the order of 131,400 kWh per year. A 70 kW unit can approach 613,200 kWh annually. A 400 W solar panel, by contrast, typically produces roughly between 750 and 1,190 kWh per year. Translated into language anyone understands: a small Vortex can be equivalent to between 110 and 175 solar panels of 400 W, and a more powerful one to between 515 and 818 panels.

Wind power plays in another division: it produces much more, yes, but it also arrives with a different scale, a different territorial footprint, and a different logic of siting. That is why the fair comparison is not who shouts loudest in megawatts, but who fits best in each place.

As for cost, microhydro is not a cheap toy, but neither is it an impossible madness. A roughly 15 kW installation can run between €50,000 and €100,000. A 30–40 kW one, between €90,000 and €180,000. And a roughly 70 kW one can sit, depending on the site, between €180,000 and €300,000.

The question is not only how much a microhydro plant costs to build. The real question is how long it takes to return its value. And there water again says something important: when the site cooperates, a well-executed installation can begin paying for itself within a few years and keep working for decades. It is not magical energy; it is intelligent engineering with patience.

6. THE REAL DEBATE

This is the heart of the matter. It is not about systematically attacking solar or wind. That would be simplistic. It is about defending something much more serious: producing energy, yes, but without razing the territory as if everything were permissible in the name of progress.

A panel on a rooftop has a logic to it. An entire field turned into an industrial tapestry over living soil is another debate entirely. A well-sited windmill can be reasonable. A massive, clumsy deployment is not. And a microhydro plant well integrated into an existing drop can, in many cases, prove more discreet, more constant, and more coherent with the place than other far flashier solutions.

Perhaps the future should not be measured only in megawatts installed, but also in scars avoided.

Water does not shout. It does not fill headlines. It does not have the same propaganda apparatus. But it remains there, waiting for someone to think about it seriously.

If saving the planet requires mutilating the earth that sustains it, then we are not looking at a solution: we are looking at another form of blindness — that of hiding, under Agenda 2030, a transformation of the territory that confuses modernity with devastation.
