# The Pendulum and the Mortar: On Some Pharmacist-Radiesthésistes, and on Gabriel Lesourd in Particular

*by Thierry Lefèbvre — STUDY — Revue d'Histoire de la Pharmacie, 92nd year, no. 344, 4th quarter 2004, pp. 527-544*

For a long time the "art of the dowser" was applied solely to hydrological and mineralogical exploration. The hazel wand and then the pendulum appealed in turn to private individuals in search of drinking water, and then to industrialists engaged in the prospecting of raw materials.

One of the first, Eugène Chevreul, attributed the gyrations of the pendulum to minute, involuntary movements of the "pendulum-swinger." Curiously, this explanation seemed to satisfy both the defenders and the opponents of the method. The "pendulum-swingers" and other "wand-wavers" found themselves comforted by it, and attributed to their own hypersensitivity the imperceptible reflex movements which they then communicated to their instruments.

The first applications of "dowsing" to human medicine and to the veterinary art date from the beginning of the twentieth century, and seem to have been at first the work of churchmen. Abbé Alexis Mermet (1866-1937), rector of the chapel of Sainte-Madeleine at Jussy near Geneva, was a pioneering figure. In his *Méthode de radiesthésie*, published in the 1930s, he claimed paternity of "radiesthetic diagnosis":

> "More than thirty years ago, around 1905-1906, the idea one day came to me that it must be possible to use the pendulum as an auxiliary to medicine. I set to work, observing the radiations of the human body and of the various organs. I had no difficulty in noticing that diseased organs no longer gave off the same count of radiations as healthy organs. I had found — I had created pendular diagnosis! And from then on, from observation to observation, in clinics, in infirmaries, as an auxiliary to doctors, veterinarians, and herbalists, I was able to establish rules which became the foundation of radiesthetic diagnosis. I taught the science of the pendulum to doctors, to veterinarians and to pharmacists, etc., who began to practise it with success."[^2]

A little further on, the abbé specified his procedure:

> "To examine a patient, one moves the pendulum all around the body, at a distance of 15 to 20 centimetres. As soon as it passes in front of a diseased organ, it undergoes a repulsion; it moves away from the body, tracing in the air, in a vertical plane, a loop which closes over the diseased point — and this loop is all the more developed the more the organ is affected. To localize the diseased point, one must establish its depth in the organism. To this end, one places the index finger of the left hand at the spot beneath which the diseased organ lies; then one tunes one's receptivity by choosing the centimetre or the millimetre as the unit of measurement. The number of oscillations of the pendulum corresponds to the depth of the diseased focus, calculated in centimetres or millimetres. The pendulum is able to render the doctor other services still: it makes it possible to discover diseases while they are still forming or while they exist in a latent state, long before ordinary diagnosis can know their symptoms. Moreover, it is of great utility for the selection of remedies. Let us place the pendulum between a diseased organ and a remedy: if the remedy is very good, the pendulum rotates in the direct direction; if it is good, it oscillates more or less strongly between the organ and the remedy; it remains motionless if the remedy is neutral; and if the remedy is bad, it turns in the indirect direction."[^3]

Let us insist on the fact that the notions of "direct" or "indirect" direction are entirely subjective: every pendulum-swinger is free to opt for his own "direct direction" (either the clockwise direction, or the reverse).

Another emblematic figure was Benoît Padey (1859-1934), better known as Frère Francisque, a member of the confraternity of the Sainte-Famille of Belley. A dowser since 1902, author of a book entitled *Les Secrets de la baguette* (with theories, it must be said, somewhat foggy), he also took an interest in "human radiations" and launched into phytotherapy. In 1929 he brought out a small booklet, *Le Médecin du pauvre, du colon, du missionnaire*, in which were collected numerous recipes composed with plants and formulated by means of the wand's indications.

Another renowned pioneer: Abbé Alexis Timothée Bouly (1865-1958). This ace of the wand would acquire a national and even international celebrity in the 1920s-1930s, by accomplishing innumerable feats which amazed his contemporaries. The parish priest of Hardelot-Plage, he was regarded as a thaumaturge, and the bus stop serving his presbytery had very significantly been renamed "the tisane stop."[^4]

Nor should we forget the Franciscan father Jean-Louis Bourdoux (1876-1963). A native of Ussel, he worked from 1905 to 1921 in the region of Poconé in Mato Grosso. J.-L. Bourdoux was the author of *Notions pratiques de radiesthésie pour les missionnaires*, reprinted many times, in which he set out the principle of "therapeutic syntonization." Let us follow his reasoning:

> "By mixing these three plants, am I not going to obtain a better formula than each of them separately? That is not certain. Some plants strengthen their activity in uniting, while others weaken or neutralize each other completely, or even produce a contrary effect. How to know whether they can unite? The simplest means, though neither the quickest nor the most economical, would be to make the mixture and study it with the pendulum by passing it before the thirty photographs of patients.[^5] That is long, and if, in the end, the mixture is an unhappy one, one has wasted one's time and one's plants. I then imagined establishing the relations the plants had among themselves — whether they attracted or repelled one another, or whether they were indifferent to each other. I used the following procedure: I placed two samples on the same plane, at a small distance from each other. Between the two, I lowered my pendulum. According as it oscillated in B or in M,[^6] I understood that the mixture of the plants would be good or bad."[^7]

Back in France, Father Bourdoux perfected his homeopathic dilutions of Amazonian plants by means of his pendulum. The Poconéol brand was registered on 3 June 1935 at the commercial court of Albi. The Poconeols are still exploited today by the pharmaceutical laboratories "Plantes & Médecines," a subsidiary of the Pierre Fabre laboratory.

## Radiesthesia, a New Science?

But the servants of God were not the only ones to take an interest in medical radiesthesia. Doctors and pharmacists also seized upon it at the beginning of the 1930s, seduced by the promises of what was then claiming to be a new science.

The word "radiesthesia" — let us not forget — was of quite recent coinage. It seems to have been pronounced for the first time by Abbé Bouly during the autumn of 1926.[^8] On 29 October, returning from a prospecting survey around the gold- and silver-bearing lead mine of Villevieille in the Puy-de-Dôme,[^9] the priest of Hardelot was interviewed by Géo London, the celebrated reporter of the Parisian daily *Le Journal*. It was on this occasion that he pronounced for the first time this word which he had imagined, some time earlier, with the assistance of Canon Louis Bayard, dean of the Faculté libre des lettres of Lille.[^10]

The choice of this learned-sounding neologism abruptly cast the dowser's art into the scientific universe, even if it concerned only its margin. "Radiesthesia" moreover was not without evoking the word "cryptesthesia," imagined a few years earlier by the Nobel laureate Charles Richet.[^11]

In 1929 the Association des amis de la radiesthésie (AAR) was founded. Its honorary committee gave pride of place to scientists of great renown: Édouard Branly, Arsène d'Arsonval, Foveau de Courmelles, Armand Viré, Georges Claude, Georges Lakhovsky and several others, not forgetting of course Abbés Bouly and Mermet and Brother Padey. From the beginning of the 1930s, the Association published a monthly, the *Bulletin de l'Association des amis de la radiesthésie*, which opened its pages widely to communications of a scientific character.

During the first years, thousands of people joined the AAR, among them probably more than a hundred doctors and pharmacists. From the mid-1930s, a "medical section," composed essentially of French members, was even set up within the association. It was made up of Doctors M.-L. Vouaux, A. Besson, Doctors Foveau de Courmelles, Ch. Vaysse, G. Maynier, Ph. Czaczkes, P. Caillard, Levêque, G. Aveline, the pharmacists Batailler, J. Cribier, L. É. Maupy, L. G. Rodillon, G. Lesourd, and the dental surgeon Couriaux.

Among the doctors,[^12] let us cite in the very first place Dr Jules Regnault, former professor at the naval academy of Toulon and director of the review *Côte d'Azur médical*. Regnault had introduced into France the original theories of the American physician Albert Abrams, founder of spondylotherapy (or reflexotherapy) and much-contested promoter of the electronic diagnosis of blood and urine (by means of an apparatus christened the "Oscilloclast").

Dr Albert Leprince, also a member of the AAR, had been one of the first to practise reflexotherapy in France. Inventor of "chromodiagnosis" (or diagnosis by colours), he signed several works devoted to medical radiesthesia: *Pendule et médecins. Sourciers et malades* (Paris, Amédée Legrand, 1933) and *Radiesthésie médicale. Applications de l'art du sourcier à la médecine humaine* (Paris, Librairie A. Legrand, 1936). Let us also mention Dr Alfred Roux, a physician at Vichy: around 1938 he published a book entitled *Autour de la radiesthésie* (Vichy, chez l'auteur, s.d.).

The pharmacists were also numerous to join the AAR. Let us cite for example: P.-L. Pipault (qualified in 1892) practising at Coulommiers; Théodore Olivain (1907) of Louhans; Gabriel Moreau (1907) of Paris; Léon Leclère (1894) of Cormeilles-en-Parisis; Jean Teynié (1928) of Bordeaux; Damien Richelmi (1892) of Puget-Théniers; Jean A. Fabre (1923) of Port-de-Bouc; Georges Guédard (1927) of Montmirail; F. Saint-Laurens[^13] (1898) of Pau; Dubs (1899) of Boulogne-sur-Mer; L. Jousseaume (1904) of Bournezeau; etc. Other names also appeared in the course of our reading: Édouard Kingler (Metz), Jean Cribier (Orléans), Émile Roux (Paris), L. G. Rodillon (Paris), Legrand (?), Batailler ("colonial colleague"), J. Raton (?), Canat (?), Laurençon (?), Mercier (?), Laubœuf (?), etc. Further research is obviously required.

Four pharmacists interest us in particular.

First of all Gédéon Meillère,[^14] hospital pharmacist (he practised at the Hôpital du Midi, at Tenon, at Necker, at La Pitié and at Laennec), doctor of medicine and member of the Académie de médecine and of the Société de pharmacie.[^15] He took a particular interest in the applications of radiesthesia to analytical chemistry.[^16]

Another notable personality: Louis Eugène Maupy, qualified in 1920 and practising at 28 rue Vignon in the 8th arrondissement of Paris. Since 1928 Louis Maupy had been the pharmacist holding the "Abbé Chaupitre" speciality brand.[^17] He was associated in this enterprise with Dr Emmanuel Porteu de la Morandière, of whom everything leads us to believe that he too was not only a homeopath but also a radiesthésiste.[^18]

Let us also mention A. Soulier, a pharmacist-biologist qualified in 1901, practising at Le Puy. He was the author of a curious work entitled *Spécification des toxines de la tuberculose et du cancer*, published by Vigot in 1932. In it he proposed, with great conviction, a technique of radiesthetic analysis of the "indosés" of urine, which was not without evoking, minus the electronic tinsel, Dr Abrams's Oscilloclast diagnosis.


## Gabriel Lesourd, Pharmacist and Radiesthésiste

The most striking personality was nevertheless Gabriel Lesourd (1890-1976). Born at Villedieu-sur-Indre in the Berry, a graduate of the École de pharmacie of Paris, he settled in 1913 at Chauvigny in the Vienne. Twelve years later, having no doubt accumulated a small nest egg, he took over the Grande Pharmacie du Nord of Achille-Marie Duvergier, located at 43 rue Simart in the 18th arrondissement of Paris. At the same time he became the lessee of several pharmaceutical specialities, among them the "Tisane du Curé de Deuil" (to which we shall return in a future article).

According to the testimony of Marcel Trauet published in 1976 in the *Bulletin de l'Association des amis de la radiesthésie*, his beginnings in radiesthesia were accidental:

> "How did he come to radiesthesia? By several routes no doubt. Like many of us, he was at first a sceptic. The first revelation of his faculties probably took place during an evening, in a small family circle where everyone was practising with a watch on the end of a chain [...]. At G. Lesourd's fingertips, the pendulum governed with vigour."[^19]

From 1935, this passionate pharmacist sat on the board of directors of the AAR.

Gabriel Lesourd published two very curious works: *Méthode radiesthésique de recherche des maladies et imprégnations microbiennes* (1934) and *Vie, maladies, radiations* (1936).

In *Méthode radiesthésique [...]*, which Dr Albert Leprince prefaced, he proclaimed his convictions loud and clear:

> "Everything in nature emits radiations: luminous ones, our eyes discern them; sonorous ones, our ears hear them; odorous ones, our nostrils accept them with pleasure or reject them with disgust, but they sense them; calorific ones, our skin stores them up; gustatory ones, if I may so express myself, our tongue is there to profit from them. [...] But these radiations, in equilibrium or not, which our known senses have been able to grasp — are they the only ones that exist? Assuredly not. [...] Therein lies the whole preliminary explanation of the gift possessed by dowsers."[^20]

By means of his pendulum, Lesourd claimed to analyse the pathological content of urines:

> "The material necessary for radiesthetic analysis comes down to very little, since the radiations of urine propagate through space like all the others. [...] An excellent pendulum, neither too heavy nor too light, preferably of pure silver or black glass. Failing that, any one at all. A series of microbial witnesses, for example in the form of vaccines in tubes sealed with a flame; a series of pure powders of organs, each in a stoppered glass tube; three or four sachets of absorbing mixture, whose utility against sources of error will be seen; a few anatomical charts; a series of coloured ribbons; a series of transparent coloured screens (Dr Leprince's chromo case); lastly, for pharmacists, the series of medicines."[^21]

The beatings of his pendulum enabled him to determine the affected organ and the type of affliction: "These are the oscillation lengths which I believe it well to name microbial wavelengths, or the wavelengths of diseases of microbial impregnation."[^22]

Starting from these observations, Lesourd strove to identify the effective medicines: to this end he proceeded to a confrontation of the diseased organs with the remedies likely to act. Their selection was thus made by "syntonic"[^23] agreement.

In her archives, Mme Marie-Paule Vellutini, present owner of the Lesourd laboratory, keeps a model of the standard form which accompanied the biological samples to be analysed (urine, blood or others). The letterhead specified "Laboratoire biologique de radiesthésie médicale — 91, boulevard Haussmann, Paris (8e) — G. Lesourd, pharmacien." Two spaces were provided for the name of the patient and that of the doctor. The following information also had to be given:

- the time of the sampling and an indication of its circumstances ("sun, clear, overcast, fog, night");
- the type of product examined;
- the medicines taken (it was specified that "their contra-indication [could] introduce a source of error");
- the "sex sense" and the "polarization sense";
- the "impregnations (magnetic fields, wavelengths or nodal)";
- the organs ("simple enumeration of the organs more or less out of balance, with the figure corresponding to the impregnation fixed upon it").

The existence of such forms and the use of a specialized jargon indirectly bore witness to the active collaboration of several doctors. Were they numerous? The influx of practitioners joining the Association des amis de la radiesthésie at the beginning of the 1930s suggests as much. One may cite in particular Dr Jean Desmier[^24] and Dr Georges Maynier,[^25] who seemed to be very directly associated with Lesourd's activities. Was it a matter of a form of cronyism? Nothing allows us to affirm it.

## Specialities Born of Radiesthesia

Gabriel Lesourd did not content himself with analysing biological products. He also formulated pharmaceutical specialities by means of his pendulum. On this subject he wrote in 1937:

> "The G. Lesourd Laboratory has scientifically studied [...] a great number of plants, then various mixtures, of which it has retained only a certain number. The Abbé Mermet, illustrious and much mourned, had communicated to it some of the syntonizations gathered in the course of his numerous experiments. From the union of phytotherapy and radiesthesia there thus emerged several plant mixtures capable of restoring vibratory equilibrium and bringing back health."[^26]

The Tisanes Lesourd thus knew a certain success (some are moreover still exploited today). They were counted in dozens, but some seemed more prized than others. The Tisane Anthylline consisted of twenty plants, "with refreshing and decongestive, tonic and depurative action." Tisane Lesourd no. 3, or "Coli-Tisane," suited patients suffering from... the intestine. Let us also cite Tisane Lesourd no. 19 or "Pulmo-Tisane," Tisane Lesourd no. 62 or "Arthri-Tisane," Tisane Lesourd no. 40 or "Neuro-Tisane," Tisane Lesourd no. 10 or "Gastro-Tisane," etc.

Other specialities saw the light of day: first and foremost the Capsules Anthelox. "Intestinal worms, whatever they may be, are the cause of a host of malaises, particularly in children, but also in many adults; it is therefore useful to wage a war to the death upon them. The Capsules Anthelox, based on non-toxic natural essences dissolved in a slightly laxative oil, do not content themselves with expelling the intestinal worms, they kill them. Anthelox is vermicide and not merely vermifuge."[^27]

Other specialities bore witness to Lesourd's fertile imagination: the Fœmigol, "cachets (2 to 4 per day) against wasting"; the Bolcitol, composed of plant extracts and recommended in diseases of the liver, the kidneys and the blood; the Onction Antipar, "against all the external parasites of the body (lice, fleas, scab, etc.)"; the Baume Agruse, "antineuralgic, non-greasy, pleasant, soothes toothaches, insect bites, all pains"; and of course the Tisane du Curé de Deuil and its several variants, the Dépuratif concentré du Curé de Deuil (abandoned in 1940) and the Tisane spéciale no. III du Curé de Deuil ("against all colibacillary and staphylococcal manifestations"), for example.

Lesourd placed in the radiesthesia reviews advertisements for his specialities which were sometimes esoteric. Thus the promotion of the Anthelox was accompanied by the mention "Champ 0/68/136." If one refers to his writings, this meant that the product in question (and thus the mixture of plants constituting it) emitted a "magnetic field" over a radius of 1.36 m, the middle of the field (0.68 m) constituting what Lesourd called the "wavelength" of the Anthelox. This "wavelength" was detected by means of the pendulum, which simply went to and fro, at that spot, in a vertical plane. Not practising radiesthesia ourselves, we have not verified the authenticity of these claims!

Alongside his purely pharmaceutical activity, the Gabriel Lesourd laboratory also marketed a certain number of "precision instruments." First and foremost let us cite the Lesourd "special pendulum of black glass": "specially prepared and moulded under a shape intentionally recalling that of a certain shell, its [sensitivity] is remarkable and multiplied by the slight magnetization of the suspension ring. It will usefully serve in all research undertaken, and particularly in the study of biological radiations, chromodiagnosis, and research on plans. It is indispensable in the analyses and measurements of the magnetic fields of bodies."[^28]

The "Lesourd ruler," for its part, allowed one to calculate the "magnetic fields": "The composition impregnating the ruler contains an absorbent preventing the radiations of the table or of objects situated underneath from parasitizing the radiation or radiations under study. It is at once rollable and washable."[^29] Sachets and "absorbing" screens filtered the "noxious waves," the nightmare of the radiesthésistes, who accused them of interfering with their measurements. The "radiesthetic diagnostic apparatus of G. Lesourd" made it possible "to multiply the sensitivity in research [...] on the human body or that of animals [...] while considerably reducing the sources of error by suggestion." Made up of a handle, an electric battery and a spring, it was supposed to objectify the gyrations of the pendulum associated with it.

Gabriel Lesourd also supplied cases of witnesses for diagnosis, namely glass tubes filled with "microbial impregnations": the Trousse Lesourd no. 1 consisted of 50 tubes, labelled from "Albumin" (no. 1) to "Leprosy" (no. 50), by way of "Cancer" (no. 15) and "Syphilis" (no. 39). The pharmacist unfortunately did not specify the mode of preparation of these witness tubes.

## Lesourd as Publisher

Not content with developing this atypical commercial activity, Lesourd also launched into the publishing of a luxurious review devoted to radiesthesia. We have found, by the greatest of chances, five issues of a periodical entitled *La Science nouvelle, revue générale de la radiesthésie et des sciences psychiques*.[^30] Not referenced at the Bibliothèque nationale de France nor in any other public library to our knowledge, *La Science nouvelle* benefited from an honourable patronage committee, which we reproduce in full: "President: M. the Pharmacist G. Lesourd; Members: M. the Abbé Mermet, M. the Honorary Governor Mahé, M. Commandant de La Bastide, M. L. Delattre, M. Doctor Desmier, M. Doctor Maynier, M. J. Sursol, M. H. Mellin."[^31]

The administration and the editorial office of the review were domiciled at Limoges, 6 rue Montalembert, apparently at the home of its manager, Jean Margerit.[^32] In his contribution to the first issue, Lesourd evoked elliptically the circumstances of the review's creation: "A journey to Riom. The chance of a conversation with an artist of great talent [no doubt Jean Margerit]. A second conversation with a man of powerful mind on the return from Riom. A silence of some weeks. A new conversation, prepared this time at Neuilly-sur-Seine.[^33] And *La Science nouvelle* was born."[^34] From this short and imprecise account one may nevertheless conclude the central role of Lesourd, who seems to have financed the operation at least in part. The many pages of advertising praising his commercial activities bear witness to this. The first page of the first issue thus begins with a column devoted to the specialities of the Laboratoire Lesourd:

> "Medical radiesthesia teaches and proves: that health is a harmonious state of vibratory equilibrium; that illness proceeds from the imbalance of the organism. To restore health, one must restore the harmonious equilibrium of the radiations. The products of the Lesourd Laboratory have all been studied radiesthetically in such a way that their components are able, by the syntonic agreement between their radiations and those of the illnesses to which they correspond, to restore the vibratory equilibrium, and to bring back health."

There follows a rapid description of several specialities, among them the Tisane du Curé de Deuil, the Anthelox, the Bolcitol and a few others. On the penultimate page (p. 31), the "Small Advertisements" praise Lesourd's "radiesthesia accessories." In the four other issues in our possession,[^35] the advertising for the pharmacist's commercial activities remains just as omnipresent. Thus, the last page of issue no. 5 is entirely occupied by an advertising frame for the Laboratoires biologiques de radiesthésie médicale G. Lesourd. All the activities then exercised in this mysterious place are listed: "Research of every kind on urine, blood, saliva, excreta or various secretions (hair, photographs), etc. Medical diagnosis. Medications and doses (for Doctors). All syntonizations. Biological, bacteriological, parasitological research. Food and industrial research. Radiesthetic analyses of waters, pharmaceutical products, etc. Colours, perfumes, precious stones and metals, etc. Human polarization (physical and psychic harmony). Research into heredity or impregnations with a view to the prevention of grave illnesses (cancer, tuberculosis, etc.). Preparation of all personal radiesthetic complexes. Research into the predispositions of the physical, cerebral, mental faculties in children, with a view to the possibilities of a future professional orientation. Research into all human imbalances, physical, nervous, psychic, etc. Téléradiesthésie."

The ebbing of the great radiesthesia "fashion," perceptible from the second half of the 1930s, no doubt led Gabriel Lesourd to put his activity as a radiesthésiste somewhat in abeyance, or at least to separate it more neatly from his pharmaceutical activity. The spectre of charlatanism also seems to have haunted him. In December 1934, he addressed the following letter to the AAR:

> "I would be grateful if you would bring to the attention of our Friends of Radiesthesia both the following facts and my indignant protest, which they will all endorse, I am sure. It appears, and I have been given to affirm it by a doctor, that in certain public places, in the hall of the Gare Saint-Lazare among others, a 'pendulum-swinger' accosts people after having surreptitiously examined them, and tells them 'your liver has this,' 'your heart has that,' 'your spleen is enlarged,' or 'your lungs are in a bad way.' The individual who acts thus deserves, does he not, our contempt? It is possible, I have been told, that a complaint be lodged against him. You should affirm that the AAR disassociates itself from those who do not act loyally and honestly. Will you do so, if only by publishing this letter and recalling what I wrote at the end of *Méthode radiesthésique*: 'I do not admit the charlatanism of the wand-waver or the pendulum-swinger who is pleased to play the savant and the treating physician,' or what our venerated [...] Doctor Meillère, also a pharmacist, wrote in signalling 'the danger presented, at the present hour, by the pretension of empirics to establish diagnoses, prognoses and treatments on simple radiesthetic observations, in vivo or on photograph, without any medical collaboration.'"[^36]

It is also probable that the increased controls of the public authorities over the pharmaceutical profession, from the beginning of the 1940s, obliged him to adopt a more discreet attitude.

Nevertheless, as Marie-Paule Vellutini reminds us, Lesourd continued to practise his passion to the end of his life. A member (as we have seen) of the board of directors of the Association des amis de la radiesthésie from 1935, he took charge of its *Bulletin* at his retirement, in 1953.[^37] The following year, he organized and presided over the International Congress of Radiesthesia, held that year at the Hôtel Lutetia. Several more years passed, and then Lesourd withdrew definitively to his property at Longpont, some ten kilometres from Villers-Cotterêts. He died there in 1976, in his 86th year. Since then he rests in the cemetery of Châteauroux.

## Other Leads

Several other pharmaceutical specialities were, clearly, conceived with the assistance of radiesthesia. This was probably the case of the Urarthone, elaborated in 1935 by René Lehning.[^38] It was also the case, as we have seen, of certain formulations said to be of the Abbé Chaupitre, as well as of the Poconeols.[^39] The examples must be numerous, and it would be interesting to examine certain evocative titles of specialities.

On the whole, phytotherapy,[^40] homeopathy and radiesthesia got along well together in the 1920s-1930s. Thus, around 1935, the Laboratoire Thérapie, located at 93 avenue de Paris at Saint-Mandé, marketed the "Panphutons." These tablets, "dosed at 0.50 g of the total plant" and housed in "elegant aluminium tubes," were used within the framework of "radiesthetic syntonization." The Laboratoire normal homéopathique for its part sold two syntonization cases: one was composed of "witness-tubes corresponding to the 154 medicines of the synergic homeopathic Pharmacopoeia," the other comprised "20 microbial witness-tubes." In Paris, at 26 rue des Petites-Champs, the [Émile] Roux laboratory marketed pharmaceutical specialities "much appreciated by radiesthésiste Doctors. Ask for free samples for syntonization [...]".

Here is decidedly a domain requiring deeper investigation! We hope that this article will provide some leads for historians of the medical and pharmaceutical sciences.

- Original French page numbers (527-544) are omitted; the Persée text is continuous. Footnote markers [^N] correspond to the author's notes, condensed below where purely bibliographic.
- The article is a work of academic history published in the Revue d'Histoire de la Pharmacie; it documents the radiesthesia-pharmacy entanglement without endorsing the method (note 1: "It is obviously not a question, in this article, of passing judgment on the validity or non-validity of the radiesthetic method.
