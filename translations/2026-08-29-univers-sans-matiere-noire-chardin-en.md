# A Universe Without Dark Matter?

*By Gabriel Chardin (CNRS), originally published 4 June 2018 in Le Journal du CNRS, "Carnets de science" No. 4.*

---

A little over a century after Einstein rid physics of the hypothesis that an unknown substance, the ether, filled space, astrophysics and cosmology are once again confronted with a curious enigma: our Universe would be filled to 95% with dark matter and dark energy whose origin and nature no one has yet been able to explain. Accepting the existence of a dark sector so pervasive as it is elusive remains, for a majority of physicists, the most obvious way to render the totality of astrophysical data at their disposal compatible with the theory of general relativity. But here is the problem: after forty years of intensive tracking, no instrument and no experiment has succeeded in observing the slightest cosmic structure or the slightest particle capable of playing the role of this invisible mass.

Without calling it a crisis, this situation is deeply frustrating for a discipline whose objective is to arrive at a theory exhaustively describing the content of our Universe and its evolution. The physicist Gabriel Chardin, who received the CNRS Silver Medal in 2007 for his work on dark matter -- notably within the international Edelweiss 1 collaboration -- believes it may be possible to find a way out by envisaging, as Einstein in his time did, a new breach with common sense: accepting that negative masses exist in our Universe, just as positive and negative electric charges exist. From this starting point, he envisions a new cosmological model with a Universe containing as much matter as antimatter, where it is no longer necessary to invoke the concepts of dark matter or dark energy. "This Universe, developed over ten years with Aurelien Benoit-Levy, is still very far from being accepted as an alternative to the standard cosmological model with dark matter and dark energy," acknowledges Gabriel Chardin, who develops his ideas in detail in the essay *L'Insoutenable gravite de l'Univers* (The Unbearable Gravity of the Universe). "But it does not rest solely on speculation; it reproduces, in a rather astonishing way, a great number of the data we possess and makes predictions that will be testable in the coming years, or even the coming months."

## Anomalies in the Coma Berenices

But for what reasons has the scientific community come to consider the existence of a dark sector as necessary? And what are the reasons that today drive a growing number of researchers to consider alternative hypotheses? The first to raise, as early as 1933, the hypothesis of an invisible mass was the Swiss astrophysicist Fritz Zwicky (1898-1974). While observing the Coma Berenices constellation, where he was measuring the velocities of galaxies orbiting in the Coma cluster, he calculated that the gravity exerted by visible matter could not compensate for the centrifugal force acting on these galaxies: they should have been ejected from the cluster like passengers on a carousel spinning too fast. He deduced the presence of a dark matter whose mass was far greater than that of visible matter. More than thirty years passed before his intuition was confirmed by the study Vera Rubin conducted on rotation profiles within galaxies. The only way to explain these profiles within the framework of the laws of gravity was to admit that these galaxies were immersed in a halo of dark matter six times heavier than their visible content. These results were subsequently confirmed, and numerous other independent observations accumulated evidence in favor of the existence of dark matter. Though concordant, these various pieces of evidence are nonetheless indirect. All the more so since none of the known forms of dark matter (planets, neutrinos, black holes) appear capable of contributing significantly to this excess mass; dark matter can only involve physics that is still unknown.

## Modifying the Laws of Gravity

This matter, or the mysterious particles that compose it, physicists around the world have therefore been tracking for forty years -- either in space, or in accelerators like the LHC, or with increasingly sensitive detectors. The trouble is that they have discovered nothing so far. This certainly allows theorists to further constrain the possible characteristics of this exotic matter, but it also raises growing doubts about its very existence. As early as 1983, the Israeli physicist Mordehai Milgrom realized that there would be no need at all to invoke the slightest dark matter... on the condition that the laws of Newtonian gravity be modified. Noting that these laws have in fact only been tested and verified in the relatively strong gravitational fields of our Solar System, he proposed to revise them when they apply to bodies moving in very weak fields -- those that prevail in interstellar space.

> This theory, called Mond (Modified Newtonian Dynamics), predicts the velocity profiles in galaxies rather well. The problem is that at this stage we have no means to test it experimentally, notes Gabriel Chardin, who is also president of the Committee of Very Large Research Infrastructures at CNRS. The most distant craft from us, the Voyager probe, is subjected to a gravitational field a hundred million times weaker than that which it experienced on the surface of the Earth, but that is still a thousand times stronger than what it would experience in so-called "weak" fields!

In the absence of this experimental proof, the majority of researchers hesitate to call into question laws that, since Einstein, have never been found wanting. All the more so since, while Mond can rid us of dark matter, this theory is in any case incapable of answering a new enigma posed by gravity: the discovery in 1998 of the acceleration of the expansion of the Universe, interpreted as the manifestation of a kind of repulsive gravity.

Since the nature of this mysterious phenomenon remained unexplained, physicists treated it as they had dark matter: they added it to the list of their "known unknowns" and measured its effects. They named this new mysterious component dark energy. Measurements from the Planck satellite indicate that this dark energy today represents slightly more than two-thirds of the energy contained in the Universe, dark matter about a quarter, and known matter barely a twentieth. By accepting this curious composition, cosmologists were able to elaborate the standard cosmological model -- a model that, while remaining compatible with general relativity and observations, strives to describe in a coherent manner the history of the Universe from the Big Bang to the present day.

## Antimatter in the Intergalactic Void?

> If it remains the reference solution for the majority of physicists, in my view and in the minds of a growing number of my colleagues, the standard cosmological model has several very inelegant aspects, nuances Gabriel Chardin. It works rather well, it is true, but it appears less and less plausible in its composition, and counts too many free parameters that must be readjusted with each new observation. In short, something is wrong with the Universe it describes...

And it is for this reason that new models have recently been proposed by theorists like Andre Maeder of the University of Geneva, or Thomas Buchert of the Astrophysical Research Center of Lyon 2 -- Universes that require neither dark energy nor dark matter to explain current observations.

This is also the case for the Dirac-Milne Universe elaborated by Gabriel Chardin. He grounds it on two revolutionary hypotheses. First, he considers that this Universe contains as much matter as antimatter, and that antimatter possesses a negative gravitational mass. "The total, or at least apparent, disappearance of antimatter from our Universe remains to this day another of the great mysteries of the standard model: the latter indicates nonetheless that the Universe contained during its very first moments after the Big Bang as much matter as antimatter," he insists. "In the Dirac-Milne Universe, there is no longer any need to explain the disappearance of antimatter: it is still there, hidden in the intergalactic voids." The supposition that antimatter possesses a negative mass is a plausible supposition, since, to date, no experiment has been able to measure the mass of antimatter. "At the atomic scale, if we compare it to electromagnetism or to the kinetic energy of particles, the effect of gravity is negligible," explains the physicist. "We have been able to produce antihydrogen atoms for more than twenty years, but we have only just found the means to slow them down sufficiently to soon be able to weigh them."

## Negative Masses in the Universe?

Theorists have in any case demonstrated that the existence of negative masses is perfectly compatible with general relativity, even if the movements that such masses would have relative to one another are very strange: while positive masses aggregate among themselves, negative masses repel each other and spread apart!

> In such a Universe, under the effect of gravity, matter -- of positive mass -- aggregates and forms large structures. In contrast, antimatter particles, of negative mass, spread out as a cold gas in what we take to be great cosmic voids.

Voids that, as they expand and repel structures of positive mass, would explain -- without any need for dark energy -- the expansion of the Universe as it is observed.

Furthermore, the assumption that particles of both positive and negative mass exist has another unexpected effect: it creates a polarization of the vacuum that modifies the equations of gravity. These then present a great similarity with those of the Mond theory. One can therefore dispense with the hypothesis of dark matter to explain the rotation of galaxies.

> In its current state, the Dirac-Milne universe model remains highly speculative, but the values it yields are astonishingly close to those already measured by the major observation programs, and it will be directly tested in the coming years, or perhaps even as early as the end of 2018, thanks to the Gbar, Alpha-g, and AEgIS experiments at Cern, which will finally measure the gravitational mass of antimatter, assures Gabriel Chardin. We will then know whether the proposed explanation of a matter-antimatter universe, guided by its arguments of mathematical and physical elegance, is indeed confirmed by experiment.

### Weighing Antimatter: A Heavy Task!

Antimatter would possess a negative mass and would behave differently from matter when subjected to gravity. This hypothesis, proposed by the physicist Gabriel Chardin, is plausible, but very audacious. For while the production of antiprotons in accelerators is now well mastered, "weighing" these negatively charged antiparticles is nearly impossible, so negligible is the effect of gravity compared to that of the electromagnetic interaction. One way to neutralize this force consists of creating neutral atoms of antihydrogen by combining an antiproton and a positron (anti-electron). The difficulty is then to manage to confine and slow down these anti-atoms before they encounter atoms of matter and annihilate. Using a magnetic trap, the Alpha collaboration at Cern recently succeeded in confining a thousand antihydrogen atoms for several hours. By the end of the year, Alpha will attempt to determine what exactly happens when these anti-atoms are "released" in a gravitational field. To be continued...
