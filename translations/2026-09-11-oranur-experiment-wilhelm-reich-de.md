1. The ORANUR Experiment - An Overview

Jon Skinner

Of all the experiments that Wilhelm Reich conducted in the course of his scientific career, none is more important for our individual well-being and for the future of our world than the ORANUR experiment (OR gone AGAINST NUclear Radiation, orgone against nuclear radiation). See Reich's book The ORANUR Experiment (Frankfurt: Zweitausendeins, 1997).

Between 1939 and 1941, Reich discovered orgone energy, the primordial, mass-free energy that is present everywhere in our atmosphere and animates all that lives. As he investigated this form of energy and became more familiar with its properties, he arrived at the conclusion that orgone and nuclear radiation are opposites, and he suspected that orgone, if sufficiently strong, could counteract the effects of nuclear radiation and alleviate radiation sickness. Against the backdrop of the Korean War and the possible use of atomic weapons, Reich decided to test his hypothesis in January 1951.


---


He acquired two units of radium of one milligram each. A sample shielded with lead protection was taken to a garage located some distance from the site of the experiment. This radium was not to be exposed to the concentrated orgone and was accordingly to serve as a comparison. The other milligram was placed inside a small orgone energy accumulator. This device, composed of successive layers of metallic and organic materials (the metallic layer always faces the interior of the box-like accumulator — one metallic layer and one organic layer form a "ply"), was invented by Reich to concentrate atmospheric orgone energy. The single-ply accumulator was placed inside a 20-ply accumulator, which was then set up in a room likewise constructed like an accumulator. The radium was left there for five hours. After this time, an examination of the accumulator room showed a much higher background radiation than normal. The radium was immediately removed from the room. This was repeated daily over six days, with the radium remaining in the orgone room for one hour each day.

On the seventh day, the radium had been in the accumulator for half an hour when a violent reaction occurred. Observers who were about 100 meters away from the building saw the atmosphere in the orgone room cloud over with a moving bluish to purplish haze. As they approached the building, they became nauseated; pressure appeared in the forehead, they had stomach cramps, disturbances of balance, and states of weakness. The radium was removed from the orgone room as quickly as possible and the experiment was declared finished.

But its effects persisted. For months, measurements of background radiation at the site of the trial remained high. People who entered the building became nauseated and were unable to work there. Three weeks later, radiation measurements within a 1000 km radius showed a higher value than normal. Eventually, the participants in the experiment fell ill. They developed the usual symptoms of radiation sickness. In addition, each suffered the return of diseases characteristic of him or her. Someone who had suffered from gallbladder difficulties had an "attack"; in another person who had heart problems in the past, these returned, etc. Each person was affected at their weakest point.

In the middle of the serious problems that coping with the "ORANUR emergency" brought with it, Reich set about forming a picture of what had happened. Since classical physics offered no explanation for how such a small quantity of nuclear material could trigger such a strong reaction, his account was based on orgone theory.

We live in an ocean of orgone energy. This life energy permeates everything, including our bodies. Normally, orgone energy is benign. But when it came into contact with the radium, it was transformed into a highly charged "killer" energy, which Reich called ORANUR. This overcharged energy — and not the radium itself — made the experiment participants ill. Thus the real danger comes not from the nuclear material itself, but from its interaction with orgone — the omnipresent energy.

The significance of the ORANUR experiment is enormous. Every time a nuclear power plant releases radioactive material (1), or when an atomic bomb is tested, the orgone ocean in which we live is disturbed and an ORANUR reaction is triggered. Sudden "flu waves" can be traced back to these events. Today, much caution is required in the use of accumulators. It is absolutely necessary that we all do what is needed to protect our personal surroundings and the atmosphere in which we live from radioactive substances.

Finally, the ORANUR experiment did bring with it some glimmers of hope for being able to neutralize the effect of nuclear radiation. Reich and his coworkers found that after the initial effects, the ORANUR had immunizing effects: they were able to cope better when they were exposed to ORANUR again. Thus the ORANUR experiment points toward a certain protection against nuclear radiation. Before any practical application becomes possible, however, humanity must be made aware of orgone energy itself and of the dangers of ORANUR, which is produced by the interaction of nuclear radiation and orgone.

3. A Subjective Look at the Effects of Atomic Bomb Tests
Vi Weeks

Many of us live with the fear of a large atomic explosion. All of us experience the effects of a smaller one weekly.


---


Two years ago, the significance of Reich's ORANUR Experiment for everyday life forced itself upon some of us in a disturbing way. When we began to see ourselves as fish in a lake of energy, we truly began to hear and see occasional media reports of atomic bomb tests in Nevada, the USSR, and the South Pacific. It seemed to us that these tests were connected with certain patterns of physical and emotional health in ourselves, our children, and our animals. Some among us were not sure whether there was a connection, but were nonetheless curious about the effects of these tests. We were all familiar with Reich's ORANUR Experiment, which had demonstrated that radioactive material interacts with the energy in the atmosphere, triggering a complicated and dangerous chain of events that does not remain confined to the place of origin. We waited and we observed.

After a year of observing and studying, we were sufficiently certain to begin informally exchanging personal anecdotes and atmospheric observations. Soon it became increasingly hard to ignore that

disturbingly, our individual experiences and observations were quite similar;
our feelings were directly linked to our observations of the sky (for example, we felt depressed and lethargic when the atmosphere looked dark and oppressive);

These disordered findings motivated us to begin a second, more formal phase of our experiment. For three months (March - May 1984), eight of us wrote down daily records of the state of our health and our ability to work, and likewise for our children, pets, and colleagues. It should not go unmentioned that a complete report of the dates of the worldwide atomic bomb tests only became available for comparison six months AFTER the anecdotal data had been collected. Thus our observations were recorded without knowledge of the actual times of the atomic tests.

What happened after each atomic test?

The most obvious effects of the atomic bomb tests on people appeared in the records of a preschool teacher. On closer examination, an unusually high number of children were physically affected one to six days after each test in Nevada. Cases of sore throat, stomach aches, and flu symptoms increased dramatically. In addition, epidemic conjunctivitis and chickenpox were commonplace. During this period the children's activities were minimal. Adjectives appeared such as "exhausted," "moody," and "tearful."

These reports testified again and again to an increase in throat and headaches, cold symptoms, and an almost unresolvable, all-pervading fatigue. A few of these symptoms also appear within a week after the Russian tests, but not with the same evidence. Further studies are necessary to uncover the effects of these more distant tests and their characteristic time course. This is made more difficult by the temporal proximity of the tests of the different countries and the apparent "tit for tat" between the United States and the USSR (the most active testing nations).
